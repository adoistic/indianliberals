summary: |-
  Written from 44 Western Court, New Delhi, on the eve of a Lok Sabha debate on the Sino-Indian border, Ranga briefs Rajaji on the state of play in Parliament. He reports on a fresh meeting with the Prime Minister, who disclosed that he had written to Mrs. Bandaranaike accepting the Colombo proposals in principle and that the Chinese had responded in like terms. Nehru now judged those proposals more favourable than his own 8 September demand and pressed the House to accept them, but the P.S.P., the Socialists, the Jan Sangh, Ranga's own Swatantra bloc and Frank Anthony's independents refused to go along. Assured by Shri Satya Narain Singh that Government would not seek Parliament's formal endorsement and would vote down any amendment, the opposition chose to file no amendment at all.
  
  The letter then lays out the tactical logic behind that choice. Talking the matter out, Ranga argues, keeps the Chinese guessing, denies the Government any positive parliamentary authority to negotiate, and preserves the unanimous 14 November resolution as the country's anchoring position. It also signals to friendly powers that domestic forces will not permit the Prime Minister to conclude a premature or weak-kneed settlement that would compromise those who came to India's aid.
  
  Ranga next turns to the Constitution Amendment Bill directed at the D.M.K. Because Rajaji has publicly questioned the wisdom of introducing such a measure, Ranga has instructed the Swatantra parliamentary party to support it silently, by vote alone, without speeches — a compromise he secured after P. K. Deo's own wish to speak in the Bill's favour. He closes by noting the division of labour for the Colombo debate: he will open the party's case the next day and P. K. Deo will sum up. Two draft telegrams appended to the letter — one to Masani and Pillo Mody in Bombay, one to Rajaji in Madras — restate the same line: the talking-out plan has been accepted by all parties, silent support for the integration Bill is recommended, the Maharaja of Kalahandi wishes to speak in its favour, and Ranga himself is disinclined to join the march-past.

## Key points

- Ranga updates Rajaji after a fresh meeting with Nehru, who disclosed that he had written to Mrs. Bandaranaike accepting the Colombo proposals in principle and that the Chinese had reciprocated.
- Swatantra, PSP, Socialists, Jan Sangh and Frank Anthony's independents refused to accept the Colombo proposals; the opposition decided to file no amendment after Satya Narain Singh confirmed Government would not seek Parliament's approval.
- The talking-out strategy is designed to keep the Chinese guessing, deny Government positive negotiating authority, and preserve the unanimous 14 November resolution as the country's official stand.
- Ranga wants to signal to friendly powers that domestic opposition will not allow Nehru to reach a premature or 'week-need' [weak-kneed] agreement that would compromise India's supporters.
- On the Constitution Amendment Bill aimed at the D.M.K., Ranga has told Swatantra members to support it silently by vote — no speeches — deferring to Rajaji's public misgivings about the Bill.
- P. K. Deo, initially keen to speak in support of the integration Bill, agreed to Ranga's silent-support line; Ranga will open the Colombo debate the next day and Deo will wind up.
- Two draft telegrams accompany the letter: one to Masani and Pillo Mody at 143 Mahatma Gandhi Road, Bombay, and one to Rajaji at Kalki, Kilpauk, Madras, both summarising the party's tactical line.
- The telegrams add that the Maharaja of Kalahandi (P. K. Deo) wishes to speak in support of anti-disintegration, while Ranga himself is 'disinclined joining marchpast'.
