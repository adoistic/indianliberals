summary: |-
  Deepak Lal argues that India has always had some classical-liberal voices but never a vibrant tradition. He points to the Swatantra Party, founded around 1960 and briefly allied with the Jana Sangh, and notes that after its decline Masani was left almost alone in carrying the liberal flag in politics until his death. He identifies the dominant obstacle as a deeply ingrained 'Nehruvian mindset', with Gandhian thought as the main alternative, and is dismissive of the trio of thinkers Modi reportedly praised as India's greatest, Deendayal Upadhyaya, Mahatma Gandhi, and Ram Manohar Lohia, warning against grounding economic policy in any of them.

  He singles out B. R. Shenoy as the most vocal liberal economist, recalling that Shenoy wrote a dissenting note on the Second Plan and was ostracised by the planning establishment but stuck to his position. Lal notes Shenoy was a member of the Mont Pelerin Society and attended IEA gatherings, where he found relief that his free-market views, treated as insane in India, were not mad. He mentions a successor, Vyas, who tried to continue Shenoy's work but was of lesser calibre, and concludes that while more Indians now lean toward free markets, classical liberalism remains a weak tradition.
