## Summary

In these seven rendered pages, M. R. Masani argues that Indian economic policy has consistently undervalued agriculture and rural producers in favour of heavy industry, urban interests, and expanding state control. Speaking at the 86th Annual General Meeting of the United Planters’ Association of Southern India in 1979, he presents agriculture as India’s foundational industry and calls for a balanced development strategy. He uses wholesale-price comparisons to illustrate the disadvantage faced by farmers, whose returns lag behind the prices they pay for manufactured goods, fuel, fertiliser, machinery, and other inputs.

Masani advocates family farming and peasant proprietorship for food grains, while defending larger plantations for cash crops such as tea and coffee. He criticises export duties, ceiling legislation, excessive taxation, bureaucratic marketing controls, public expenditure, and the wider “Statist” pattern of the Indian economy. The speech then broadens into a defence of competitive markets, reduced regulation, and a more limited but clean and effective administration. Masani links excessive government control to political corruption and argues that independent social forces—including the plantation industry and UPASI—are necessary to preserve a free and pluralistic society. The closing pages place national renewal in the hands of teachers, doctors, nurses, entrepreneurs, parents, social workers, and others who serve through private initiative and civic commitment.

## Key points

- Masani identifies the neglect of agriculture and the prioritisation of heavy industry as central causes of India’s rural and economic difficulties.
- He presents agriculture as India’s basic industry and argues that national policy must recognise the country’s continuing agrarian character.
- The speech supports family farming and peasant proprietorship for food grains, while endorsing larger plantation units for tea, coffee, and other cash crops.
- Price-index figures are used to show that farmers receive less favourable prices for their products than they pay for non-farm goods and agricultural inputs.
- Masani criticises export duties, land-ceiling restrictions, excessive taxation, state trading, and bureaucratic regulation of plantation agriculture.
- He argues that excessive public expenditure and state control have produced inflation, concentrated power in New Delhi, and weakened political standards.
- The speech calls for competitive markets, de-control, clean administration, and government attention to core functions such as law and order and infrastructure.
- Masani concludes that India will be sustained not primarily by politicians but by teachers, medical workers, entrepreneurs, parents, social workers, and patriotic citizens.
