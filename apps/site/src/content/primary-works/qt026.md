# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 26 (July-September 1960) is a wide-ranging quarterly issue combining literary criticism, political-economic argument, poetry, fiction, historiography, an in-depth rebuttal of Edward Shils' sociological study of the Indian intellectual, reportage on a seminar on literary obscenity, an extended translated Polish philosophical polemic on Marx's theory of truth, a substantial book-reviews section, and correspondence. Its editorial marks two years under Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta and restates the magazine's identity as a 'journal of ideas' (its motto drawn from Whitehead's Adventures of Ideas) rather than a magazine of creative arts, while defending the continued inclusion of poetry and short fiction. The issue's centre of gravity is the debate, across several pieces, about India's cultural and intellectual condition after independence -- from Sudhindranath Datta's essay on Victor Hugo and Madhao Achwal's theory of literary obscenity, to Amlan Datta's argument against a rigid capitalism/socialism dichotomy of property, to Vinod Sena's long counter-statement to Edward Shils on the 'dilemmas of the Indian intellectual,' which argues that British colonial education created a lasting cultural dualism that only a revival of indigenous tradition (as in Vivekananda, Tagore and Gandhi) can resolve. Two Warsaw-based Marxist philosophers, Leszek Kolakowski and Adam Schaff, contribute a translated exchange on the epistemology of the young versus the mature Marx. The issue closes with reportage on a Bombay seminar on obscenity in literature, a discursive books column, six book reviews (on Indian modern art, Tagore translations, a Tamil novel, African nationalism, an American poet, and a Churchill pictorial biography), and two letters continuing earlier debates on the Swatantra Party and on Shils' essay.

## Essays

### Editorial
An unsigned editorial (voiced collectively by editors Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta) marking two years since they took over Quest, restating and amplifying the policy statement first laid down in Quest 17 (April-June 1958). It defends the magazine's 'heavier fare' against complaints that Quest is too heavy or insufficiently bright, and diagnoses India's 'tragic lack of realisation of the value of cultural freedom' as arising from three causes: a long tradition of authoritarianism that has 'prepared the soil' for totalitarianism, a poverty so severe that freedom of culture seems an 'expendable quantity,' and the deeper claim that India has 'ceased to possess a living culture' in the realm of ideas even while its ancient heritage remains alive. It sets out Quest's self-appointed role in 'cultural revitalisation,' argues that English -- though not a plausible medium for Indian creative literature -- is nonetheless the necessary common language of Indian thought pending the maturing of the regional languages, and a postscript explains why the magazine nonetheless retains poetry and short fiction sections.

- Marks two years since Ayyub and Datta's editorship began, citing Quest 17 (April-June 1958) as the founding policy statement.
- Restates Quest's identity as 'a quarterly journal of enquiry, criticism and constructive thought' rather than 'a bimonthly of arts and ideas.'
- Diagnoses India's lack of cultural freedom to three causes: authoritarian tradition, poverty-driven indifference, and a 'dead' contemporary culture of ideas.
- Argues English cannot be a natural creative medium for Indians but remains indispensable as the common language of Indian thought.
- Reveals the magazine's motto is drawn from Whitehead's Adventures of Ideas.
- Postscript defends retaining poetry and short-story sections despite scepticism about creative writing in English or in translation.

### Hugo and Others
*By Sudhindranath Datta*

Marking the seventy-fifth anniversary of Victor Hugo's death, Sudhindranath Datta mounts a defence of Hugo's 'absolute greatness' against a modern reader's instinctive coolness toward him. He catalogues Hugo's personal moral inconsistencies (romantic promiscuity, bourgeois possessiveness toward his wife, political opportunism) at length, then argues that a great writer's very faults 'rescue him from oblivion' by first provoking and then converting critical hostility. The essay ranges comparatively across Racine, Tagore, Baudelaire, Mallarme, Rimbaud (via J. M. Cohen's Poetry of this Age), Lorca, Yeats and Tennyson, using Hugo's visual imagination, vast vocabulary and formal daring as the true grounds of his stature, and closes by proposing three possible objective standards of criticism -- endurance, intention, and the gap between aim and achievement -- any one of which, Datta argues, secures Hugo's place among the immortals.

- Marks Hugo's seventy-fifth death anniversary and recalls the scale of his 1885 funeral.
- Catalogues Hugo's personal 'inconsistency between words and deeds' at length before arguing this does not diminish his poetic achievement.
- Compares Hugo's visually organised imagination explicitly to Tagore's, finding Hugo achieves a pictorial unity Tagore's Bengali seldom attains.
- Uses J. M. Cohen's Poetry of this Age to discuss Lorca's and Yeats's compression of imagery against Hugo's more expansive method.
- Contrasts Hugo's Le Cimetiere d'Eylau with Tennyson's Charge of the Light Brigade as poetry versus propaganda on the same patriotic theme.
- Concludes that time's test, sincerity of intention, or the distance between aim and achievement would each independently establish Hugo's greatness.

### The Literary Aspect of Obscenity
*By Madhao B. Achwal*

Madhao B. Achwal argues that obscenity is fundamentally an extra-literary, sociological category rather than an aesthetic one: literature can only be judged artistic, inartistic, or non-artistic, never moral or immoral, because its content is 'a-moral.' A literary work succeeds when its elements are organised into a 'dynamic equilibrium' that creates the illusion of life; obscenity results not from the choice of sexual subject matter but from bad art -- content used purely for 'sense-stimulating' effect rather than integrated into an aesthetic whole. He tests the theory against Lady Chatterley's Lover (saved from obscenity by its 'predominant mood' of protest), Madame Bovary, James Jones' From Here to Eternity, and Moravia's Time of Indifference, and closes by arguing that positive cultivation of aesthetic consciousness, not negative legal suppression, is the only real remedy for obscenity.

- Defines obscenity as a 'social fact' rather than an 'aesthetic fact,' making it an extra-literary category.
- Argues a literary work is one whose internal tensions reach a perceptible 'dynamic equilibrium.'
- Uses Lady Chatterley's Lover, From Here to Eternity, Madame Bovary and Time of Indifference as test cases distinguishing artistically 'inevitable' content from gratuitous sense-stimulation.
- Coins the memorable formula distinguishing the artistically justified nude from 'the nude with socks.'
- Concludes that society's laws on obscenity have so far been 'entirely negative' and recommends cultivating positive aesthetic consciousness instead.

### Economic Transition and the Concept of Property
*By Amlan Datta*

Amlan Datta challenges the Marxist habit of treating 'capitalism' and 'socialism' as an absolute dichotomy of proprietorship versus expropriation, arguing that in practice property is always 'a bundle of finely adjusted rights' shared among individuals, groups and the state, so that 'absolute proprietary rights exist only as a legal fiction.' He surveys Yugoslav market-socialist 'revisionism' and Gandhian trusteeship as alternatives to the rigid dichotomy, then turns to a comparative study of economic leadership in transition: Japan's Meiji-era samurai-led modernisation, which drew on traditional prestige to win the peasantry's consent with a minimum of social dislocation, is contrasted with the Bolsheviks' post-1917 leadership, alien to the peasantry and reliant on terror to force collectivisation. Datta concludes that transitions succeed with least waste when a society's economic, political and intellectual leaderships remain distinct yet mutually reinforcing, and warns that any 'absolutist' concept of property -- recognising only unlimited private or state ownership -- tends to concentrate power dangerously.

- Argues Marxist and non-Marxist historians' stage-theories of economic development do not actually correspond neatly to each other.
- Property in practice is 'a bundle of finely adjusted rights' among multiple parties, not an absolute; 'absolute proprietary rights exist only as a legal fiction.'
- Cites Yugoslav market-socialism and Gandhian trusteeship as evidence that the capitalism/socialism dichotomy is dissolving in practice.
- Contrasts Japan's Meiji Restoration (traditional leadership evolving with peasant consent) with the Bolshevik Revolution (alien leadership relying on terror against the peasantry) as a study in the costs of transition.
- Warns that a society's economic, political and intellectual leaderships should remain distinct, since concentrating all three in one hand -- as an absolutist concept of property encourages -- is dangerous.

### The Nature and Use of Criticism
*By N. K. Devaraja*

N. K. Devaraja offers a philosophical account of literary criticism as the combination of 'analysis' (disclosing the qualities that make a work's 'enjoyed experience' valuable) and 'interpretation' (placing the work within its cultural-historical milieu). He argues that no evaluative judgement of a poem can be made in isolation: a critic's competence rests entirely on comparison with other works previously read and enjoyed, so that ultimate critical standards are, in the end, the accumulated 'overall feelings' left by acknowledged masterpieces rather than any single theory (vividness, organic unity, realism, and so on) claimed as the exclusive cause of artistic greatness. He closes by distinguishing criticism, as a philosophical activity concerned with particular works, from aesthetics, which concerns itself with the general forms of art.

- Defines the critic's task as making the enjoyment of a work 'an articulate and conscious process.'
- Distinguishes analysis (isolating constituent qualities) from interpretation (situating the work historically and culturally).
- Argues evaluative judgement is inherently comparative, requiring a critic's prior experience of other works.
- Suggests language differentiates the poetry of different ages more than it differentiates contemporary poets from one another.
- Concludes criticism is philosophical in character, distinguished from aesthetics by its focus on particular works rather than general forms.

### In Search of a Future
*By Sushil Dey*

Writing from long personal observation as an FAO official meeting young Indians studying abroad, Sushil Dey reports that today's overseas students are markedly abler than earlier generations but also more disillusioned, torn between idealistic hopes of serving their country and a dawning conviction that they will find neither reward nor scope for their skills at home; he illustrates the resulting national 'wastage of skill' with the case of a brilliant nuclear researcher who returned to India only to be starved of facilities and pay. Dey then broadens the argument to national planning itself, using an account of a village's own bottom-up planning process (for irrigation, roads, a school, a clinic) to argue that development plans imposed only 'arithmetically' from a central planning elite, without genuine village-level participation, are the deeper cause of the country's pervasive disorientation. He concludes that the young generation's real peril is not material want but the absence of 'a large and liberating cause,' calling for political education in the classical sense.

- Reports, from decades of contact with Indian students abroad, that today's overseas students are more capable but also more disillusioned than earlier generations.
- Illustrates the country's 'wastage of skill' with the case of a nuclear-physics researcher who returned to India to poor facilities and pay.
- Distinguishes external brain-drain from the larger, internal wastage caused by unsuitable placement and discouraging work conditions.
- Uses a first-hand account of bottom-up village planning (irrigation, roads, school, clinic) as a model that national planning fails to emulate.
- Concludes the young generation's crisis is the absence of 'a large and liberating cause,' not material privation, and calls for political education.

### Lamaism
*By D. B. Vohra*

D. B. Vohra surveys the history of Tibetan Lamaism from its 8th-century founding by the Indian missionary Padma Sambhava, whose Tantric Buddhism fused with the animist Bon-po religion of Tibet, through the Guhya-samaja Tantra's doctrine of Nirvana attainable in one lifetime via the union of male and female (Prajna) principles, to the successive reform movements of Atisa (11th century) and Tsang-Khapa (from 1390), whose strict, celibate 'Yellow Cap' Gelugpa sect eventually triumphed over the older 'Red Caps' with Mongol military backing. He traces the Mongol-conferred title of Dalai Lama (from 1578) and the resulting doctrine of Lamas as recognised reincarnations, quotes the French missionaries Hue and Gabet's 1842 observation of Lamaism's ritual resemblance to Catholicism, and closes by describing the everyday popular practice of the mantra 'Om Mani Padme Hum' via prayer wheels and prayer flags.

- Opens with Kublai Khan's 13th-century Xanadu assembly of religious 'savants,' won by the Tibetan Lama Phags-pa.
- Traces Lamaism's founding to the 8th-century Indian missionary Padma Sambhava and its fusion with Tibet's animist Bon-po religion.
- Explains the Guhya-samaja Tantra's doctrine of Nirvana attainable in one lifetime through union of the male and female (Prajna) principles.
- Describes the reform movements of Atisa and Tsang-Khapa, producing the 'Red Cap' and 'Yellow Cap' (Gelugpa) sects and, with Mongol backing, the Dalai Lama lineage from 1578.
- Quotes the 1842 French missionaries Hue and Gabet on Lamaism's ritual resemblance to Catholicism, while noting the direction of influence is 'a moot point.'

### Historical Rethinking
*By Kirti N. Chaudhuri*

Kirti N. Chaudhuri surveys the mid-twentieth-century shift in historical method away from constitutional/political explanation toward economic and institutional analysis, opening with F. M. Cornford's argument that Thucydides' narrative was itself shaped by the model of Greek tragedy, and tracing the influence of Ranke's 'objective' method, Machiavelli's cyclical statecraft, and Karl Wittfogel's The Oriental Despotism. He gives an extended account of the Whig historiographical tradition of English constitutional progress (Hallam) and its overturning by Trevor-Roper and others, focusing especially on the Tawney/Laurence Stone versus Trevor-Roper controversy over whether the 'Gentry' rose or declined economically before the English Civil War -- a controversy Chaudhuri suggests has 'peculiar relevance' to India's post-independence professional middle class displacing the princely order. He closes with Charles Boxer's and J. C. van Leur's revisionist historiography of European contact with Asia, which replaced assumptions of European moral or military superiority with an account of largely equal trading relations disrupted only later by industrial-era power.

- Opens with F. M. Cornford's thesis that Thucydides' history was shaped by the pattern of Greek tragedy.
- Traces the shift from Rankean political history to economic determinism, citing Karl Wittfogel's The Oriental Despotism on 'Oriental society.'
- Surveys the Whig historiographical tradition (Hallam) of English constitutional progress and its mid-century overturning (Trevor-Roper, G. R. Elton).
- Details the Tawney/Laurence Stone vs Trevor-Roper controversy over the rise or decline of the English 'Gentry' before the Civil War, drawing a tentative parallel to India's post-independence middle class.
- Closes with Charles Boxer's and J. C. van Leur's revisionist accounts of European-Asian trade contact, arguing against attributing early European dominance to moral or military superiority.

### The Party and the Truth
*By Kolakowski and Schaff*

Translated extracts from a 1959 Polish philosophical controversy. Leszek Kolakowski (Professor of Philosophy, University of Warsaw) argues that the young Marx's 1844 Manuscripts contain an 'embryonic' epistemology in which practical human activity does not merely verify but actually constitutes reality and truth -- a reading Kolakowski likens to Spinoza and distinguishes from both the 'classical' correspondence theory of truth later associated with Engels and Lenin, and from Jamesian Pragmatism. Adam Schaff (also a Professor of Philosophy at Warsaw and a member of the Central Committee of the Polish Communist Party) rebuts this at length as a 'crazy construction': he argues Marx's thought was continuously materialist from the Theses on Feuerbach onward, that Marx never departed from Engels' account of dialectical materialism, and that Kolakowski's reading illegitimately opposes an immature 'young Marx' to the mature Marx and to Engels/Lenin, effectively turning Marx into 'a pure subjective idealist.'

- Kolakowski argues the young Marx's 1844 Manuscripts imply that human practical activity does not just verify but actually constitutes reality, resembling Spinoza's philosophy in this respect.
- Kolakowski distinguishes this reading from both the classical correspondence theory of truth (associated with Engels and Lenin) and from Jamesian Pragmatism.
- Schaff rejects the young Marx/mature Marx distinction as historically false, citing the Theses on Feuerbach and Marx's continuing endorsement of Engels' philosophical writing.
- Schaff frames the dispute as a test of materialism versus idealism, charging that Kolakowski's argument effectively makes Marx 'a pure subjective idealist.'
- Both authors are identified as Professors of Philosophy at the University of Warsaw; Schaff is additionally identified as a member of the Central Committee of the Polish Communist Party.

### Lines for Stanley Kevelson
*By R. de Loyola Furtado*

A three-part philosophical poem addressed to 'Stanley Kevelson,' opening with an epigraph from Simonides on Orpheus, that turns on the temptation and danger of holding 'a positive mind' -- mocking hair-splitting indecision in part I, questioning whether the intellect should 'dissect' the 'siren-song of mystery' in part II, and closing in part III on the unresolved, almost comic tension between scepticism ('Nothing') and conviction.

- Epigraph quotes Simonides on Orpheus charming birds and fish.
- Part I mocks the habit of endless hedging ('splitting your hairs / About either/or').
- Part II asks whether 'the probing intellect' should be allowed to 'ravish' the 'siren-song of mystery.'
- Part III ends on the comic, unresolved tension between doubt and conviction, 'seeking penetration / Not obliteration.'

### These Girls
*By Amaresh Datta*

A poem imagining young women as transient natural forces -- 'broken waters,' 'pleasant and meaningless songs of birds' -- who either die into motherhood or, refusing to vanish, persist at the world's margins; it ends with a cosmic image of renewal after the world's end, in which they 'rise first as flowers for the bees.'

- Compares young women successively to breaking waves, birdsong, and hunting 'green eyes of the prey.'
- States they 'die, for the girls are born to die / Into mothers' or else persist, refusing to vanish from men's dreams.
- Closes on an apocalyptic-then-renewing image: after the world lies 'buried under another world of rocks and seas,' they rise again 'as flowers for the bees.'

### Nothing Secures Him
*By Keshev Malik*

A poem of interior restlessness: an unnamed 'he' is never secured by reassurance, launching repeatedly on 'similar schemes' and retreating into 'confusions.' He commands his inner 'music' either to 'emerge full-bodied' now or never, fearing it will die unborn, and the poem ends with this tension slowly dissolving, 'glacier-wise,' into 'waves of milk.'

- Describes a figure who is never reassured and repeatedly retreats from his own aspirations in 'confusions.'
- Central imperative: 'show your face now or never... Or lie still upon the cold heart pyre,' addressed to his own creative impulse.
- Ends with the tension resolving slowly into stillness, likened to a glacier meeting warm seas.

### The Birth of a Butterfly
*By Lila Ray*

A short poem tracing a butterfly's emergence from the chrysalis -- 'like a headache' the shell splits -- through its vulnerable, wet-winged clinging, to the wings drying and stiffening, and finally its 'frilled flight' out of 'perilous seclusion' into sunlight.

- Opens with the chrysalis splitting compared to a headache.
- Describes the newborn butterfly as 'small and soft and sodden,' fighting the 'downward drag' of wet wings.
- Ends with the wings drying, stiffening, and the butterfly's first flight into sunlight.

### The Angel of Vengeance
*By Jeremy Nelson*

A short story intercutting the death of an unnamed, poor young man ('vomited blood and pus over his hands and died') with the story of Joe Guggenheim, a wealthy, whisky-loving philanthropist whose charitable barbecues for the poor mask years of recurring nightmares of blood, judgment and a faceless 'presence.' At a barbecue party Joe, drunk and staring into the coals, finally breaks down entirely, runs among the dancers with a poker shouting 'I am the angel of vengeance!', wounds a guest, and is beaten into submission and committed to an asylum -- while, unnoticed, the same poor young man dies the same night 'unmourned except by an angel.'

- Opens and closes with the framing death of an unnamed poor young man, mourned only by 'an angel.'
- Satirises Mrs. Guggenheim's charity circuit, The Women's Charitable League, and its patronising rhetoric about 'the poor.'
- Traces Joe Guggenheim's escalating private nightmares of blood alongside his public reputation as 'Guggenheim the jolly fellow.'
- Climaxes at a barbecue where Joe, in a psychotic episode, declares himself 'the angel of vengeance' and attacks the guests with a poker before being subdued and committed.
- The story's irony rests on the contrast between the rich man's melodramatic public breakdown and the poor man's silent, unmourned death.

### Metamorphosis
*By Jai Nimbkar*

A man visits a once-celebrated, now fat and crippled classical singer, Tarabai Kharkar, whom he had loved and proposed to as a young man in her family's garden (she refused, mocking herself as 'a cripple'). Meeting her again after her concert, he finds his romantic memory deflated by her worldly self-possession, her laughter at his old proposal, and his own diminished sense of himself as merely 'a clerk in the post office.' Leaving, he realises the 'change' in her is only superficial: she was always this confident and successful in her own terms, while he is the one who has 'blundered into the world.'

- The narrator visits Tarabai Kharkar, a famous singer he had proposed to years earlier, who had refused him, joking about her own disability.
- The reunion deflates his nostalgic image of her as fragile and ethereal; she is now fat, crippled, and worldly-confident.
- He feels shame at his own comparatively unglamorous life as 'a clerk in the post office.'
- The story ends with his realisation that she has not really changed -- she always regarded the world as 'her oyster' -- while he was the one who misjudged reality.

### The Indian Intellectual: Reply to Shils
*By Vinod Sena*

Vinod Sena mounts a sustained rebuttal of sociologist Edward Shils' Quest essay 'The Culture of the Indian Intellectual,' which had argued that the Indian intellectual's sense of alienation is a 'romantic distortion' since he remains, at bottom, thoroughly Indian in upbringing and sensibility. Sena argues Shils' interview-based method cannot grasp a 'living' culture, and that Shils fails to see how uniquely damaging British rule was: unlike earlier conquerors (Chingez Khan, Timur, even Aurangzeb), the British neither destroyed native tradition outright nor settled and intermingled with it, but instead, as merchants-turned-rulers convinced of their own civilisational superiority, imposed an English-medium education that created a lasting 'fatal dualism' between everything 'English' and everything 'Indian' in the intellectual's mind. Sena traces this dualism through language, culture and politics -- including the earlier appeal and subsequent disillusionment with Communism after Hungary, Tibet and Chinese 'encroachments' -- and closes by proposing that a revival and reinterpretation of India's own spiritual and philosophical traditions, as achieved by Vivekananda, Tagore and Gandhi, offers the only real route out of the intellectual's paralysis, rather than substituting one borrowed model (Western liberal-democratic or Soviet) for another.

- Credits Edward Shils with a valuable pioneering study of the Indian intellectual but disputes his interview-based method as unable to grasp a 'living' culture.
- Argues British colonial education uniquely created a 'fatal dualism' between 'Indian' and 'English' identity, unlike the effect of earlier conquerors.
- Cites Nehru's description of himself as 'a queer mixture of East and West, out of place everywhere, at home no where' as evidence against Shils' thesis.
- Traces the Indian intellectual's earlier sympathy for Communism (as anti-British defiance) and subsequent disillusionment after Hungary, Tibet, and Chinese incursions.
- Proposes that Vivekananda, Tagore and Gandhi modelled the way out of cultural dualism by reviving, rather than abandoning, indigenous tradition.

### Seminar on Obscenity in Literature
*By Gangadhar Gadgil*

Gangadhar Gadgil reports on a two-day seminar on 'Obscenity in Literature,' jointly organised by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom and the P.E.N. All-India Centre on 6-7 February. Smt. Bhagwat opened by arguing that Victorian prudery is foreign to Indian tradition and that the obscene has always had a legitimate place in Indian culture; Prof. A. R. Wadia took a cautious middle position; lawyer C. C. Shah surveyed comparative obscenity law (the US most liberal, Japan most conservative, Indian courts influenced by both British and American precedent); Gujarati writer Jyotindra Dave discussed the Sanskrit principle of auchitya (propriety) as a still-relevant guide; and Madhao Achwal previewed the extra-literary theory of obscenity published as his own essay elsewhere in this same issue. No consensus definition of obscenity emerged, though most participants agreed pure pornography could be identified and discouraged.

- Two-day seminar (6-7 February) jointly organised by the I.C.C.F. and the P.E.N. All India Centre.
- Smt. Bhagwat argued the 'obscene' is not foreign to Indian tradition and is not inherently socially harmful, distinguishing it from the 'pornographic.'
- Lawyer C. C. Shah compared obscenity law across the US, Britain, Japan and India.
