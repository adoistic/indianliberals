summary: |-
  This typescript by J. B. Kripalani lays out a six-point minimum common programme he first sketched in his inaugural address at the Unity Conference at Lucknow, convened by members of the P.S.P. and S.P., as a basis on which democratic opposition parties might unite against the Congress. His premise is arithmetical: the Congress has swept post-independence elections with less than fifty per cent of the vote, because the anti-Congress vote is scattered across rival parties. A unified opposition, agreeing to run only one candidate per constituency against Congress and confining its shared platform to a small set of items, is the only way to displace what he portrays as a corrupt, complacent ruling establishment.
  
  The six items span administration, foreign policy, national unity, ideology, and the economy. The first is honest, efficient administration and an opposition-led scrutiny of official corruption, including independent commissions of enquiry when ministers protect the guilty. The second is unbroken resistance to Communist China until every inch of Indian soil under Chinese occupation is recovered; Kripalani mocks fatalistic sentiments (attributed to leading Congressmen and even to Srimati Indira Gandhi at Amroha) that dismiss the lost Ladakh territory as barren snow, and blames a de facto ceasefire that leaves the border a no-man's land. The third calls for active goodwill missions and popular understanding across the two halves of the partitioned sub-continent, treating friendship with Pakistan as an educational project that opposition parties must lead if Congress will not.
  
  Items four and five contain the polemical heart of the pamphlet: Kripalani wants "social justice" substituted for "socialism" as the declared goal of national endeavour, arguing that after Marx socialism collapsed into the nationalisation of industry, and thence into State capitalism, mechanised big industry, and — through all-encompassing planning — totalitarianism of either the Right or the Left. He invokes Gandhian trusteeship, the Sarvodaya movement's Bhoodan, Gramdan, Sampatidan and Shramdan, co-management, profit-sharing, high income and property taxation, co-operatives, and decentralised electric-powered industry as non-violent, non-dictatorial paths to the same end. The fifth item therefore asks for a moratorium on further nationalisation until existing State enterprises run as efficiently as well-managed private ones, invoking a Parliamentary intervention on the Third Five Year Plan in which he warned that, like Alexander, Napoleon and Hitler, India should not extend an empire of nationalised industry without first consolidating what has already been undertaken.
  
  The sixth item completes the economic picture with effective regulatory control, not ownership, of the private sector: no cartels or combines, limits on family holdings in joint-stock and limited liability companies, regulated profits tied to costs, and State standardisation of goods, so that private enterprise is bent to a social purpose. Kripalani is careful to describe the whole programme as tentative — meant to strengthen the opposition and the health of democracy rather than to form a government — and closes by insisting that only an agreed minimum programme, adopted by a properly convened meeting of opposition leaders, can produce the single joint candidate per seat that alone can defeat Congress at the polls.

## Key points

- The programme originates in Kripalani's inaugural address at the Unity Conference at Lucknow, convened by members of the P.S.P. and S.P., and is offered as a tentative basis for opposition unity, not a governing manifesto.
- Item one demands clean and efficient administration; Kripalani argues that Congress ministers protect corrupt officials and proposes that a united opposition set up its own Commissions of Enquiry to expose ministerial misconduct.
- Item two insists on continued resistance to Communist China until every inch of occupied Indian territory is recovered, condemning the drift into a de facto bilateral ceasefire and the willingness of some leading Congress figures — including Indira Gandhi at Amroha — to write off snow-covered mountain tops.
- Item three treats reconciliation with Pakistan and mutual goodwill missions across the partitioned sub-continent as a task the democratic opposition must shoulder if the ruling party will not educate people about the value of good neighbourly relations.
- Item four asks that "social justice" replace "socialism" as the goal of national endeavour, on the argument that post-Marx socialism has become synonymous with nationalisation, State capitalism, mechanised big industry and totalitarian planning, and that Gandhian trusteeship, Sarvodaya (Bhoodan, Gramdan, Sampatidan, Shramdan), profit-sharing, co-operatives and decentralised industry can deliver social justice without violence or dictatorship.
- Item five imposes a moratorium on further nationalisation until existing public undertakings match well-managed private ones in efficiency, invoking his Parliamentary critique of the Third Five Year Plan and the warning against extending an empire of nationalised industry "like Alexander, Napoleon and Hitler" without prior consolidation.
- Item six calls for effective regulation rather than ownership of the private sector — bans on combines and cartels, limits on individual and family holdings in joint-stock companies, profit and price regulation tied to costs, and State standardisation of goods — so that private enterprise serves a social purpose.
- The whole programme is presented as electorally instrumental: only an agreed minimum common platform can persuade the various opposition parties to put up a single joint candidate per constituency, as Kripalani says was done in Rajkot and Amroha, and so defeat Congress at the polls.
