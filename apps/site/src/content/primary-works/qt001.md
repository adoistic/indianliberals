# Quest

## Summary

This is the inaugural issue of Quest — Vol. I, No. 1, August 1955 — a bi-monthly of arts and ideas edited by Nissim Ezekiel and sponsored by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, itself affiliated to the Paris-based Congress for Cultural Freedom. In the rendered pages, which cover roughly the first quarter of this 72-page issue, the front matter identifies Quest's sponsoring bodies and, via Ezekiel's editorial, sets out the magazine's stated purpose: to offer serious discussion and criticism as an alternative to propaganda and 'didactic' enlightenment, while admitting that its 'dynamism is also ideological' and 'concerned with the relation between politics and culture.' The rendered pages then carry the issue's first article in full — M. G. Bailur's polemical critique of the Bhoodan and Sarvodaya land-gift movements associated with Acharya Vinoba Bhave — followed by a short complete poem by Molly Daniels ('To...'), and the opening chapters, through printed page 18, of Narendra Mitra's Bengali short story 'Sap' (translated by Lila Ray), about a palm-sap tapper's successive marriages in rural Bengal.

## Essays

### Essay 0
In the rendered pages, Nissim Ezekiel's editorial to this first issue disclaims any special claim to excellence for the material assembled, arguing instead that Quest's justification lies in the kind of journal it can be: an honest, natural vehicle of 'mind and sensibility' independent of the media created for particular audiences, and a corrective to journals dependent on 'rehashing the stereotyped ingredients of mass media.' He argues discussion and exposition are better instruments of an idea than propaganda, pamphlets, or mere pronouncement, and notes with some irony that a friendly columnist found the word 'critical' in Quest's sub-title 'suspicious.'

- States Quest's aim is quality and deliberately 'provocative' seriousness rather than claimed excellence, positioned against mass-circulation journalism.
- Frames discussion and exposition as superior instruments of ideas than propaganda, pamphlets or speeches.
- Notes that a fellow columnist found the word 'critical' in Quest's sub-title suspicious, and defends criticism as a legitimate, necessary function of intelligence.
- Acknowledges Quest is ideological as well as intellectual, being sponsored by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom and 'concerned with the relation between politics and culture.'
- Singles out Narendra Mitra's story 'Sap,' published in English for the first time, as the editorial's proudest inclusion.

### Bhoodan and Sarvodaya
*By M. G. Bailur*

In the rendered pages, M. G. Bailur's essay 'Bhoodan and Sarvodaya: a critique of an ideology' argues that Sarvodaya's professed aim of universal welfare is a tautology already claimed by every government, and that Acharya Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan (land-gift) movement rests on unverifiable moral exhortation and a 'whole mythology' of false sociology rather than demonstrable results. Bailur contrasts the movement's romanticised village self-sufficiency and idealisation of poverty and manual toil with Jawaharlal Nehru's technocratic, industrial vision of development, and separately criticises Rajendra Prasad's rejection, at a Nai Talim education conference, of science as incompatible with an 'Eastern conception of happiness.'

In the rendered pages, the essay's core argument is empiricist: Bailur insists 'facts, verifiable results, are the only criterion,' not faith, mythology, or 'exalted delusions about the past.' He quotes Vinoba Bhave's own stated target of gathering fifty million acres from India's three hundred million cultivated acres and argues this assumes society will simply 'stay put' rather than change. He concludes that Bhoodan's ethic of faith, sacrifice and austerity does not abolish poverty but formally idealises and perpetuates it, and questions Vinoba Bhave's right to 'legislate for other people's happiness' without asking whether they want the life he prescribes.

- Opens by calling Sarvodaya's aim of 'the welfare of all' a tautology already professed by every government and political party.
- Traces the basic-education debate, contrasting Nehru's reading of it against Mahatma Gandhi's original conception and Rajendra Prasad's objection that it was being distorted into 'a romantic return to the past.'
- Applies an empiricist test to Bhoodan: verifiable results, not faith or mythology, must be the criterion of its success.
- Cites Vinoba Bhave's stated target of 50 million acres out of 300 million cultivated acres in India, arguing the plan assumes a static society.
- Argues Bhoodan's ideal of subsistence living sustained by 'faith, sacrifice and austerity' amounts to permanently idealising poverty rather than ending it.
- Criticises Rajendra Prasad's rejection of science as incompatible with an 'Eastern conception of happiness,' calling this inconsistent with the deprivation of India's 'half-starved masses.'

### Sap
*By Narendra Mitra*

In the rendered pages, Narendra Mitra's story 'Sap' (translated from Bengali by Lila Ray) follows Motalef, a skilled date-palm tapper, through a sequence of marriages shaped by economic calculation and desire. He marries the widow Maju Khatun mainly for her labour boiling sap into sugar, then covets the younger, recently divorced Fulbanu; her father Elam Shekh demands a bride-price Motalef cannot at first afford. Over a tapping season Motalef earns the money (while coming to suspect Maju Khatun of skimming sugar), divorces Maju Khatun, and pays to marry Fulbanu, who then joins him in a year of shared agricultural labour — paddy harvesting, jute retting, and palm tapping.

In the rendered pages, Maju Khatun is in turn matched by Rajek's brother Wahed to a widowed boatman, Nadir Shah, and leaves across the river, while Motalef and Fulbanu settle into an affectionate domestic routine through the following seasons. The narrative breaks off, in the pages seen, as a new and more prosperous tapping season begins in earnest; the table of contents lists the story running to printed page 26, well beyond where the rendered pages end at printed page 18, so its remainder and resolution were not seen.

### All Play and No Work
*By S. Ookerjee*

Complete within the rendered pages (printed pp. 27–31), this essay is a sustained polemic on the state of Indian university education. Ookerjee catalogues 'traditions' of student indiscipline — skipping lectures, disrupting class with paper-arrows and whistling, and studying only from 'guides' rather than texts — and indicts a 'special breed' of guide-book authors as modern Sophists who profit from and reinforce this ignorance, aided by lazy teachers and examiners who set 'stereotype questions' answerable from the same guides. He extends the critique to endemic examination malpractice (smuggled radios, bribed examiners, leaked papers) and argues that the root cause is not any single legacy (he explicitly rejects blaming the Civil Disobedience Movement) but sheer overcrowding driven by the demand that universities admit everyone regardless of aptitude. The essay closes by calling for a 'drastic overhauling' of the system: restricting admission to genuinely capable and motivated students, insisting universities pursue 'sound education... undeterred and unashamed' of any political, economic, or social pressure, and warning that superficial fixes (like reshuffling course structures) address nothing.

- Ookerjee describes Indian university culture as governed by 'traditions' of skipping class, disrupting lectures, and relying on 'guides' instead of texts.
- Guide-book publishers are cast as modern Sophists, profiting from and perpetuating student and even teacher ignorance.
- Examination fraud — bribed examiners, smuggled radios, leaked papers — is described as widespread and enabled by a stereotyped, repetitive examination system.
- He rejects the theory that indiscipline is a legacy of the Civil Disobedience Movement, blaming instead the pressure to admit unmanageably large numbers of students.
- The essay calls for radical reform: shrinking enrollment to the genuinely capable, and freeing universities from external political and economic pressure.

### "Gate of Hell"
*By D. G. Nadkarni*

Complete within the rendered pages (printed pp. 33–36), Nadkarni's review situates the Japanese film 'Gate of Hell' (dir. Teinosuke Kinugasa) alongside Akira Kurosawa's 'Rashomon' and 'Tora-no-o' as period pieces set in a lawless medieval Japan. He praises the film's Eastman colour cinematography — credited to colour consultant Sanzo Wada and cameraman Kohei Sugiyama — as visually extraordinary, especially in its first half, but argues the plot (Moritoh's fatal infatuation with Kesa, wife of the warrior Wataru) is thin and that the film's second half collapses into 'conventionally melodramatic' Western-style drawing-room tragedy, losing the historical texture Kurosawa's films sustain throughout. Actress Machiko Kyo's performance as Kesa is singled out for its expressive range even as the character itself is judged underwritten. The review closes by noting Gate of Hell is regarded in Japan as one of Kinugasa's weaker films, and ends on the film's own moral maxim.

- Nadkarni compares 'Gate of Hell' to Kurosawa's 'Rashomon' and 'Tora-no-o' as fellow period pieces on medieval Japanese unrest.
- The film's Eastman-colour cinematography (Sanzo Wada, colour; Kohei Sugiyama, camera) is praised as a visual achievement, especially in the first half.
- The plot is judged simplistic, and the second half is criticized as sliding into Western-style melodrama detached from its historical setting.
- Actress Machiko Kyo's performance as Kesa is praised despite the character's thinness.
- The review notes 'Gate of Hell' is considered in Japan one of Kinugasa's weaker films, despite its international acclaim.

### The Future of Indian Music
*By Comolata Dutt*

Comolata Dutt argues that Indian music has stayed culturally exclusive by clinging to regional notations instead of adopting a single universal one, and traces its long oral tradition and raga-based harmony. She compares Indian and Western harmonic systems, credits Western composers whose experiments have converged with Indian scales, describes her own attempts to compose Indian music for orchestra, and calls for harmony and counterpoint to be taught formally in Indian music schools alongside a universal notation.

- Dutt argues Indian music has stayed exclusive by clinging to regional notations instead of a universal one, unlike China and Japan.
- She traces Indian music's antiquity (a claimed 2,500-year oral tradition) and argues the raga system already implies a 'constantly changing harmony' through the drone of fifths played on the tamboura.
- She contrasts Western 'block harmony' and counterpoint with Indian melody, and suggests counterpoint suits an Indian point of view better than chordal harmony.
- She credits Western composers, notably Debussy and Scriabine, who intuitively arrived at scales close to South Indian ragas, and describes her own experiments composing Indian ragas for Indian and Western orchestras.
- She closes by urging that harmony and counterpoint be taught in Indian music schools and colleges, and that a universal notation be adopted so Indian music can be 'played and understood all over the world.'

### An Indian in Paris
*By Jean Bouret*

Jean Bouret, a Paris art critic, recalls meeting three young Bombay painters, including Laxman Pai, in his editorial office some years earlier, and traces Pai's development into a recent solo Paris exhibition. He describes a shift from densely detailed, hieratic early work toward a sparer, more abstract idiom that still draws on twelfth-century Sanskrit poetry and Goan imagery, and reads Pai's technical debts to Impressionism and Van Gogh as evidence that an artist is more than a product of social or racial background.

- Bouret recalls three young Bombay painters (Padamsee, Raza and Laxman Pai), trained at Bombay's J. J. School of Arts, visiting his editorial office in Paris some four years earlier.
- He focuses on Laxman Pai's development since, culminating in a recent solo exhibition on the Right Bank of the Seine.
- He traces Pai's shift from a densely detailed, hieratic style toward a sparer, more abstract idiom that still draws on twelfth-century Sanskrit poetry and traditional themes.
- He credits Pai with technical debts to French Impressionism and to Van Gogh, noting Van Gogh had himself borrowed devices originating with Pai's own artistic ancestors.
- He reads Pai's trajectory as bearing out Andre Malraux's theory that an artist is not merely a reflection of social and racial background but an individual sensibility.

### The Long North Wind
*By M. Krishnan*

M. Krishnan introduces Nakkeerar's Nedu-nal-vaadai, a roughly 188-line Sangam-era Tamil poem, arguing it is more genuinely poetic than later, more ornamented Tamil classics because it is free of self-conscious display. He dates the poem, recounts a legend about the poet's integrity, and walks through its structure of vivid winter imagery, the besieged city at dusk, the queen's sleepless longing, and a sudden cut to the king's battle camp, closing on its dedicatory wish to the goddess Durga.

- Krishnan presents Nakkeerar's Nedu-nal-vaadai ('The Long, Good North Wind'), a roughly 188-line Sangam-era (3rd century AD) Tamil poem, as more genuinely poetic than later, more ornamented Tamil literature.
- He dates the poem via its reference to the Pandiya king Nedunjezhian's victory at Talaiyaalanganam, and recounts a legend about Nakkeerar's uncompromising criticism of a verse attributed to Siva.
- He walks through the poem's structure: vivid images of a cold, wet winter, a description of the besieged city at dusk, the queen's sleepless longing at midnight, and a sudden cut to the king's battle camp.
- He highlights the poem's closing wish to the goddess Durga for the king's swift victory, praising its 'flashforward' structure as sophisticated craft.
- He frames the piece as evidence that plain, closely observed Sangam verse can appeal to a modern reader of English poetry more than more virtuosic later Tamil classics.

### Fever in the South
*By T. R. Ramiah*

T. R. Ramiah surveys the Dravida Kazhagam and Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, describing their programme for an independent, casteless 'Dravida Nadu' as resting on a race theory that casts Brahmins as Aryan invaders who imposed Sanskrit, caste and religion on the Dravidian South. He details the movement's anti-Brahmin hostility, its promotion of atheism as a liberating tool, its vaguely communist rhetoric drawing on Marx, Lenin and Mao-Tse Tung, and quotes Nehru's dismissal of it as 'childish nonsense,' before concluding that although electorally negligible, the movement's ideas are seeping into wider non-Brahmin opinion and could still provoke a damaging agitation.

- Ramiah describes the D.K. (led by E. V. Ramaswami Naicker, 'Periyar') and the more vigorous breakaway D.M.K. (led by C. N. Annadurai), both aiming at an independent, casteless 'Dravida Nadu.'
- He argues their programme rests on a race theory holding Brahmins to be Aryan invaders who imposed Sanskrit, caste and religion on the Dravidian South, fuelling explicit anti-Brahmin hostility.
- He describes the movements' promotion of atheism and 'rationalism' as a deliberate tool to loosen religious authority, citing their propaganda (including M. R. Radha's play 'Ramayanam') and denunciations of the chastity of Kunti and Sita as illustrations.
- He notes the movements combine race-hatred, caste-hatred and anti-religious feeling with vaguely communist rhetoric drawing on Marx, Lenin and Mao-Tse Tung, and quotes Nehru's dismissal of the movement as 'childish nonsense,' also citing 'the late Srinivasa Sastry's' remark that 'Tamil society is in a fever, therefore leave it alone.'
- He concludes the movement has little realistic chance of winning secession by election or agitation, but warns its ideas are seeping into non-Brahmin opinion and could still provoke a damaging, doomed uprising.

### The Illusion of the Epoch
*By Yogendra Chopra*

This full book review covers H. B. Acton's 'The Illusion of the Epoch: Marxism-Leninism as a Philosophical Creed' (Cohen and West, London, 18s.). Chopra praises Acton's analytical clarity, comparing his technique to G. E. Moore's practice of refuting grand theories through patient logical dissection rather than counter-proof. He summarises Acton's core diagnosis that Marxism's fatal flaw is ambiguity rather than mere vagueness -- above all in its 'Marxist naturalism,' which tries to fuse positivist and Hegelian claims that Acton shows to be incompatible. The review singles out Acton's treatment of the Materialist Conception of History as the book's strongest section, where Acton (agreeing with Martin Buber) allows the theory coherence only as a modest, technological account of history, and traces Marx's debt, via Feuerbach, to Hegel's Phenomenology and its concepts of Entausserung ('alienation') and Entfremdung ('estrangement').

- Full review of H. B. Acton's 'The Illusion of the Epoch: Marxism-Leninism as a Philosophical Creed' (Cohen and West, London, 18s.).
- Praises Acton's lucidity and technique, likening his method to G. E. Moore's style of refuting theories through logical analysis.
- Summarises Acton's central charge that Marxism's defect is ambiguity, not vagueness, especially in its attempted fusion of positivism and Hegelianism ('Marxist naturalism').
- Highlights Acton's discussion of the Materialist Conception of History, Marx's Paris Manuscripts, and Hegelian terms (Entausserung/'alienation,' Entfremdung/'estrangement') mediated through Feuerbach, as the book's strongest section.
- Concludes the book, despite covering ground familiar to non-Marxists, is 'a major contribution to the study of Marxism' and an 'extremely suggestive exercise in the philosophy of social science.'

### Echoes
*By Sadhan Kumar Ghosh*

Ghosh places Mackenzie, alongside Max Beerbohm, in the fading tradition of the personal, nostalgic essay, recalling Mackenzie's earlier novels 'Guy and Pauline,' 'Sinister Street' and 'Bygone Summers.' The review closes by praising Mackenzie's trustworthy memory, curiosity, and delight in people, books, and nature.

### Everyday Life in Ancient India
*By Arati Mukerji*

This full review covers Padmini Sengupta's 'Everyday Life in Ancient India' (Oxford University Press, Rs. 5/-). Mukerji praises the book as accessible popular history that gives flesh to India's prehistory and antiquity for the lay reader rather than the specialist, citing its treatment of the Indus Valley 'regular city civilisation' at Mohenjodaro and Harappa by roughly 2500 B.C. -- its pictographic script, copper and bronze tools, ornaments, town planning and drainage -- and its likely destruction by Aryan invasion. She follows the book's narrative through the Vedic civilisation into the age of the epics and the Buddhist and Mauryan periods, and endorses Sengupta's closing thesis that a persistent cultural unity, expressed in the idea of 'Bharatvarsa,' allowed India's regions to grow and diversify without losing a common identity.
