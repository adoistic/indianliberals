# QUEST

## Summary

Quest 42 (No. 42, JULY/SEPT. 1964; 'monsoon 1964' on the cover) is the Bombay quarterly's Nehru-memorial number and its non-violence number at once. Jawaharlal Nehru had died on 27 May 1964; the issue opens with an unlisted tipped-in memorial portrait bearing his autograph in facsimile, follows it with two unlisted pages of extracts from his own prose, and drops a further Nehru quotation as a filler at the end of M. M. Bhalla's article. The argumentative centre, however, is a sustained assault on the Gandhian inheritance conducted from inside the journal. Raj Krishna's lead article - a revised G. V. Mavalanker Memorial lecture - argues that non-violence is one value among many, that Gandhi himself licensed violence under stated conditions, and that a 'righteous cold war' with an arms race and a balance-of-power policy can be the least evil course; co-editor Abu Sayeed Ayyub then answers him at length in an open letter in the DISCUSSION section, conceding most of the case while insisting that violence can be justified only negatively and that 'cold war psychosis' must be condemned. Sudhir Chandra attacks the same inheritance historically, arguing that the Indian National Movement's non-violence was borrowed from Britain and the West rather than indigenous, and D. C. Mathur reinterprets the Gita's niskama-karma to restore goal-commitment to moral action. Around this core the issue sets A. S. Nadkarni and R. Srinivasan on the intellectual's and the technologist's place in planned development, T. K. Oommen's thesis that India suffers from over-integration at the intra-group level rather than under-integration, M. M. Bhalla on the function of criticism, Joseph Jordens on the Shakuntala as poetic drama, Ashin Das Gupta's defence of Percival Spear against a reviewer in Quest's own previous number, five poems (Kamala Das taking the 1964 Annual Quest Poetry Award), a long short story by M. C. Gabriel, seven signed book reviews and two letters answering an essay in Quest 41.

The issue's institutional entanglements are dense and nowhere disclosed. The lead article is answered by a serving co-editor; the Assistant Reviews Editor signs one of the reviews; another review takes the measure of A. B. Shah's 'Scientific Method' while, twelve pages later, the journal's own sponsor advertises a Shah-edited volume containing a contribution by the other co-editor; and Raj Krishna's footnote 10 cites the Congress for Cultural Freedom's Forum Service for the Russell-Hook exchange without noting that the CCF is the parent body of Quest's own sponsor, the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom.

## Essays

### Non-Violence, War and Peace
*By Raj Krishna*

Raj Krishna sets out a general formulation of moral choice - several values are always in play, every course of action realises some at the expense of others, and the moral problem is to find the course with the best net balance - and then applies it to non-violence. His conclusion is that a commitment to non-violence can only be conditional, not because of expediency but because we are simultaneously committed to other values. He reads Gandhi's own writings to derive three conditions under which Gandhi himself sanctioned violence: when non-violence would sacrifice other values of great worth (the honour of women, the freedom of a nation, the survival of democracy); when the victims of injustice are not prepared for genuine satyagraha; and when the oppressor is not 'convertible'. On the third he is harshest: conversion works only where oppressor and victim share a moral vocabulary, which is why Gandhian resistance defeated British imperialism and could not have saved Hitler's or Stalin's victims. From there he rehabilitates dharmayuddha, the righteous war, and extends it to the 'righteous cold war', arguing that the Western arms race has kept such peace as there has been since 1945, that non-alignment has been 'palpably immoral' where it condemned both sides indiscriminately, and that India has itself been conducting cold wars with Pakistan and China while denouncing everyone else's. He allows one untested alternative - a massive, simultaneous, genuinely even-handed international satyagraha for disarmament - but holds that pacifists who campaign only where campaigning is free have no right to campaign at all.

- Recasts non-violence as one value among many, so that commitment to it is necessarily conditional on commitments to other values - not on grounds of expediency.
- Derives three conditions legitimising violence from Gandhi's own published positions: sacrifice of other great values, unpreparedness of the satyagrahis, and the non-convertibility of the oppressor.
- Argues conversion through satyagraha succeeds only where the parties share a moral vocabulary, which explains the Indian success against Britain and the futility of satyagraha against Hitler, Stalin, Jinnah or the Japanese.
- Revives dharmayuddha and extends it to a 'righteous cold war', crediting Western balance-of-power and nuclear brinkmanship with preserving post-1945 peace and calling indiscriminate non-alignment immoral.
- Concedes one untested possibility - a simultaneous, equally intensive international satyagraha for disarmament in every armed camp - and argues that unequal campaigns make satyagrahis functionally saboteurs of the defensive side.
- Sums up the anti-Gandhian polemic in the aphorism 'Moral monism is unIndian', locating pluralism in the Indian tradition rather than in modernity.

### Doctrine of Niskama Karma
*By D. C. Mathur*

D. C. Mathur reads the Bhagavad Gita as a philosophy of moral action and finds the received doctrine of niskama-karma unfit for 'changing and revolutionary times'. He accepts the Gita's warning against pride in one's own agency, since every significant moral act is a cooperative enterprise, but argues that subordinating moral action to the metaphysical end of moksa destroys the autonomy of moral judgement: if perfect equipoise secures liberation whichever side one takes, the distinction between right and wrong actions in specific situations is voided, and any status-quo or obscurantist course can be justified. His second objection is that the Gita's deontology derives the content of duty from svadharma and svabhava, a static code that cannot guide the genuinely problematic situations in which moral advice is actually needed - Arjuna's own, for one. Mathur proposes instead that non-attachment be read narrowly: one should be firmly committed to a goal reached by rational appraisal of consequences, while remaining detached about the goal's effects on one's personal fortunes. He supports the reinterpretation by pointing out that Krishna, as tradition depicts him, was a master strategist and a superb politician - goal-oriented rather than self-oriented - so the reading is closer to Krishna's practice than to his preaching. The unresolved tension between goal-directed social action and ultimate concern for the serene Atman, he argues, has marked Indian culture ever since and accounts in part for its relatively static character.

- Grants the Gita's critique of karttrtvabhimana - pride in one's own agency - because moral action is always socially cooperative.
- Objects that referring every moral act to moksa lifts concrete situations 'into the haze of metaphysical doctrines' and renders right/wrong distinctions nugatory.
- Objects further that a deontology drawing duty from svadharma and svabhava is a static code useless in the crisis situations the Gita purports to address.
- Proposes reading non-attachment as detachment from personal consequences only, retaining firm commitment to a rationally appraised goal.
- Argues the reinterpretation matches Krishna's own depicted practice as strategist and politician even if not the text's preaching.
- Diagnoses the unreconciled tension between goal-orientation and concern for the Atman as a source of Indian social stasis - either lip-service and withdrawal, or opportunistic pursuit of selfish goals.

### Function of Criticism
*By M. M. Bhalla*

M. M. Bhalla opens by admitting he cannot define literary criticism, and demonstrates the point by setting Eliot, Donald A. Stauffer, Leavis, John Crowe Ransom and the rasa theorists of Anandavardhana and Abhinava Gupta against one another. Criticism, he argues, 'lacks a philosophy, a consistent science, a common and agreed idea about what it is'. The rival schools - sociological, Marxist, psychological, Empsonian, affective - each abstract one attribute from what Coleridge called 'substantial knowledge', the intuition of things that arises 'when we possess ourselves as one with the whole'. Against them Bhalla asserts that human experience is objective and subjective in one inseparable process, so that literature images the structure of meaning of a whole way of life and literary studies are a concern with culture itself. He then assigns the critic three roles - self-disciplined individual, educator concerned with the health and tone of society, and judge awakening conscience - and develops the second at length with Coleridge on unity and method, Arnold on despotism and government by clubs and newspapers, and Leavis on 'a discontinuity of consciousness'. Modern mass civilisation, advertisement and industrial machinery have broken traditional wisdom and turned education into 'a colossal machine for the manufacture of Philistines'; critical discipline is his proposed training against it, and he ends by claiming for literary criticism the status the classics once held in university education.

- Refuses a definition of literary criticism and shows the disagreement by quoting Eliot, Stauffer, Leavis, Ransom and the rasa theorists against each other.
- Rejects the sociological, Marxist, psychological and Empsonian schools as abstractions of a single attribute from an integral human process.
- Draws on Coleridge's distinction between introspection (subject held aloof from object) and reflection (their coincidence) to ground the critic's self-discipline.
- Diagnoses mass civilisation - advertisement, newspapers, wireless, industrial machinery - as having destroyed traditional wisdom and made education a producer of Philistines.
- Claims criticism has a valuational dimension that sets standards and can be a force in education, and should hold the place the classics once did in universities.

### Shakuntala: Rhythm, Fluidity, Harmony
*By Joseph Jordens*

Joseph Jordens asks why analysing the Shakuntala by plot, character and theme always ends in frustration, and answers that the method is wrong for the object. Analytical plot-study makes the curse the pivot and then cannot interpret it without a ridiculous disproportion between sin and punishment; analytical character-study finds Dushmanta and Shakuntala so perfect that they 'totally lack personality'. Jordens proposes instead that the play is poetic drama in Traversi's sense, to be approached from its dramatic and poetic unity. Each act, he argues, is a self-enclosed visual unit - a tableau vivant comparable to the Ajanta wall paintings, with action carried off stage and reported in the act's introduction - and its essential unity lies in its particular emotional tonality of the single rasa of love: idyllic in Act I, idealising in II, consuming in III, bitter-sweet in IV, harsh in V, remorseful in VI, mature in VII. He shows the tonality permeating the descriptions of both persons and nature, quoting the flower imagery of Act I against the fire and fever imagery of Act III. The acts then repeat one another like the movements of a piece of music, and Act VII symbolically closes the circle begun in Act I. He closes by connecting this to two Hindu attitudes: rhythm as the essence of life, which makes the play pathetic but never tragic and suggests a mandala rather than a directed graph as its emblem; and the 'fluidity' of Indian aesthetics, in which drama flows into dance and music, the conceptual recedes, and art passes over into a nature mysticism that is 'profoundly aesthetical, and unobtrusively religious'.

- Argues the classical analysis of plot, character and theme is the wrong instrument for the Shakuntala and produces only fringe qualities - 'a perfect body without a soul'.
- Reclassifies the play as poetic drama in D. Traversi's sense, borrowed from his account of Shakespeare's last plays, and insists it be approached from its poetic unity.
- Reads each act as a self-enclosed tableau vivant, comparable to the Ajanta murals, unified by one emotional tonality of the rasa of love.
- Demonstrates that the tonality governs the description of nature as much as of persons, contrasting Act I's flower imagery with Act III's fire and fever.
- Dissolves the curse as pivot: rhythm, not the curse, is the cause, and the curse is merely an occasion by which suffering enters love.
- Connects the reading to Hindu conceptions of cosmic rhythm and of the fluidity between art forms and between art and religion.

### Economic Development, Democracy and the Indian Intellectuals
*By A. S. Nadkarni*

A. S. Nadkarni asks what the intellectual can do about the tensions that economic development creates for Indian democracy. He defines the intellectual by the manner in which he forms judgements, and excludes political leaders and businessmen as 'divided personalities' whose one-track pursuit of power or profit destroys the minimum detachment required; in Indian conditions, he argues, 'intellectual' and 'educated' are effectively co-terminous. He then sets out the tensions: prolonged frustration of elementary needs weakens the sense of social responsibility until people 'behave desperately even over relatively minor issues'; development simultaneously creates new opportunities and demands sacrifices, so there is 'effort all round to seize opportunities and evade sacrifices'; and it accentuates conflicts between caste groups, between linguistic regions, and between rural and urban India. He adds a further dimension: the rich industrial interests are by definition urban, so their influence on policy produces an urban-oriented strategy that starves the villages, and since the very rich come from only certain linguistic regions, class conflict takes linguistic form - which makes egalitarian policy doubly stabilising. Ranking intellectuals by their access to the public, he puts journalists first and educators second, but concedes that none can match the politicians' face-to-face reach in a largely illiterate society. The intellectual's role therefore lies not in reaching the people directly but in influencing the behaviour of politicians: expounding the impartial academic position, supplying a basis for inter-party agreement on a minimum programme, and demonstrating democratic conduct in the conduct of their own corporate affairs.

- Defines the intellectual by detachment in forming judgements and excludes party activists and businessmen as divided personalities.
- Treats 'intellectual' and 'educated' as co-terminous in Indian conditions, given mass illiteracy, while denying that identification for the literate West.
- Identifies a lag between desires and awareness of social responsibility as the central threat to democracy under development.
- Locates the stresses in caste competition, linguistic chauvinism and the widening rural-urban gap, and adds the urban bias of rich industrial interests.
- Argues egalitarian economic policy reinforces democracy twice over - directly, and by blunting linguistic chauvinism.
- Concludes the intellectual's leverage is on politicians rather than on the people, and requires that intellectuals run their own corporate bodies democratically.

### Non-Violence in the Indian National Movement
*By Sudhir Chandra*

Sudhir Chandra dismisses three standing explanations for the non-violent character of the Indian National Movement - the failure of 1857, a cowardly or peace-loving national character, and an immemorial Indian tradition of non-violence - and replaces them with the character of the ruling nation. On the second he is blunt: Indians have never been conspicuous for adherence to peace or order, and the 'barbaric frenzy' of 1947 and the present 'pathological inclination for riots, disorders and disturbances' prove it. On the third he argues that Indian thought's tradition of non-violence never stipulated projecting non-violence into politics, that the scriptures licensed force and made it the Kshatriya's duty, that even Ashoka used force at Takshashila, and that Christianity and the West had an equally ancient tradition, first broadcast by Isaiah. The real causes he finds in British rule itself: a government based on the semblance of constitutional machinery, a policy of piecemeal reform that channelled grievance into lawful agitation, and an educational policy that exposed India to Western currents and supplied both a new concept of liberty and the technique of constitutional agitation - a technique whose adoption he illustrates from Dadabhai Naoroji's 1906 Calcutta presidential address and from Burke's hold on the period. Gandhi, he argues, drew his ideas of non-co-operation and satyagraha from Tolstoy and the Sermon on the Mount rather than from Hinduism, and it was Tilak, not Gandhi, who first declared the principle of passive resistance at the Benares Congress of 1905. Non-violence in similar movements across British colonies makes the Indian case no novelty, and he closes by conceding that national prejudice makes an impartial Indian account of the movement unlikely for many decades.

- Rejects 1857, national character and indigenous tradition as explanations, arguing the character of the ruling nation was as decisive as that of the subject people.
- Insists the Indian scriptural tradition of non-violence never extended to politics and that the West had an equally ancient tradition, first stated by Isaiah.
- Credits British constitutional machinery, piecemeal reform and educational policy with channelling Indian grievance into lawful, non-violent agitation.
- Argues Gandhi's satyagraha derived largely from Tolstoy and the Sermon on the Mount, and that Tilak, not Gandhi, introduced passive resistance at Benares in 1905.
- Points out that non-violence was common to independence movements across British colonies, so it is no Indian peculiarity, and corrects Tara Chand on the point.
- Concludes that national prejudice will delay an impartial Indian history of the movement 'many decades yet'.

### Technology, Government and Planning
*By R. Srinivasan*

R. Srinivasan argues that planning is inevitable not because of 'the compulsions of a backward economy' but because of what the application of technology does to society, and that Indian social scientists who ignore technology will be as unprepared as English public men were by the Industrial Revolution. His evidence is a survey of resource exhaustion and ecological backlash: the Fertile Crescent and Libya turned to desert, Lebanon's cedars reduced to a few thousand, English tin gone and coal seams thinning, the Mississippi stripping 750 million tons of soil a year, American per-capita consumption on the Paley Commission's figures, and a jet fighter's copper requirement rising from 125 lb in 1944 to 513 lb in 1954. He adds Darwin on clover and cats, China's Operation Sparrow, and the DDT chain that passed through cattle into milk and into infants, to argue that only the state can map resources or formulate conservation policy - no private enterprise could have produced the Paley Commission's five volumes. Automation compounds the case: the United States has created an Office of Manpower, Automation and Training and provided $435 million to retrain 400,000 workers, which he sets against an Indian government 'sworn to planning' that offers nothing to goldsmiths thrown out of work by its own policy. But he is equally sharp about planning's dangers, invoking C. P. Snow's Science and Government on Churchill and Lindemann, the Atomic Energy Commission's Project Chariot, and India's own crores spent on khadi and prohibition; and he charges that most Indian intellectuals have been 'bribed into silence' by appointment to the Planning Commission's hundred and one agencies.

- Grounds the inevitability of planning in the social consequences of technology rather than in economic backwardness, and warns social scientists that Keynes and Marshall offer no ready-made solutions.
- Marshals resource-depletion and ecological data - soil erosion, exhausted ores, the Paley Commission's consumption figures, DDT in milk, Operation Sparrow - to argue only the state can plan conservation.
- Contrasts the American response to automation (Office of Manpower, Automation and Training; $435 million for retraining 400,000 workers) with Indian inaction over displaced goldsmiths.
- Argues computers and automation make government scientifically fitted to comprehend social problems for the first time.
- Sets against this the dangers of 'closed politics' at the top of government, illustrated by C. P. Snow on Churchill and Lindemann and by Project Chariot, and by India's khadi and prohibition spending.
- Accuses Indian intellectuals of being disarmed by co-option into the Planning Commission's agencies, and warns that if academics betray their responsibilities 'we will be on the high road to serfdom'.

### The Over-Integrated Society
*By T. K. Oommen*

T. K. Oommen argues that India's integration problem has been misdiagnosed. Reviewing the claim that the Indian village is a 'community', he holds that the three supports of that claim - relative isolation, the Hindu Yajmani system and caste and kinship - have all been weakened by industrialisation, urbanisation, monetisation and the free transfer of property, so that the sense of belonging has migrated from the village to the caste or jati. The village is therefore 'a vertical entity made up of several horizontal layers each of which is caste', and the individual is claimed by a set of ever-widening circles - caste, village, region, linguistic area, religion - arranged by their decreasing power to elicit loyalty. He then argues, against the usual alarm, that competitive bargaining by such groups for benefits from the state may actually build a democratic culture and could weaken the groups' own basis once their aims are met, provided the basis of group formation shifts from particularistic criteria to universalistic, merit-oriented ones. The group releasing most of the present tension, he suggests on historical rather than statistical grounds, is the urban middle class, whose aspirations outrun the possibilities of fulfilment - and whose numbers and whose problem will both grow. His own contribution is the closing thesis: since the Indian urbanite has not been uprooted at all and does not hunger to belong, the problem is over-integration at the intra-group level, and over-integrated in-group loyalties produce under-integrated inter-group relations. What India needs, he proposes, is to develop an indifference on the part of the individual to the group - to 'manufacture the balanced man who is attached to but independent of the group'.

- Denies that the Indian village is a community, tracing the collapse of its three supports - isolation, the Yajmani system, caste and kinship - under industrialisation and monetisation.
- Arranges caste, village, region, linguistic area and religion as ever-widening circles competing for the individual's loyalty in decreasing order of power.
- Argues competitive group bargaining for state benefits is not necessarily alarming and may foster a democratic culture, if the basis of group formation shifts from particularistic to universalistic criteria.
- Identifies the urban middle class, with aspirations outrunning fulfilment, as the group most active on the social tension front - and notes the near-absence of data for the claim.
- Warns that economic growth is itself politically destabilising because it raises organised demands, so 'economics cannot solve our riddles'.
- Advances the title thesis: the problem is over-integration at the intra-group level, and the remedy is an individual indifference to the group.

### Non-Violence and Cold War
*By Abu Sayeed Ayyub*

Abu Sayeed Ayyub answers Raj Krishna in an open letter that begins 'Dear Dr. Raj Krishna' and ends 'For the rest I am with you'. His method is to concede the conclusion while rebuilding the opponents' case. He sorts Raj Krishna's opponents into three classes - absolute pacifists, condemners of the cold war between the blocs, and advocates of nuclear disarmament - and objects that more than half the paper is spent on the first, the least significant, and that there is no logical connection between Gandhian non-violence and condemnation of the cold war: 'the principle of universal non-violence is rather a dead horse to flog'. He then disputes Raj Krishna's history of value pluralism, citing the Vedic singer's 'Kasmai devaya havisha vidhema', the four purusharthas, the Gita's jnana-karma-bhakti, the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and Greek tragedy, and asks who the alleged 'moral monists' are, given that Raj Krishna himself exempts Gandhi and declares moral monism un-Indian. His substantive amendment is that violence can be justified only negatively - by the demonstrable balance of evil it prevents, never by the good it may promote - since the positive form licenses wars of liberation, a Western preventive war, and the Communist case for liquidating the capitalists; and he charges that Raj Krishna nowhere discusses the possibility of a disastrous short-lived nuclear imbalance emerging from the arms race. Finally he defends one kind of condemnation of the cold war: not of military preparedness, which he grants, but of 'cold war psychology' or 'psychosis', the mental declaration of war that blackens the enemy and is 'so obvious a prelude to hot war'. Coexistence with thugs is one thing; coexistence between two systems accepted as enduring historical entities is another, and only the latter can avoid a nuclear holocaust. He asks Indians to be as critical of their own vices as of the other side's, notes that 'the right side of the iron curtain' produced Hitler, Mussolini and McCarthy, argues China may best be checked by Soviet-American collaboration, and concludes that intellectual freedom is not the only value - the Communist East may claim some superiority on racial and economic equality - and that the cold war's mental climate is incompatible with the intellectual's own ideals.

- Concedes broad agreement with Raj Krishna while arguing that his opponents' case 'could have been presented a shade better'.
- Denies any logical connection between belief in non-violence and condemnation of the cold war or the arms race, making universal non-violence 'a dead horse to flog'.
- Traces value pluralism to the Vedic singer, the purusharthas, the Gita, the Ramayana, the Mahabharata and Greek tragedy, against the suggestion that it is a modern discovery.
- Insists violence be justified only by the balance of evil it prevents, never by the good it may promote - otherwise wars of liberation and Communist liquidation of capitalists follow.
- Faults Raj Krishna for not discussing the risk of a short-lived but disastrous nuclear imbalance emerging from the arms race itself.
- Distinguishes military preparedness from 'cold war psychosis' and defends condemnation of the latter as the one sensible form the condemnation of the cold war can take.

### Bit by Bit
*By M. C. Gabriel*

M. C. Gabriel's story is a ten-page comedy of literary fraud. Gurumurthy Subramaniam, who spends his evenings in innocent non-committal chatter with Raghavan and Krishnan at the South Indian Cafe, accidentally produces the rhyme 'You've got to walk a mile, to find a smile', takes it for a revelation of his inner depths, and decides he has been called by the Muse. Into the group drifts Vasudevan - 'Vasu' - a voluble literary critic who reviews for the Sentinel and whom nobody admits having brought. - Satirises literary pretension through a protagonist whose entire equipment is one accidental rhyme and a shelf of unread books.
- Makes the change of name - Gurumurthy Subramaniam to 'Gurtys', modelled on Keats - the first and funniest of his frauds.
- Builds its comedy from talk: the cafe group's malice, Raghavan's pointless punctuations, Krishnan's relish in carrying remarks between people.
- Supplies the title from Vasu's verdict on Gurtys - 'a case of conscience dying bit by bit'.
- Turns on the boy Raman, whose directness ('But I must write') is the one thing Gurtys cannot answer, and whose poem he finally mistakes for his own.

P. T. Chacko reviews Frantz Fanon's 'The Damned' with a Foreword by J. P. Sartre, under the display title 'The Third World'. The review is openly hostile. ### Cultural Unity of India
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

A letter from Punya Sloka Ray, writing from The University of Chicago on 17 June 1964, responding to Asaf A. Fyzee's essay on the cultural unity of India in Quest 41. Ray refuses the starting point: cultural unity has been an early Indian aspiration, as Bharatvarsha, Jambudvipa and Hind attest, but no cultural unity anywhere is provable in the scientific or legal sense - Europe's and Britain's are no less unprovable. What demonstrably exists is the legal reality of the Indian republic since 1947, and he asks whether India needs any cultural unity beyond that legal one. The grain of truth in the demand, he concedes, is that legality must be backed by a community of persuasion, which he defines as 'the conscious, personal and mutual allegiance of a dominant number of opinion moulders' - a community that excludes those not actually participating in the culture and includes many outside the legal community. India already has such a unity, he argues, consisting of those who value the government of India for the opportunities for intellectual, spiritual and social freedom it has sought to create, inseparable from love for four dead men: Gandhi, Patel, Azad and Nehru. Much of the older Hindu, Islamic and other tradition is incompatible with this new culture but need not be persecuted. He then addresses the Indian Muslims directly: the mass of them remain convinced their interests are unsafe in a state whose ultimate authority is not Muslim, and for every Hindu working to alienate them there are one to three Muslims who already feel alienated; this excessive fear is the root of the trouble, and it is due to ignorance. Too few Muslims know the best of the Hindu tradition well enough to appeal to it, where Gandhi could quote the Bible or John Stuart Mill against the British. Educated Indian Muslims must therefore write, speak and teach without depending on the government for their livelihoods, and Hindu opinion moulders must lose their hysterical fear of crypto-Pakistanis and grasp that a purely Hindu state would count for less in the world than a secular India. He closes on President Ayub Khan's possible change of heart: if Pakistan becomes a safe neighbour, 'a Pathan from Sandhurst will share with a Brahmin from Cambridge that ultimate honor which was denied to Gandhi and Azad'.

- Denies that any cultural unity - India's, Europe's or Britain's - is amenable to scientific or legal proof, and takes the legal reality of the republic as the demonstrable fact.
- Defines a cultural community as the conscious, personal and mutual allegiance of a dominant number of opinion moulders, not as a potentiality shared by all citizens.
- Locates India's existing cultural unity in allegiance to the freedoms the state has sought to create, and in love for Gandhi, Patel, Azad and Nehru.
