# Quest

## Summary

Quest No. 33 (contents page 'APRIL/JUNE 1962'; cover 'spring 1962') is a general number of the Bombay quarterly 'of Inquiry, Criticism and Ideas' sponsored by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, edited by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta with Nirad C. Chaudhuri and Laxmanshastri Joshi as advisory editors, Nissim Ezekiel as Reviews Editor and Mrs. Laeeq Futehally as Astt. Reviews Editor. Its argumentative centre is a sustained attack on the official rhetoric of 'national integration' from three directions at once: a three-part DISCUSSION in which M. Ruthnaswamy likens the National Integration Committee to Sydney Smith's Dame Partington mopping back the Atlantic and proposes love of the physical land of India in place of the jargon, Satish C. Saberwal argues that emotional integration is a sickness when pursued as a goal rather than as a by-product of social justice, and Sudhir Chandra charges the Government of India with deliberate hypocrisy about caste and proposes legislating the mention of caste out of personal names. Running alongside it is H. R. Pasricha's eleven-page indictment of Indian society as stuporous, complacent and afflicted by a post-colonial 'release phenomenon', with socialism imposed by edict at Avadi and a Djilas-style New Class living off permits and licences, and William Phillips's 'Letter from New York' on an America polarised by the bomb between the John Birch Society and the back-yard fallout shelter on one side and SANE and the Committees of Correspondence on the other.

The literary and cultural half is unusually combative. Agehananda Bharati, a Viennese Indologist at Syracuse, proposes 'cultural criticism' as a formal anthropological and philosophical tool and applies it to what he calls the Hindu Renaissance, insisting that genuine criticism must antagonise its informants; G. V. Karandikar reads realism as a polymorphic concept whose literary value rests on 'resonant correspondence' and 'creative objectivity'; J. A. Ramsaran surveys the American Negro novel from Richard Wright to Paule Marshall; Hephzibah Jesudasan traces Tamil womanhood across twenty centuries and finds the Sangam age freer than what followed; Rajendra Avasthi expounds Thoreau's anti-institutional individualism; P. R. Daswani analyses what makes a body of ideas an ideology; and Jyotirmoy Datta reports twelve months of reviewing Indian films as an anatomy of mass addiction rather than of art. Chatursen Shastri's translated Hindi story 'The Price of Cucumber' turns a bazar bidding war over two cucumbers into a suicide; four poems (two by Nissim Ezekiel, and one each by Ira De, G. C. S. Marar and S. R. Rajan) fill pp.71-74. Sadhan Kumar Ghosh's roving books column, ten signed reviews - David McCutchion's demolition of Srinivasa Iyengar's 'Indian Writing in English' is the most severe - eight unlisted short notices, an unsigned obituary of the former Quest reviewer Pratap Chatterji, and two blocks of contributor biographies close the issue. The contents page lists none of the short notices, the obituary, the contributor blocks, the short story's translator, or the boxed Rabindranath Tagore quotation printed as filler on p.48.

## Essays

### Realism as a Literary Value
*By G. V. Karandikar*

Karandikar argues that realism cannot be defined by an essential quality, because nothing belongs to naive realism, naturalism, surrealism and socialist realism alike that does not also belong to something else. What the kinds share is an operational rather than an essential unity: all are referential, all claim excellence through some correspondence with something external to the work. Reduced to two words, the significance of 'real' in realistic literature and in the creative attitude behind it is 'correspondence' and 'objectivity' - an objectivity that is not disinterestedness, since no surrealist or Marxist realist treats art as a disinterested pursuit.

He then insists on a distinction he thinks is the cause of much confused thinking 'in this part of our country': aesthetic values and literary values are not the same, and literature is not merely a fine art to be judged by laws of configuration or formal beauty. Defining literature as 'a configuration of imaginative action in language', he locates aesthetic value in 'configuration' and specifically literary value in 'imaginative action in language'. He refines his two key terms into 'resonant correspondence' - the quality by which conventional linguistic symbols are informed with the significance of human experience, operating across an internal, a subjective and an objective field - and 'creative objectivity', which achieves objectivity not by denying emotion but by referring it to the total emotional experience of humanity, and which he calls the instrumental value to resonant correspondence's intrinsic value. He closes with two conjectures offered as experimental: that a realistic element has played a liberating role in every period of revolutionary literary change, working with romanticism and classicism alike while decadent forms of both revolt against it; and that many authors who never professed realism passed through a realistic phase from which they drew strength, the development from romantic impulse to classical or purely aesthetic restraint usually being mediated by it.

- Realism is a polymorphic concept with no essential quality common to all its kinds; its unity is operational, resting on correspondence with something external to the work.
- The significance of 'real' in realistic literature reduces to two words: 'correspondence' and 'objectivity'.
- Realist objectivity is a craving to consider human experience in its essential character, not a claim to disinterestedness.
- Aesthetic values and literary values are distinct; literature is a fine art plus something more, judged by an additional set of values.
- Literature is defined as 'a configuration of imaginative action in language', and its characteristic values are refined into 'resonant correspondence' and 'creative objectivity'.
- Historically, a realistic element has played a liberating role in every period of revolutionary literary change; biographically, many non-realist authors passed through a realistic phase.

### Cultural Criticism as a Tool for Social Studies
*By Agehananda Bharati*

Bharati proposes 'cultural criticism' as a new heuristic device for the social sciences, and defines it formally: criticism pronounced by an investigator born in culture A about the patterns of culture B, addressed to native informants of B. His own case is the example - a native of Vienna criticising Indian cultural patterns before Indian hearers and readers. He separates two uses. For the anthropologist or sociologist the point is purely to elicit information, like a psychologist deliberately irritating a patient; for the philosopher it is to change what he criticises, like a psychotherapist who annoys a patient to improve his health. He warns that the tool will not make friends: it will excite, annoy or infuriate informants, that being the price of interesting information, and the alien critic will meet official as well as private censure, since 'mind your own business or get out' is the standing proposition of governments towards him.

The substance of the essay is a distinction between trivial complaint and genuine cultural criticism. Complaints about bad roads, hygiene, red tape or corrupt officials in India - or about juke-boxes, medicated meats and impolite waiters in America - may be well founded but are trivial, because every intellectual knows them already. Real cultural criticism attacks patterns: the oleographs of gods and Gandhi and Nehru; Gandhi's ideas about sexual continence, resented precisely because the charismatic's predilections become morally normative; the Hindu conjunction of academic knowledge with spiritual status, which he calls 'intellectual dishonesty'; the Brahmin professor who dismissed Stcherbatsky as no scholar because he visited Paris nightclubs. He cites Edward Shils on the Indian intellectual tradition seeking fusion with an already-known principle rather than discovery, Shils again on non-attachment inhibiting private libraries, and McCutchion on Indian resistance to the aesthetically experimental. He rejects the objection that criticism slurs national honour - only refutation of contents is a valid objection - attacks 'cradle-committedness', denies that finding cultural differences must mean fighting, offers 'let's enjoy our differences' as a new basis for coexistence, complains that American anthropologists shy away from literate high cultures for want of a tool, and insists that the enterprise must be mutual, with Indians criticising Americans and Europeans in turn.

- Defines cultural criticism as criticism of culture B's patterns pronounced by an investigator native to culture A, addressed to B's own members.
- Distinguishes the anthropologist's use (criticise to elicit information) from the philosopher's (criticise to change the pattern).
- Complaints about bad roads, hygiene and corrupt officials are not cultural criticism but trivia everyone already knows.
- Genuine cultural criticism must antagonise informants; the antagonism is a fact, not a justification, resting on ethnocentricity.
- Illustrates with the 'Hindu Renaissance': Gandhi on sexual continence, the conflation of academic knowledge with spiritual status, and the pandit who denied Stcherbatsky was a scholar.
- Argues the enterprise must be reciprocal to become a medium of cosmopolitan communication.

### Our Stuporous Society
*By H. R. Pasricha*

The longest piece in the issue, in six numbered sections, described by its author as an attempt to describe, assess and record the state of Indian society from the intellectual, political and social points of view, with no claim to originality beyond compiling and applying the considerations. Pasricha begins with a warning that India is emotionally and traditionally tied to Great Britain and constantly compares itself with British conditions, though what works in Britain may not work here. He then formulates a law of criticism, borrowing J. K. Galbraith's Indian remarks on criticism as an index of fraternity: the ability to withstand criticism is directly proportional to a society's cultural development and inversely proportional to its primitiveness, so that excessive sensitivity is an overt expression of an inner knowledge of inferiority. Newly liberated nations are touchy about prestige, and Indians suffer from what he names a 'RELEASE PHENOMENON' - long servitude producing an introvert's conviction of unrecognised merit, which he sets out after release to prove, confining the exposition to spiritual matters since he has little else to show. The claim of antiquity is unassailable but age is not merit, and can mean senility.

He then reads Indian history as largely a dismal tale, a millennium of subjugation leaving disunity, shortsightedness, supine acceptance, obsequiousness and sycophancy and a personality cult on a bigger scale than Russia's; credits the British with geographical unity, English, admiration for parliamentary institutions and above all a disciplined national army, without which partition could not have been survived; and diagnoses a dual personality in the educated Indian, an advanced liberal outside the family and centuries back inside it. On this soil socialism was superimposed by edict at Avadi without countrywide discussion. He argues that economic theories hold only for short periods, that Adam Smith, Malthus, Ricardo and Marx have all been falsified or await verification, that Marxism needs revision to stay scientific and that Revisionism should be normal; that socialist planning presupposes dedicated men who are likelier to come from the class with secure incomes; that no government could provide work for more than a small minority; and that prosperity achieved by socialist means would sound socialism's death knell. Section V describes Djilas's New Class in India, old guard plus bandwagon riders, making fortunes from permits and licences convertible into currency more readily than currency itself. Section VI turns on the educated, whose isolation and disdain have let leadership pass to half-educated agitators, with encouragement from the party in power and its leader; on women, whom he judges disproportionately unenlightened and no restraining influence on their husbands; and on the businessman, degraded by the ancient law-givers and degrading business in return, who stands to gain most from free enterprise and does least for it. He concludes that Indian society is apathetic and smug in the belief that everything will come right by the grace of God.

- Formulates a law: the ability to withstand criticism is directly proportional to a society's cultural development and inversely proportional to its primitiveness.
- Names a 'release phenomenon' by which long servitude produces an inverted inferiority complex and an over-anxious assertion of spiritual superiority.
- Credits British rule with geographical unity, the English language, parliamentary ideals and a disciplined army, and blames a millennium of conquest for disunity and sycophancy.
- Argues socialism was imposed by edict at Avadi without national discussion, and that economic laws are transient, Marx's included, so Revisionism should be treated as normal.
- Applies Djilas's 'New Class' to India: an indigent, unscrupulous political class enriching itself through permits and licences.
- Holds that India's freedom rests dangerously on the goodwill of a single person and that no one party or government should become irreplaceable.
- Indicts the educated classes, Indian women and the business community in turn for abdicating social leadership.

### Racial Themes in American Negro Novels
*By J. A. Ramsaran*

A survey of what Ramsaran calls, for want of a more concise label, the Negro novel - fiction by Negro novelists chiefly about Negro life. Whatever the vocabulary bandied about in discussion (identity, affirmation, integrity, assimilation, engaged, disengaged), the dominant notes are fear, hate and anger, and the only freedom most Negro novelists seem to enjoy is the freedom to write on squalor, crime and delinquency generously mixed with sex. Harlem is no longer Canaan and Chicago no longer the Yankee El Dorado; the discontent of the 'Negro Renaissance' of the twenties has grown into the clamour for desegregation and found literary expression in the protest novel. Ramsaran notes Frank London Brown's insistence that Trumbull Park was a novel of statement rather than protest, since protest implies supplication, but finds that most social novels by Negroes follow one pattern: grievances realistically presented, cumulatively indicting American democracy - while conceding that the right to protest, claimed and exercised in the North, is itself a redeeming feature of that democracy. Richard Wright's Native Son supplies the prototype protest hero in Bigger Thomas, and The Outsider adds disillusion with communism and more violence but less savagery.

Ralph Ellison's Invisible Man is for him the outstanding recent treatment of self-awareness, brilliantly conceived, its invisibility both objective and subjective: the white man's inability to see the Negro as human and the Negro's own unawareness of his identity. On the obsession with identity he is sceptical of Negritude, warning that in a land where colour-blindness is a social disease, belief in the superiority of blackness may spread a doctrine as iniquitous as the one it hopes to supersede, and quotes Baldwin's account of finding himself a kind of bastard of the West as the best statement of the dilemma. He traces the love-hate and rape complex to slavery and the mammy-raised white child, notes Faulkner's Intruder in the Dust and finds nothing in Negro writing as moving as Delta Autumn; surveys the theme of 'passing' from Chesnutt and Weldon Johnson; the economic subsidiary theme of sharecropping, union exclusion and lynching in Killens's Youngblood; the West Coast novels of Chester Himes; and the delinquency and crime novels of Mark Kennedy, Julian Mayfield and Willard Motley. He concludes that these novels mainly serve as social documentaries, and singles out Paule Marshall's Brown Girl, Brownstones and Baldwin's Go Tell It on the Mountain as the two that add poetic insight to documentary and so approach art.

- Whatever the critical vocabulary, the dominant notes of the Negro novel are fear, hate and anger.
- Richard Wright's Native Son is the prototype protest novel; Ellison's Invisible Man the outstanding treatment of self-awareness and identity.
- The right to protest, exercised in the North, is itself counted a redeeming feature of the American democracy the novels indict.
- Warns that Negritude and cultural chauvinism risk a doctrine as iniquitous as the racism they oppose.
- Traces the rape and love-hate themes to slavery and to white children raised by black mammies, with Faulkner's Delta Autumn the most moving treatment of convergence.
- Most such novels function as social documentaries; only Paule Marshall and Baldwin add the poetic insight that raises the form towards art.

### Tamil Womanhood Down the Ages
*By Hephzibah Jesudasan*

Jesudasan studies the Tamil woman across almost twenty centuries of literature under three heads - her appearance, her position in society, and her own contribution to the literature that reflects her - on the ground that few women can be observed against so long a changing background. On appearance she notes that the Sangam conception of beauty was not today's: the poets saw the woman's complexion as 'maamai', the dark purple to light mauve of a tender mango leaf, golden being a sign of pallor, and it is the Aryan entry that displaces maamai with gold, perhaps bringing the habit of turmeric with it.

On position she argues, with regret, that Tamil womanhood has never recovered the prestige of Sangam times, which even Subramania Bharati, Tiru. Vi. Ka. and the Freedom Movement have not fully restored. Sangam society was masculine and near-military and paid bride-price, but it was monogamous among the masses, ignored polygamy, never honoured the prostitute, understood wifely jealousy, kept the woman free of her in-laws and of child marriage, and through Tiruvalluvar held that the good woman is her own guardian - in pointed contrast to Manu's chastity enforced by father, husband or son. Wifehood rather than motherhood was the corner-stone, and the worship of noble wifehood became almost a religion. Degradation follows in stages: the Post-Sangam idealisation of the prostitute Madavi on a level with the wife Kannahi; the Bhakti movement's flood of Sanskrit literature and Aryan custom, bringing child marriage, seclusion, subjection and polygamy, so that woman's power revives only as mother and the joint family is founded; Jain and Buddhist influence; and the Siddhars, who fearlessly whipped hypocrisy and casteism but dragged woman into the pillory as a snare, imposing a prudery worse than Victorian on the upper-class woman while leaving her poorer sister, whose one enemy was poverty, untouched. The British period brought a mass awakening through English education and the prohibition of child marriage and sati, though custom dies hard. On literature, her output rises and falls with her social hold: the Sangam anthologies include daughters of hunters and potters, and Adiyaman gave the poetess Avvai the Nelli fruit of immortality rather than eat it himself; Karaikkal Ammaiyar, a tradesman's wife, is the morning star of Tamil and hence Indian bhakti poetry; Andal is the one major Bhakti poetess; then silence, but for the twelfth-century second Avvai, the People's Poetess and Singer-for-Gruel of the beggar class, the most quoted Tamil poet of all, after whom 'the rest is silence' until the recent return of the Tamil woman to fiction, the short story and literary discussion.

- Sangam beauty was 'maamai', the dark mango-leaf complexion; golden signified pallor until the Aryan entry reversed the ideal.
- Sangam society gave the Tamil woman monogamy, freedom from in-laws and child marriage, and Tiruvalluvar's doctrine that the good woman is her own guardian, against Manu's enforced chastity.
- Wifehood, not motherhood, was the corner-stone of ancient Tamil society, and the prostitute was never honoured in it.
- Decline proceeds through the Post-Sangam idealisation of Madavi, the Bhakti movement's importation of Aryan custom and polygamy, and the Siddhars' shaming of sex.
- English education and British prohibition of child marriage and sati produced a mass awakening among the upper classes who needed it most.
- Her literary output tracks her social standing: labourers' daughters in the Sangam anthologies, Karaikkal Ammaiyar and Andal in the Bhakti age, the beggar-class second Avvai in the twelfth century, then near-silence.

### "Ideology" in Social Change
*By P. R. Daswani*

A short conceptual analysis of what an ideology is, what social function it corresponds to, and what its conditions of success are, worked through Communist ideology as the example most familiar to readers but explicitly without comparing its merits to any other. Ideology is both factor and product of social change: a factor because action springs from ideas, a product because no idea emerges without an already changed situation. But scattered ideas are only opinions; an ideology begins to be born when they are raised to a social theory covering the general relationships or values of a society and ready to answer every social problem, and it is completed only when an organisation exists that binds people to realise it. Without that organisation a complete social theory is an individual philosophy (Marx's Communism before the Communist League and the Manifesto) or at best a doctrine, like laissez faire; the two conditions may arrive in either order, but both are needed.

Its social function corresponds to, without being identical with, the interplay of conservative and radical forces - both of which have a role, since without the radical element society degenerates into senile weakness and without the conservative it leaps into a stormy whirlpool. No ideology is wholly one or the other: the same ideology may be conservative on one question and radical on another, so classifying ideologies by their dominant element is a facile over-simplification, and calling one reactionary or utopian is true only relative to a particular question and to the viewpoint of the person using the word. Success depends on external factors - rival ideologies, the values, habits and customs of the people, and the 'social realities' needing prior attention - and on internal ones: the ideology's vitality, the inner strength of the organisation, its ability to present a united front, and possession of the means, whether the parliamentary adaptation the Communists attempted in Kerala, a propaganda and election machine in present-day India, or the equipped army that let them exploit China in the nineteen-forties. Finally, success means only that the ideology has come to be accepted as the basis for action and planning, not that its aims are realised: Russia has kept Communism as the basis for social action since 1919 while the withering away of the state has receded and the abolition of private property has been much modified.

- An ideology requires two things: a more or less complete social theory, and an organisation bound to realise it; either may come first but both are necessary.
- Without an organisation a social theory is only an individual philosophy or, if widely held, a mere doctrine such as laissez faire.
- Ideologies correspond to, but are not identical with, the conservative and radical forces in society; both forces are necessary.
- No ideology is wholly conservative or wholly radical, so 'reactionary' and 'utopian' are true only relative to a single question and the speaker's viewpoint.
- Success depends on external conditions (rival ideologies, popular values, pressing social realities) and internal ones (vitality, organisational unity, available means).
- Acceptance as a basis for action is not realisation: Russia's withering-away of the state has receded while Communism remains its basis.

### Social & Political Ideas of Thoreau
*By Rajendra Avasthi*

An exposition of Thoreau built almost entirely from quotation, with a critical coda. Avasthi begins from the claim that Thoreau holds something everlasting - a universal constructive genius - underlies all creation, so that all life is one and man is primarily related to Nature rather than tied to institutions, a universal and not merely social organism. Knowledge lies in the unity of the soul with Nature and is attained by living experience, not doctrinisation; truth is known by the feeling within, corresponds to love, and requires no authoritative intermediary. Evil arises when man is not one with Nature, so responsibility lies wholly with him and blaming circumstances is neither useful nor justifiable, though evil can only be minimised, not eliminated: disease is a permanent condition of life. Reform must therefore begin and mostly end with the individual - 'Follow your own genius' - and economic and social reform can do no good until he reforms himself, after which they become superfluous.

From this follow the practical doctrines Avasthi sets out at length: simplify, so that the necessities leave leisure for higher callings; live in the present, which dissolves theft and hoarding; make riches means and not end; treat all work as an absorbing pursuit and never earn money merely; substitute cooperation for competition; perform daily physical labour as moral and spiritual discipline, but stop short of exhaustion. The machine is not to be dispensed with but limited, since as used it multiplies demands and imposes a division of labour that impoverishes the non-worker as much as the worker - 'it is not the tailor alone who is the ninth part of a man; it is as much the preacher, and the merchant and the farmer'. On institutions Thoreau is against business, government, religion and social forms, and would abnegate marriage and family for the highest type of life. The State rests on violence and confronts only the body; the best government governs not at all; a healthy citizen should be no more aware of his government than a healthy man of his bodily functions, and a really healthy society is one in which no one thinks of participating. Where government is tyrannical there is a right to revolt without waiting for a majority, by non-violent passive resistance and civil disobedience - 'under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison'. Democracy is majority imposition, rule of the physically strongest, demanding average thought; voting for the right is doing nothing for it. ### Letter from New York
*By William Phillips*

A dispatch on the American political and literary mood, carrying an editorial footnote that it 'has been inadvertently held over from our last issue'. Phillips reports that the cold war has begun to shake up the country: official thinking has become tougher though its consequences often remain secret, a new right is flexing its muscles, and anxiety has swept those who feel powerless on both left and right - but since the American system is designed to conceal rather than express conflict, the new attitudes crop up in odd, almost eccentric forms. The reasons are not hard to find: the grim euphony of 'red or dead', the sinking-in of the fact that American power is no longer absolute, and the bomb hovering over everything, which is destroying politics by reducing it to diplomatic manoeuvre. For the first time the idea of progress - which he glosses as the notion that everything will become better by remaining the same - can no longer be counted on, and not knowing what lies ahead has become the accepted view, the lack of perspective converted from a national prejudice into an historic truth.

Crisis has polarised. On the right, Goldwater, the National Review and the John Birch Society thrive on fear of communism, reviving the McCarthy game of smear and double talk with an appeal to red-blooded Americanism and masculinity, while the saner spokesmen trade on the notion that the country has been too soft because it is infected with communism, socialism and liberalism lumped together; the effect is to make anti-communism synonymous with strength and so make it easier for communists to line up with reform and progress. On the left, SANE is the most balanced multilateralist body, with Norman Cousins and Norman Thomas prominent, while H. Stuart Hughes is the least eccentric unilateralist, though unilateral disarmament is too masochistic to make headway. He notes one difference from abroad: intellectuals elsewhere take purer, more relaxed positions because there are no immediate consequences, whereas everyone in America shares the feeling that what the country does is decisive, so that even socialists and pacifists find it hard to disengage and 'responsible' thinking has reached epidemic proportions in the new breed of military intellectuals. Against it on the left stands the Committees of Correspondence, circulating an undoctrinaire bulletin, with Riesman and Fromm most active. In the arts nothing so dramatic: Malamud's A New Life shows how domesticated the best modern fiction has become, Salinger builds far-out associations round conventional elements, Robert Lowell's translations give foreign poetry a native personality, and essay collections by Mary McCarthy and Hannah Arendt are notable for an intelligence and unconventionality he finds atrophying. He closes on an administration that seems intelligent and civilised by contrast but is rattled by the right, held back by stored-up smugness, and lacks a theory of history.

### One Year as a Film Critic
*By Jyotirmoy Datta*

Datta's report after twelve months as a film critic, three evenings a week. He opens by refusing the expected inside view of the racket: he has had no chance to kiss an actress or see her black-money account book, though he can confirm that stars are paid mostly in black money, which is common knowledge. His adventures were limited to sneaking in and out of dark halls where the air is foul with a thousand noses and the stink of urinals, from which he draws his central thesis: people do not see films for entertainment but to get the kick the Romans got from a gladiatorial show. Art can accommodate what shocks and horrifies - Macbeth's head on a platter, Tamburlaine's chariot drawn by captive kings - when art comes close to life; but the epic poets and nineteenth-century novelists produced works that are no substitute for the casino or the brothel, and a good novel leaves one more receptive to experience, whereas an evening at the cinema leaves almost no feelings at all. He documents the aftermath clinically: headache, moist cold palms, a pulse above 100, a mild hangover of the nerves, and concludes that most film-goers are addicts and the industry an unwitting gang of dope peddlers.

He then sizes the fact. Over 400 features a year in 4,000 show-houses, an estimated five hundred million tickets, against 1.6 million radio sets in 1959 and a registered daily-newspaper circulation of 3.8 million in 1958 - one copy for every hundred Indians. The State, like Vijaynagar taking a cut of brothel revenue, takes a third of a three-rupee ticket in West Bengal, duties imports, cesses exposed film, charges rent for compulsory documentaries and appoints censors as sanitary experts. The cinema therefore commands the largest audience, but he doubts it communicates anything, and calls the belief that it does - voiced by Nehru at the Delhi film festival and by the Vice-President - a widely held and dangerous myth, since the censors act on it: no medium can both lead public opinion and have mass appeal, as Northcliffe learned. He then argues against the follower of Vinoba Bhave who blames industrialisation, pointing out that feudal societies are excellent film markets and that Krittibash's Bengali Ramayana carries the same 'vulgar' obsessions, rancid jokes and treacly sentiment, as do Shakespeare, Dickens and Dostoyevsky; the craving for strong nervous stimulants is not industrial in origin. He demolishes the sex-appeal myth with evidence from a film magazine's question columns, where of fourteen worshipful questions eight were asked by women about actresses or men about actors, and notes that fans adore rather than desire. Finally he classifies films into three genres - art films, where the director like Satyajit Ray uses story, camera, actors and score as pliable material and uses no stars at all; story films faithful to an original novel, as with Saratchandra; and formula films built round stars, with hacks patching up plots and five Calcutta releases in three months credited 'Story-Story Dept.' He ends on the martyrs of filmdom, the handsome newcomers too fine or velvety for this world of shocks, and on a hope: the Elizabethan conventions were bad too, so a crop of geniuses might yet make great popular art out of conventions he calls shocking, shocking, shocking.

- People do not see films for entertainment but for the kick of a gladiatorial show; the post-film state is a mild nervous hangover with a pulse above 100.
- Most film-goers are addicts and the industry has become, unawares, a gang of dope peddlers, taxed by the State as Vijaynagar taxed brothels.
- Cites over 400 features a year, 4,000 show-houses and an estimated 500 million tickets against 1.6 million radio sets and 3.8 million newspapers.

- Denies that industrialisation caused mass vulgarity, citing Krittibash's Bengali Ramayana, Shakespeare, Dickens and Dostoyevsky.

- Classifies films as art films (Satyajit Ray, no stars), story films faithful to a novel, and formula films built round black-money stars.

A story in seven numbered parts, translated from Hindi by N. N. Banerji. ### Essay 17
*By Satish C. Saberwal*

The second DISCUSSION piece, printed under its own head 'National Integration II'; the contents page leaves its title cell blank. Saberwal grants that the spate of conferences has been hailed as belated recognition of a real malady, but argues that something has gone seriously wrong in the diagnosis and in several of the prescriptions. The basic problem, in his judgement, is that the overwhelming majority place their loyalties with ascriptive groups - caste, religion, language - whose membership is determined by birth and carries high emotional tone, while the concept of social justice has struck only shallow roots, so that when two groups' interests clash individuals take their cues from group interest and not from justice; and this holds for most graduates of Indian universities as much as for illiterate villagers.

He isolates two factors in the current thinking. The first assumes the people need an alternative focus for emotional investment and offers the nation. The second assumes that removing overt symbols of these groups - from the names of political parties, universities, possibly hotels and hostels - will make the groups themselves lose significance; of which he writes that the whole operation looks like one vast effort at sweeping the dirt and putting it under the charpai, and the dirt remains. He then presses three objections. It ignores the psychological bases: eight-year-olds in India already hold fairly elaborate and almost invariably adverse images of a bhangi, a bania, a brahmin, a mussalman and a madrassi, and investigation of highly educated adults would probably show little difference. It ignores the sociological bases: a caste group is an endogamous kinship group, marriage outside one's caste is among the rarest of things whatever the Constitution is said to have abolished, and a man running a business, standing for election or making appointments yields all too frequently to pressure from his kinsmen. The specific danger of an emotionally charged national image is a climate in which heresy is taboo: arguing for a plebiscite in Kashmir would run into that sanctity, Sheikh Abdullah finds no friend in India, and any would-be friends remember Mridula Sarabhai's fate. He tells the story of a young man who asked a saintly public figure whether it was morally right to let visitors go to Kashmir during B & K's 1955 visit and was told 'I think we can trust Nehru's judgment on foreign affairs' - noting there is not even a faint echo in India of the British protest over Suez or the American minority opposition over Cuba. He asks how many Indians have suffered with Harijans and Muslims over wells, restaurants and residential areas, or married outside their caste and encouraged their kinsfolk to, and closes with William Empson's lines on the poison that fills the whole blood stream and the waste that remains and kills.

- Diagnoses the malady as ascriptive group loyalty plus shallow roots for the concept of social justice, holding equally for graduates and illiterate villagers.
- Dismisses the removal of overt group symbols as sweeping the dirt under the charpai - the dirt remains.
- Objects that the psychological bases are ignored: eight-year-olds already hold adverse out-group stereotypes little different from educated adults'.
- Objects that the sociological bases are ignored: caste is an endogamous kin group and marriage outside it remains among the rarest of things.
- Warns that an emotionally charged national image makes heresy taboo, citing Kashmir, Sheikh Abdullah and Mridula Sarabhai.
- Argues emotional integration is a sickness as a goal and healthy only as the by-product of decisions dictated by social justice.
- Contrasts Indian slogan-mongering with American students' adaptation of Gandhi's techniques to desegregate public facilities at the cost of jail.

### Essay 18
*By Sudhir Chandra*

The third DISCUSSION piece, printed under its own head 'National Integration III'; the contents page leaves its title cell blank. Chandra names caste the most prominent and dangerous obstacle to integration, and charges the Government of India with deliberate blindness amounting to hypocrisy. His illustrative episode: when Taya Zinkin, then the Manchester Guardian's India correspondent, gave an All India Radio talk in which the word 'caste' was changed to 'group', because as far as the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting is concerned there is no caste in India except untouchability, which has been abolished. Stretched to its logical implications, this amounts to saying that no all-out assault by the Indian Government on the system is in question - 'We fight against an enemy only when we recognise his existence, not against one whom we think, rightly or wrongly, to be dead.' The disappointed fact, he writes, is that the secular leaders of secular India have done much to eradicate untouchability and nothing whatsoever to destroy caste, untouchability being only a constituent of the larger problem.

Against those who expect caste to die naturally with secularism, technology, rationalism and democracy he answers that the same argument has been made for fifty years and more without appreciable ground gained, and that caste has shown remarkable adaptability in an uncongenial atmosphere; the national crisis leaves no time to let it go its own obstinate way. His central proposal is a law prohibiting the use and therefore the mention of caste or sub-caste along with a person's name, on the ground that caste has no autonomous continuity but survives on a fresh lease of life from each new generation, and that loyalty to caste is not innate but carefully cultivated in every child by family and community - so the plastic minds of the young must be protected during their impressionable years. Little can be hoped from the present generation, who even when they speak against caste 'suffer from a strange schizophrenia', intellectually opposed and emotionally leaning towards it, as Edward Shils pointed out of Indian intellectuals. To the objection that legislation must come from below and needs a prior psychological transformation, he replies that this holds only to some extent for politically stable and experienced democracies; in newly independent countries the legislature must also pass laws that may not please the majority, and the choice is between waiting until legislation is unnecessary and passing it at once. To those who treat the problem as economic he grants that untouchability's removal is really economic but denies it for caste as a whole: the three upper castes and their sub-divisions cannot be ranked as backward or well off, since all contain every economic cross-section, so the superstructure rests on social and not economic discrimination, and the task is not to improve a caste's material position but to make more people see such distinctions as a denial of elementary justice and of the inalienable rights of man. He adds simpler measures for those who really wish caste to go: give children inter-communal and inter-provincial names, following Ustad Alauddin Khan, whose daughters are called Sarojini and Anusuya, or a North Indian acquaintance who named his son Sundaram, or at least delete caste surnames; and encourage inter-caste and inter-provincial marriage, which requires liberal parents to abdicate the traditional Indian right of arranging their children's marriages, since such marriages must be of love and not of parental negotiation - and which requires genuine co-education, not the existing institutions where boys and girls live in two water-tight compartments.

- Charges the Government of India with hypocrisy over caste, illustrated by AIR changing 'caste' to 'group' in Taya Zinkin's broadcast.
- Holds that secular India's leaders have done much against untouchability and nothing to destroy caste itself.
- Rejects the expectation that caste will die naturally: the argument is fifty years old and caste has proved remarkably adaptable.
- Proposes legislation prohibiting the mention of caste or sub-caste alongside a person's name, since caste loyalty is cultivated, not innate.
- Answers the 'legislation must come from below' objection: newly independent legislatures must also pass laws displeasing to the majority.
- Denies that caste as a whole is an economic problem, since the three upper castes contain all economic cross-sections alike.
- Recommends inter-communal names, deletion of caste surnames, and inter-caste love marriages supported by genuinely un-segregated co-education.

### Land Reforms and Group Dynamics
*By Satish C. Saberwal*

Printed under the headline 'The Future of our Village'. Saberwal says the book evokes ambivalent reactions and proceeds in order: summarise the argument, note the welcome features, criticise the rest. Baljit Singh and his associates gathered primary data in six villages of Uttar Pradesh, three each in the western and eastern districts. The low productivity of Indian agriculture is documented again, with the contention that the poorest villagers have lost further ground in the last decade; since only a few castes own most of the land, they employ land-based power to dominate the others; the landless strongly favour ceilings and redistribution and the outnumbered owners oppose them; conflict between the groups is frequent and sometimes violent; and factions within the land-owning castes are described with insight. On land reform Singh reports that the Zamindari Abolition and Land Reforms Act of July 1952 raised proprietary households in the sample villages from 9% to 25% but removed only the intermediaries between state and cultivator, some cultivators continuing to own large areas usually tilled by hired labour and share-cropping - Saberwal notes that no data for the sample villages could be found, but that for U.P. as a whole about 4% of cultivators held 25% of the land in lots of 15 acres or more. Ceilings followed by Japanese-style redistribution appear to Singh more or less inescapable, and he argues that the recent U.P. land consolidation strengthened the large cultivator and was therefore untimely. Informants' felt needs run in the order redistribution, ceilings, consolidation, cooperative farming - the last on an 8% positive response, which Singh discounts in favour of the fact that some cultivators already feel the need for cooperation.

Saberwal credits the book as an indicator of the growing empirical temper of the time, praises the legwork behind the data, counts it a contribution to the slowly growing corpus of field reports on Indian villages - asking whether more of the primary data might be published cheaply, cyclostyled, for professionals - and welcomes the small step, taken via the Tepoztlan reports from Mexico, towards recognising that the Indian peasantry is not unique. Then the objections. Generalising for the whole of India from six Uttar Pradesh villages is only partially mitigated by the Surveys used, and field data and extensive generalisation should at least have been presented in separate sections. The tendency to extrapolate startlingly is worse: Saberwal quotes in full the passage in which, from a clear majority opposed to joint ownership of costly farm equipment, Singh derives the 'proof' that each type of service cooperative will grow in popularity as people gain experience of it. Most deplorable of all is the simultaneous attempt to report primary field data, generalise freely upon it, and sell readers proposals that have no demonstrated relationship whatsoever to the data - advocacy which belongs properly to political pamphleteering, an honourable occupation in its own right, but which should not be mixed with a scientific monograph.

### Tales from the Calendar
*By Ferrokh K. R. Mehta*

Printed under the headline 'Brecht as a Story Teller'. Mehta notes that since Brecht died in 1956 he has become a world-wide vogue and a legend in Europe, chiefly through his plays: in West Germany the frequency of their production has surpassed Shaw's and is less frequent only than Shakespeare's. This slim volume contains most of the short stories Brecht wrote and considered worth preserving, and a few of his poems. In giving it its title Brecht recalls the 'peasant calendars' of an earlier age, when collections of satirical tales, fables, aphorisms and little descriptions of historical or everyday episodes were a traditional form of popular instruction and entertainment in rural areas - a deliberate revival of the name, and a choice of contents, in accord with his clearly expressed view that the function of literature, whether in drama, verse or prose, is to stimulate through entertainment the wits, the social consciousness and the moral sensibilities of ordinary men and women.

Both the prose and the verse display a disarming simplicity in perfect consonance with that approach, and Brecht's skill as a prose writer rests soundly on his capacity to communicate a story briefly, conveying the effect of rapid action in a few vivid strokes. The purpose of Galileo was, Brecht said, 'to teach us to survive', and many of the tales and poems concern people who are mute symbols of humanity's will to endure; the 'Brechtian moment' of the plays - man selling his fellow-men - is also the theme of some of the work here, though while he found men and women acting inhumanly towards each other he accepted that they too were victims of their social environment. Plagued by the defeat of goodness in life, Brecht evolved into a cynic but, like most cynics, retained a hard core of pity; 'The Augsburg Chalk Circle' and 'The Heretic's Coat' show him at his most humane, and in all his stories he shies away from the dramatic and the spectacular, containing himself and evoking a response through narrative lucidity. The most entertaining and engrossing, for Mehta, are those dealing with a major event or the men of history - 'The Experiment', 'Caesar and his Legionary' and 'Socrates Wounded' - amazingly fresh, as though a hidden facet had suddenly been illuminated by the clear light of Brecht's vision. He advises those who have never encountered Brecht before, who in India would be legion, to make his acquaintance first in the plays, since enchanting as most of these stories and poems are they lack the scope and power of the drama - but once acquainted, the reader will want to read everything. On the much-discussed political creed he is brisk: read his works, his political views just couldn't matter less; if he had any creed to expound as a writer it was his ferocious pacifism as an anti-militarist, and above all he comes through as a compassionate cynic with an eye for the humour in most situations and a dogged, determined love for life, such as it is.

But he objects to the emphasis that makes ethical concepts merely tools for the achievement of social results, since 'Sociality does not exhaust morality': even where ethics is part of the process by which the desires and actions of a community's members are harmonised, the ideal on which that ethical organisation rests rises above its social significance. What Toulmin fails to make with sufficient clarity is the distinction between a moral principle and an established practice of a society, and the result is the rather limited place he assigns to reason - two kinds of moral reasoning only, one justifying a practice by reference to the ideal of social harmony, the other justifying a particular action by showing it an instance of an established practice or principle.
