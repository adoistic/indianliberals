# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 45 (April/June 1965; 'spring 1965' on the cover) is the Bombay quarterly of the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, edited by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta with Nirad C. Chaudhuri and Laxmanshastri Joshi as advisory editors, and it reads as a number organised around two shocks: the death of T. S. Eliot in January 1965 and the deepening American war in Vietnam. Seven articles, three current-affairs pieces, a two-letter discussion, five poems, a short story, eight book reviews and two letters fill 124 printed pages. The argumentative centre is a sustained liberal anti-monism: Nirad C. Chaudhuri argues that Hindu India is disabled by paired opposite traits and cannot tolerate public dissent; Hoang Van Chi argues that American policy in Vietnam has mistaken industrial development for the cause of democracy and has therefore bought 'strong men' instead of building a democratic movement; Ashin Das Gupta argues that Soekarno's confrontation with Malaysia is a fake crisis and that the real question is whether a plural society can hold; Punya Sloka Ray argues for the deliberate 'mutual isolation' of India and Pakistan; and the long Raj Krishna-Ayyub exchange tests whether moral values can be reduced to a common numeraire at all, with the economist saying yes and the co-editor saying no. Two literary sequences frame this: Nissim Ezekiel's personal reckoning with Eliot's decline and Leslie Paul's previously unpublished 1958 BBC interview in which Eliot defends a class society and predicts that a purely materialistic civilisation ends in boredom.

## Essays

### Dichotomy in Hindu Life
*By Nirad C. Chaudhuri*

Nirad C. Chaudhuri argues that Hindu life is structured by permanently paired opposite traits, and that this dichotomy — not any doctrine or interest — is what shapes India's external relations. He announces nine such pairs (solidarity/disunity, megalomania/self-abasement, xenophobia/xenolatry, authoritarianism/anarchic individualism, violence/non-violence, militarism/pacifism, possessiveness/indifference, courage/cowardice, stupidity/cleverness) and treats the first three at length. Hindu solidarity, he says, is separative rather than unitive: it unites Hindus only against non-Hindus, so it contributes nothing to internal nationalism while making public dissent from a national position unthinkable. Against this he sets British counter-examples — the Pro-Boers, Burke and Sheridan, John Bright — and observes that many high-placed Hindus privately disapproved of the occupation of Goa but none protested publicly. He then argues that the political unity of India is a British legacy to which the new regime has added nothing, and that provincial sentiment is the real emerging national passion.

The second and third pairs are handled as a psychological history. Aryan self-confidence, he says, curdled into a defensive megalomania under Muslim and then British rule, sustained by an illusion of greatness; the nineteenth-century interlude of rational self-criticism is over and self-abasement has reverted to a degraded form, which he illustrates with the shopkeeper's refrain 'Desi aisai hota'. Xenophobia and xenolatry are presented as the outward projection of the same pair: he quotes Alberuni and Abbé Dubois on Hindu contempt for foreigners, reports the abuse his own appreciative book on England drew in 1959, and then attacks the post-independence 'degrading xenolatry' of a ruling class that will not entrust a project to an Indian if a foreigner can be had. The piece closes by calling itself 'only a sampling', offered as a working hypothesis. The article's own printed head is longer than either the cover or the contents-page title: 'Dichotomy in Hindu Life and Its Impact on India's External Relations'.

- Nine paired opposite traits are announced as the mechanism of Hindu life; only the first three (solidarity/disunity, megalomania/self-abasement, xenophobia/xenolatry) are treated in this article.
- Hindu solidarity is argued to be separative, not unitive: it mobilises only against non-Hindus and therefore contributes nothing to internal nationalism.
- The suppression of public dissent is the central political claim — a Hindu's behaviour is said to be controlled 'not only at the stage of action, but even at that of thought and feeling'.
- India's political unity is credited to British imperialism, with provincial sentiment named the true emerging national passion.
- Post-independence 'xenolatry' is attacked as morally degrading and economically ruinous, with the National Physical Laboratory and imported foreign economists as the worked examples.
- The essay carries an unsigned italic head-note defending the use of the word 'Hindu' rather than 'Indian', initialled '-N.C.C.'

### T. S. Eliot: A Personal Review
*By Nissim Ezekiel*

Writing months after Eliot's death, Nissim Ezekiel predicts a partial restoration of an influence that had declined badly over the previous decade, and then draws a firm line between the earlier and the later Eliot: the early criticism is seminal, the early poetry revolutionary, the early plays pioneering, while the later writing could be imagined as that of an intelligent disciple. He images the poetry from 1917 to 1943 as a Himalayan range with The Waste Land as its Everest and the Quartets as a high plateau, and argues that the temptation to treat that mountainscape as the whole sovereign territory of poetry was what made Eliot, in Blackmur's phrase, a literary dictator. When the dictatorship of the poetry stopped working after the Second World War, he says, the critical assessments went on being effective.

Ezekiel is candid about what he cannot take. He finds much in the 1920 poems and The Waste Land tiresome, is unpersuaded by Leavis's 'rich disorganisation', thinks the St Augustine and Buddha allusions 'pathetically inadequate', and yet calls The Waste Land a great poem because of the unity of personality expressed in it. He rejects Eliot's self-description as classicist, royalist and Anglo-Catholic as an adequate summary, finds the social dogmas constricting where the literary tradition is liberating, but insists the programme for the poet — to hold the mind of one's culture as more important than one's own — exalts. The second half is a reading of the Four Quartets as chamber music, tracing the mystic moment through Burnt Norton, East Coker and Little Gidding. He closes on the drama: Murder in the Cathedral and The Family Reunion are both good plays and good poetry, while the three later plays are flabby and, in The Elder Statesman, a parody of themselves.

- A dated periodisation: everything up to 1943 is the real Eliot; the post-war criticism, poetry and plays are a decline.
- Eliot's critical authority is argued to have outlived the authority of his verse — the 'literary dictator' whose readings of Milton, Dante and the Metaphysicals stuck.
- Ezekiel records personal resistance to The Waste Land's technique while still calling it great, locating its unity in personality rather than in method.
- Eliot's social and religious dogmas are separated from his conception of literary tradition, the first called constricting and the second liberating.
- The Four Quartets are read as chamber music organised around the timeless mystic moment, with the poems' abstract and concrete registers deliberately alternated.
- The three later plays are judged inferior as drama and in language, with The Cocktail Party's only exciting poetry said to be a quotation, not Eliot's.

### A Conversation with T. S. Eliot
*By Leslie Paul*

This is the transcript of a discussion Leslie Paul arranged with T. S. Eliot for the BBC European Service in 1958 and which, for reasons now obscure to both men, was never published; the first part of the conversation was lost because the technicians forgot to switch on the tape. Paul opens on politics, asking whether Eliot still holds that the choice is between a new Christian culture and a pagan one. Eliot withdraws the word 'pagan': Nazism and Communism were attempts to bypass Christian culture rather than genuine paganisms, and neither can survive, because their religious fervour depends on always presenting the image of an enemy and an earthly god. From there the talk turns to what Paul calls a 'cultureless culture' on the model of Orwell's 'foodless food' — the mass entertainment culture that Eliot agrees is visible in the absence of values in plays and novels, and which he predicts will end, if explosives do not intervene, simply in boredom.

The long central exchange is on class. Eliot distinguishes the Christian elite of The Idea of a Christian Society, drawn from all cultural strata, from the class structure considered in Notes towards the Definition of Culture, and offers a paradox: contemplating a stratified society moves one emotionally towards classlessness, and contemplating an actual classless society moves one back. He defends arguing the case for a class society because equalitarianism is the accepted idea, holds that a healthy classless society will tend to form classes and a healthy class society to facilitate transition between them, and locates the evil of each in privilege without responsibility and in responsibility atomised until everyone is equally irresponsible. Paul then raises the writer's position — the divorce of poet from people, the cost of publishing verse — and Eliot answers partly as a publisher. He explains the sandwiching of his plays and poems, says the inspiration for Burnt Norton was lines cut from Murder in the Cathedral, describes hammering out a metric as far as possible from the iambic pentameter, and hopes he may have helped future dramatists return to blank verse 'as a fresh instrument'. The piece ends with the lost manuscript of The Waste Land: bought by John Quinn, absent from the sale of his collection, and — Eliot says, in two minds — perhaps better lost.

- Eliot retracts 'pagan culture' as the likely alternative to Christian society and substitutes the pseudo-religions of Nazism and Communism, which he argues cannot last without an enemy to keep them hot.
- The mass-entertainment 'cultureless culture' is diagnosed as a process of spiritual drying-up whose terminus is boredom.
- The defence of a class society is put as a paradox of emotional oscillation rather than a doctrine, with flexibility between classes as the test of health.
- Eliot speaks as a publisher on the economics of poetry: costs rise while the buying elite stays roughly constant however the population grows.
- He reveals that Burnt Norton grew from lines cut out of Murder in the Cathedral as irrelevant to the action.
- He says he is glad, for his own reputation and The Waste Land's, that Ezra Pound's blue-pencilled manuscript has disappeared — while also wanting it found as evidence of Pound's 'maieutic abilities'.

### Industrial Labour and Management in India
*By Narsing Bahadur Sinha*

Narsing Bahadur Sinha, a lawyer who spent fifteen years as a general manager in Delhi industry, argues in three parts that India does not yet have an industrial working class at all, that it has no independent managerial cadre, and that its industrial-relations law protects neither. Part I holds that the men in town factories remain agricultural labour in character — retaining village roots, language and festivals, reverting to the land on the least pretext, acquiring narrow skills by chance rather than training, indebted, and dependent on 'self-interested and poorly equipped labour union leaders'. His remedy is a system of permanent industrial labour colonies, classified by the workforce of the employing unit, financed by pooled contributions from government, industrialists and workers, with rental ceasing once half the building cost is paid off and occupancy rights then settling on the workers' children. The colonies are to carry schools with a technical bias, night schools and literacy centres, dispensaries and maternity wards, cooperatives, recreation halls and museums of working models of small machines.

Part II argues that managers in the private sector are chosen for kinship or reliability rather than merit, that this blocks able young men and starves industry of talent, and that a government-maintained zonal register of qualified managers — from which industrialists would be obliged to choose, while keeping a wide final choice — would fix it. Part III turns to the law and is the sharpest section: the Chinese attack produced only a temporary industrial truce; workmen who complain are turned out and charge-sheeted afterwards by legally equipped staff; industrial tribunals are staffed by retired men with legal knowledge but no experience of industry, a point Sinha says he put to Prof. Richardson, an I.L.O. expert, who agreed and advised the government accordingly without result. He ends with a list of specific amendments to the Industrial Disputes Act — deleting the sub-clause that excludes managers earning over Rs. 500 a month from the definition of 'workman', tightening the lay-off and retrenchment definitions, and making works committees compulsory in every unit.

- The central claim is definitional: India's town factory workforce is agricultural labour that has not been absorbed in agriculture, not an industrial class.
- The proposed remedy is permanent industrial labour colonies with tripartite financing and inheritable occupancy rights, plus technical schooling from childhood.
- Private-sector managerial appointment by kinship and 'confidential recommendation' is argued to damage industry as much as it damages the excluded.
- Managers earning over Rs. 500 a month fall outside the Industrial Disputes Act's 'workman' and get neither provident fund, state insurance, gratuity nor pension — the article's most concrete legal grievance.
- Lay-off under S.2(kkk) and retrenchment under S.2(oo) are said to be routinely manipulated through manufactured shortages of raw material and accumulations of stock.
- Named specific amendments are proposed, including deleting the proviso that withholds even half wages for lay-off beyond 45 days in a year.

### The Changing Lushai Society
*By Krishan Kumar*

Krishan Kumar sets out to measure social change in the Mizo Hills by taking three cross-sections of Lushai society — before British administration, at the end of British rule, and at the time of writing — and comparing them. The pre-administration cameo is of a society whose unit was the village rather than the clan, focused on the Zawlbuk or bachelors' hall as a training ground for warriors and a school of discipline and social obligation; governed by 'tlawmngaihna', an untranslatable duty of hospitality and unselfishness; self-sufficient in food and cloth; without prostitution, with almost no crime, with a sharp class division between chief and commoner that was nevertheless checked by the villagers' power to migrate away from a despot, and with poverty as we know it practically unknown.

The rest is a catalogue of consequences, most of them unintended. Cheap administration required that the chiefs be preserved, so the British removed the right of migration and thereby turned the chiefs into genuine despots divorced from the people; pacification ended war and with it the chiefs' reason for existing; the Assam Rifles brought disease and a loss of vigour along with order; bridle paths stimulated local trade; mission schools, opened against the administration's own policy, produced the country's third-highest literacy rate and with it a class of educated youths who would not work in the jhums, an exodus to the plains that became 'the eyes and ears of the hills', and the decay of the Zawlbuk system. The shift from barter to coin is treated as the single most disruptive change: it tied local prices to outside markets, produced money-lending at exorbitant rates, undercut the reciprocity of 'tlawmngaihna' because money does not perish, and — through inflation — destroyed the deterrent value of fixed fines and the compensatory meaning of bride price. Christianity is credited with the message of equality and blamed for loosening village bonds, creating a surplus of women by forbidding easy divorce, and driving drinking into private with 'the wholesale practice of deceit'. After independence the dams burst one by one: the chiefs went abruptly, leaving a leadership vacuum; high schools multiplied from one in 1945 to over forty; a 36% population rise in 1951-61 shortened the jhumming cycle to as little as three years and put starvation in view. Kumar's verdict is that transition was inevitable but is arriving as a series of explosions, and that Lushai society is sacrificing its co-operative ethic at the altar of economic advancement.

- The method is explicitly comparative — three 'cameos' of the same society at three arbitrary points of time, used to separate effects of British rule from effects of independence.
- The paradox of indirect rule is the article's strongest claim: preserving the chiefs cheaply required removing the villagers' right to migrate, which turned a checked authority into despotism.
- Mission education, pursued against administrative policy, produced both very high literacy and a class of educated youths unwilling to work the jhums.
- The move from barter to a money economy is treated as the deepest disruption, dissolving 'tlawmngaihna' because money, unlike rice, does not perish.
- Inflation is shown destroying two institutions at once: fixed fines as deterrents against rape and illegitimacy, and bride price as compensation to the father.
- A 36% population increase in the 1951-61 decade plus a jhumming cycle down to three years is presented as a chronic food deficit in the making.

### Non-Attachment in Buddhist Texts and the Gita
*By G. S. P. Misra*

G. S. P. Misra sets out to show, by parallels of phraseology rather than of doctrine alone, that the Gita's teaching of non-attachment borrows from the ascetic religions of sixth-century B.C. India, and in particular from early Buddhism. He begins from Aldous Huxley's remark in Ends and Means that 'non-attached' is the best word for the ideal man of the free philosophers, mystics and founders of religion, and argues that the Gita's achievement was to hold both pravrtti (active life) and nivrtti (complete renunciation) in abeyance — a Brahminical device for assimilating the ascetic ideal and so securing the predominance of Brahminical religion. In the process, he says, it could not help borrowing ideas and principles which then received enunciation in almost the same words.

The argument then proceeds by paired citation. Egoism is identified as the fulcrum of all attachment; the Gita's remedy is subjective purification plus God-realization, a theistic route that Misra compares with the Christian gospel of attachment to God and calls, paradoxically, the preaching of niskama karma by way of sakama karma. Buddhism, having no supreme God, reaches the same doctrine by rational analysis of universal suffering, including parinamadukkhata, the suffering caused by the changeable nature of things, which equates even pleasure with pain; some sects, he notes from the Kathavatthu, denied that any feeling of pleasure exists at all. He then sets Gita verses against passages from the Sutta-nipata, the Samyutta Nikaya and the Theragatha on equanimity, on the monk as a lotus unwetted by the water, on the man who has left both good and evil, on the mind as an ape leaving one branch for another, and finally on the tortoise withdrawing its limbs into its shell — a simile he shows recurring almost word for word in Gita II.58. The conclusion is careful: the practical and dynamic doctrinizing of non-attachment was first furnished in the Gita, but the contribution of the ascetic religions to the theory cannot be denied.

- The thesis is one of borrowed phraseology, not merely parallel doctrine — the Gita is said to reproduce Buddhist formulations almost verbatim.
- The Gita is read as a Brahminical assimilation device: suspending both pravrtti and nivrtti to secure the predominance of Brahminical religion.
- Buddhist non-attachment is derived from the analysis of universal suffering and is therefore said to be free of any theistic influence.
- A three-way comparison is drawn in passing with Christianity, whose devotion to God Misra reads as niskama karma pursued by way of sakama karma.
- The tortoise simile (Samyutta IV and Gita II.58) is offered as the decisive textual parallel.
- All original texts are quoted from Pali Text Society translations, as a closing note states.

### The Decline of the Prophet
*By Om Prakash Sharma*

Om Prakash Sharma's short note, printed under the head 'A Brief Note on the Decline of the Prophet', marks Arnold Toynbee's eighty-fifth birthday by observing that it is a matter of wonder his critics have allowed him to live that long. The argument is that history has let the great historian down: his theory of ageing cultures and civilisations has, Sharma says, a respectably neurotic German pedigree running from Hegel through Nietzsche and Marx to Spengler, all of them believers in predetermined phases marked by catastrophic convulsions; and when Spengler's promised decline of the West failed to arrive after the First World War, Toynbee retreated hastily from a linear to a cyclical view. A Study of History is reported as rifled by experts for 'logical incoherence and empirical invalidity', labelled by H. R. Trevor-Roper 'untrue, illogical, and dogmatic', and yet — the note's central irony — simultaneously more popular than ever, which prompts the question whether one must renounce the academic Eden to gain the whole world.

The second half restates the quarrel as one about fundamentals: what is history and what is the historian's role. Sharma grants that facts without interpretation are 'merely a blind datum of experience', quotes Edward Carr's fishmonger's slab from the Trevelyan lectures, and concedes that any interpretation is somebody's eye-view. But Toynbee's taste, he says, is omnivorous and reckless: a bridge over the deep past and deep future from which he takes frequent leaps in the dark, taking his bearings not from the living present but from the Graeco-Roman world of two thousand years ago. Against J. B. Bury's 1909 emphasis on chance in social evolution, Toynbee's religious temperament posits the meaning of history outside history itself, which Sharma finds hard to distinguish from the ancient Greek's Vast Impersonal Forces. He ends by filing Toynbee's 'meta-history' alongside Adam Smith's hidden hand, Berdyaev's super-historical force and Hegel's Zeitgeist, and letting A. J. P. Taylor have the last word.

- The note is an obituary-in-advance of Toynbee's reputation rather than of Toynbee, whose eighty-fifth birthday is the occasion.
- Toynbee's determinism is given a genealogy — Hegel, Nietzsche, Marx, Spengler — and his shift from a linear to a cyclical view is read as a retreat after Spengler's failed prediction.
- The collapse of Toynbee's academic standing and the rise of his popular standing are presented as possibly causally connected.
- Sharma sides against Ranke's 'crude corpus of ascertained facts' while still refusing Toynbee's leaps beyond the evidence.
- The concluding move is taxonomic: Toynbee's 'meta-history' is filed with Adam Smith's hidden hand, Berdyaev's super-historical force and Hegel's Zeitgeist as a deus ex machina.
- A. J. P. Taylor's washing-up gibe is given the last word, deflating decline-of-civilisation talk into a complaint about domestic service.

### A Solution for Vietnam
*By Hoang Van Chi*

Hoang Van Chi, a Viet Minh veteran who escaped North Vietnam in 1955, argues that American policy in South Vietnam rests on a chain of category errors. His premise is that guerrilla war is not the kind of war Western strategists know: military strength is a function of political capability, and political strength in turn a function of ideology and just cause. The Viet Cong, weak in 1958, grew strong because the Saigon government and the 'nationalist' camp could offer no adequate alternative. He then reconstructs a longer history: traditional Vietnamese society was a pyramid of ten levels cemented by Buddhism and Confucianism, with non-hereditary nobility and administrative posts open to brilliant scholars; the French conquest overturned it, jailing patriotic mandarins and installing unpatriotic ones, whose descendants became the high officials and senior officers of the Diem administration — anti-Communist mainly from fear of losing privilege, and therefore equally afraid of non-Communist opposition.

The article's core is a numbered indictment of six Western mistakes: mistaking industrial development for the cause of democracy and so inventing the 'strong man', which he calls the most damaging theory for liberal American prestige since 1945; limiting knowledge of the Vietnamese to English-speaking city elites and so missing rural injustice; rejecting the Communist solution without offering another; over-estimating Catholic resistance in a primarily Buddhist country; equating stability with progress when nine years of stability under Diem were precisely when the Viet Cong grew; and treating Buddhist and student demonstrations as dangerous instability. To the objection that Buddhists and students may be infiltrated, he answers that genuine popular movements are harder to infiltrate than a few ambitious politicians or officers are to buy, and cites the Kuomintang officers who joined Mao. His prescription is that Washington should favour, or at least not hinder, a non-Communist democratic movement grounded in Buddhist tolerance and student demands for democracy — but he judges his own hope faint, because the South Vietnamese army has undergone a complete 'kuomintang-isation' of which the coup of 20 December 1964 is the latest proof.

- The governing formula is a chain of dependencies: military strength depends on political capability, which depends on ideology and just cause.
- The 'strong man' theory is traced to mistaking industrial development (the effect) for democracy (the cause), and named the most damaging theory for American prestige since 1945.
- Anti-Communism among Saigon's officials is explained as fear of losing privilege, which makes them equally hostile to democratic opposition.
- Nine years of stability under Diem are turned against the stability argument: that was precisely when the Viet Cong acquired their power.
- Buddhism and the student movement are offered as the indigenous antidotes to class struggle and to Communist claims of a just cause.
- A counterfactual pair of historians' verdicts — one generous, one about 'contractors' paid a billion dollars a year — is used to dramatise the gap between stated and actual policy.

### Red Herrings in Indian Ocean
*By Ashin Das Gupta*

Ashin Das Gupta argues that Soekarno's confrontation with Malaysia is a manufactured crisis: the real danger to Malaysia comes from China, not Indonesia, and Indonesia has no earthly reason to be desperate about a neighbour that can do her no harm. He begins by dismantling the national categories. Malay, Indonesian and Filipino are racially more or less one proto-Malay population of about sixty million spread from the Malayan peninsula through Formosa and the Philippines into Polynesia, while Indonesia and Malaysia together hold well over a hundred million, so the archipelago is an 'open world' in which precise definitions are more than usually foolish. But he refuses Soekarno's inference that Malaysia is therefore artificial: nations there are emerging from historical experience rather than demography, since 'imperial boundaries proved to be national frontiers' — otherwise most modern African nations would not make sense.

The substance is a history of Chinese settlement and of Malay nationalism. Taking the peninsula with Singapore, 45% of the population is Chinese against 43% Malay, a situation Das Gupta says is unique in the world; almost all Malaysia's problems trace to that one fact, and the fear is of Malaya becoming an Overseas Territory of Communist China. He distinguishes three waves of Chinese arrival — the 'Queen's Chinese' merchant princes, the nineteenth-century entrepreneurs, and the twentieth-century working class radicalised after the depression — and shows Sun Yat Sen's Tung Meng Hui, its Singapore branch of 1906 and its Overseas Department capturing Chinese vernacular schooling so completely that textbooks made no mention of Malaya. Malay nationalism, by contrast, he dates only to the Japanese occupation, which drove the Chinese into Communist resistance and set Malay against Chinese; UMNO was founded in 1946 against the Malayan Union, the Communist Emergency broke Chinese political power and thereby made independence possible, and Tunku Abdul Rahman's Alliance combined UMNO with the Malay Chinese Association and the Malay Indian Congress. The essay ends by refusing the puppet thesis: British investors do want Malaysia, but foreign bases and an inability to defend oneself against a stronger neighbour would make puppets of a great many governments, and Lee Kuan Yew's People's Action Party is the genuinely encouraging development. What Malaysia needs is time; the arguments about Indonesia and the British are 'so many red herrings'.

- The thesis is that Soekarno's threat is a fake — it can neither win militarily nor split Malay nationalism — while China is the real danger.
- Racial and linguistic categories in the archipelago are shown to be too diffuse to ground nations; historical and imperial boundaries are credited instead.
- The 45% Chinese to 43% Malay head-count for the peninsula plus Singapore is called a situation unique in the world and the root of Malaysia's problems.
- Chinese settlement is divided into three politically distinct waves, with the radicalisation of the working class dated to the depression of the thirties.
- Malay nationalism is dated only to the Japanese occupation, with UMNO founded in 1946 as a right-wing, princely-privilege party later transformed by Tunku Abdul Rahman.
- Plural society and multi-national formation are advanced as the direction of travel, with Macapagal's Maphilindo named as where the future lies.

### India, Pakistan & the Indian Muslims
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

Punya Sloka Ray opens from a maxim of European diplomacy — that every alliance involves a horse and a rider — to argue that co-operation between peoples always means accepting an inequality, and that Muslims and Hindus have never managed an alliance of the tolerable kind achieved by Britain and the United States. Had there been no partition and had Gandhi's and Azad's counsels prevailed, he suggests, India's Muslims might have occupied a position analogous to that of the South in Indian politics today. Partition being irreversible, he confines this article to the India-Pakistan relation and defers the relation between Indian Muslims and Indian Hindus.

His analysis is that Pakistan has the clearer grip on the fact that the subcontinent is a single geopolitical space, and will therefore hate India as long as it cannot love her, but will never be indifferent; the anecdote of General Iskandar Mirza throwing his glass against the wall and crying 'Dilli chalo!' on learning of partition carries the point. Unable to be junior partner, Pakistan has pursued a pax pakistanica — a reformulation of pax islamica — helped by the diehard Tories, then by the USA, and now by Communist China; its strategy, Ray says, is to engineer a moral collapse of the Indian state with Chinese assistance. The flaw is that China will not prefer a pax pakistanica to a pax sinica or even to chaos. Since neither pax pakistanica nor pax indica is on the horizon, the remaining solution is mutual isolation, achievable by giving Pakistan access to Kashmir, by independence for East Pakistan, by complete exchange of populations, or most importantly by establishing an external paramount power — a role he sees the USA drifting into with a measure of Soviet co-operation, denying Communist China access to the subcontinent. The cost, he concludes, will be borne by India's Muslims, whose ties of family, religion and language across the border will weaken until Indian and Pakistani Muslims are no more akin than Turks and Arabs; and since that is inevitable, the question becomes whether it can be accepted consciously and 'planfully' so as to rationalize, moralize and humanize it. The article closes by naming the problem of India's cultural unity as, in the main, the problem of finding a cultural identity for the nationalist Indian Muslim.

- All co-operation between governments or peoples is said to require the acceptance of an inequality — the horse-and-rider aphorism.
- Pakistan is credited with a clearer grasp of the subcontinent as a single geopolitical space, and therefore with permanent non-indifference to India.
- Three pieces of 'extraordinary providence' are identified: the diehard Tories, the USA as buyer, and Communist China as active friend.
- The strategy attributed to Pakistan is engineering a moral collapse of the Indian state; its flaw is assuming a disinterested China.
- Mutual isolation is proposed as the only available solution, with four named mechanisms, of which an external paramount power is called by far the most important.
- Indian Muslims are identified as the party who will bear the cost, and their cultural identity as the real content of the 'cultural unity of India' question.

### Non-Violence and Cold War
*By Raj Krishna and A. S. Ayyub*

The first half of the DISCUSSION is Raj Krishna's letter to co-editor Ayyub, continuing an argument begun with his article 'Non-Violence, War and Peace' in the monsoon 1964 Quest. He insists that non-violence as the one prime value is very much alive in India and has kept policy-makers 'in a state of guilt, confusion and indecision in the teeth of great danger', so that the neglect of India's defences was partly an intellectual muddle about the operational implications of the theory. He denies that he thinks value pluralism a modern discovery — quoting himself to the effect that 'Moral monism is unindian' — and names as monists the panacea-mongers for whom non-violence, or celibacy, or nature cure, or Ramanama, is all.

The philosophical core is a defence of quantification in moral choice. He rejects the popular neo-Gandhian separation of good ends from good means: the same weighted value-set and the same knowledge of consequences that make one will an end must make one will the most efficient means, so that a surgeon's painful operation, or efficient police firing to end a riot, cannot be called bad means to a good end unless 'bad' just means 'inefficient'. Answering Ayyub's charge that values cannot be added and subtracted, he argues as an economist that any ordering of alternatives implies a numeraire, that the numeraire is not itself a value but 'the value-ness of all particular values', and that only a pluralist needs one. He denies that non-violence is more basic than other values, since under abnormal conditions — millions reduced to will-less soldiers by a leader identified with aggressive violence — even killing may have to be organised; and he collapses Ayyub's distinction between justifying violence by the evil it prevents and the good it promotes, since every value has a correlate disvalue. Finally he legitimises the defensive cold war: where one side does not genuinely believe in coexistence but tries to convert, subvert, conquer or dominate, a defensive cold war forced on the threatened nation 'may be an inescapable dharma'. The letter is signed from the Institute of Economic Growth, Delhi, and dated 26 November 1964.

- The charge is that moral monism about non-violence produced real strategic damage — 'the criminal neglect of India's defences'.
- Ends and means must be evaluated by identical criteria; the neo-Gandhian separation of the two is rejected as valuational double-think.
- A numeraire is argued to be a logical necessity of choice, and specifically a pluralist's necessity, not a monist's.
- Non-violence is denied basic status: labelling favourite values 'basic' does not resolve value-conflict.
- Justifying violence by evil prevented rather than good promoted is called a verbal, hair-splitting distinction.
- A defensive cold war forced on a nation by another's aggressive cold war is defended as an inescapable dharma.

Co-editor Abu Sayeed Ayyub's reply, printed under the same DISCUSSION head, first disputes the factual premise. - Democracy is conceded to be a value for only a section of mankind, a concession paired with an unqualified right of self-defence against those who would impose their preferences by force.

### A Sick Faun's Afternoon
*By Cedomir Minderovic*

'A Sick Faun's Afternoon', by the Belgrade poet and library director Cedomir Minderovic and translated by T. Ladan, is a long free-verse monologue of erasure. - History appears as a 'pathetic gun-carriage' bearing grinning skulls, the poem's only overtly political image.

### Fragment: Autobiographical
*By Joan Mas*

Joan Mas's 'Fragment: Autobiographical' is a prose-poem in three numbered parts plus a postscript, spoken partly in the third person about a woman watching a child through a window and partly in the first person in a letter she is writing in her imagination. Part I sets the poem's governing contrast of architecture: the child lives in a cornerless house of pale gold glass, round as a ball, through which everything is observed and reflected perfectly, while the watching woman's house is of so complicated a design, of so many corners and corridors and cupboards and shelves to put things on — rambling, twisted, dim, and a place in which she grows afraid. Part II is the imagined letter to someone never known and known always, a lover addressed as eternal as the stars and asked to 'Be hands. And voice. And ideas. And laughter' — someone who has lit the strange blue house of the speaker's self with delicate candles and planted trees that will flower when he comes. Part III breaks the reverie: the child calls 'mummy', and over the round rim of the child's head, like a moon, the woman aches with sadness while the restless leaves against her windows 'gabble and gabble ... And are like fools, mocking'. The POSTSCRIPT closes the sequence in a register of consummated burial, the warm dark earth opening its arms and drawing the speaker close, so that she can sleep through time as a woman sleeps who after being loved lies relaxed and at peace, her body joined to her lover's 'like a flower to a bough'.

- Structured in three numbered parts plus a POSTSCRIPT, alternating third-person narration with a first-person imagined letter.

- The addressee of the letter is explicitly someone never met, which makes the love it declares wholly imagined.
- The child's interruption in Part III converts the reverie into an ache, framed by the mocking gabble of leaves.

### The Waiting
*By Amaresh Datta*

Amaresh Datta's 'The Waiting' is a single thirty-line poem on a man whose whole life has been deferred. Its opening and closing lines frame it as a vigil for 'the laughter of the sun', qualified at the end into 'the rhododendron laughter of the sun'. The second half diagnoses the deferral. Long days of languid calm, 'Idle and honied with sedative hopes', have formed the wheel of his fate; he now waits not for spring but for a storm to swing his nest, for a violent assurance, for 'the loud noise of light' that would let him see beyond the bounds and the tautologic tale of life. Tamed, tubbed and pruned to the fashionable elegance of taste, he sighs for an unknown anguish of the soul that turns earth into foliage and makes verdure with sapphires of the eye. The wonder touches the edge of despair, and in the cool valley of silence he is still waiting. The indentation alternates between flush-left and indented blocks, marking the alternation between what he waits for and what he once wanted.

- Framed by a repeated line — waiting 'For the laughter of the sun' at the start and for its 'rhododendron laughter' at the close.

### Night of the Dark Trees
*By Abraham Eraly*

Abraham Eraly's 'Night of the Dark Trees' is a first-person story about a man who cannot believe in his own happiness. Varma, a college lecturer in European philosophy, sits in the garden with his wife Sitha and their daughter Urmila and feels a great fear descend with wide black wings; between himself and his wife he keeps seeing the shadow of Itty, barring their parallel lives from converging. The story then goes back three years to a moonlit beach where Itty — tall, indistinct as an old photograph, with a deep bass voice that lent gravity to whatever banality he spoke — accosted him and, over coffee, announced that he was considering marriage and was 'in a hell of a muddle' between a proposal from a well-connected family, a former fiancée, and Sitha.

- The narrator's condition is stated at once — happiness itself is what triggers his fear of unhappiness.
- Itty functions as a shadow rather than a rival: the threat is a claim he once made, not anything Sitha has done.
- Two class-marked love stories are set side by side: Karthu, disposed of by the narrator's mother, and Sitha, lost by the narrator's own warning.
- Itty's narration of the courtship is the story's moral centre — a project of breaking a proud woman, undone by his own indecision.

### Great Sanskrit Plays
*By David McCutchion*

David McCutchion reviews P. Lal's Great Sanskrit Plays in Modern Translation alongside H. W. ### Doctrine and Argument in Indian Philosophy
*By J. N. Chubb*

J. N. Chubb's review of Ninian Smart's Doctrine and Argument in Indian Philosophy, printed under the head 'A Penetrating Interpretation', opens with a general claim about why philosophy needs periodic restatement: thought moves on and raises new problems, and no philosophical system is ever complete, since the maturation of a point of view is not merely the coherent formulation of key concepts but 'a penetration and deepening of insight, a process of soul-seeking and soul-finding'. Criticism, he argues, can operate at two levels — keeping pace with the texts, or going beyond and transforming them without contradicting them, as Plato said his own writings 'beautified and rejuvenated' Socrates. A critical account of Indian philosophy in the second sense has yet to be written, and readers hoping for it here will be disappointed; but Smart's book has penetrativeness without marked profundity, and its exposition and interpretation are thorough and accurate, comparing favourably with Hiriyanna's Outlines and doing something new in assembling arguments from different schools on a few major topics.

The substantive quarrel is with Smart's chapter on religious factors in Indian metaphysics. That logic follows spiritual experience in Indian philosophy Chubb calls true but commonplace, adding that the experience meant should be anubhava or saksatkar — the same as moksa — and not religious practice. Smart's more original thesis, that Indian contemplative experience is pluralistic and that only injected devotionalism turns it into a doctrine of the Absolute, Chubb finds 'as much lacking in truth as it is full of originality', and answers with three points: that the first condition of spiritual life is the turn from bahir-mukha to antar-mukha, that the Self realized by the jnanin is not the individual self, and that Smart himself notes how undifferentiated experience in Nirvana abandons the notion of individual souls.
