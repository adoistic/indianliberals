# Freedom First

## Summary

Freedom First No. 572 (February 2015) is a special issue dedicated to Gopal Krishna Gokhale on the centenary of his death (19 February 1915), timed around a day-long symposium on "Gokhale's Legacy" held on 15 November 2014 by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom with the Project for Economic Education, the Indian Secular Society and LiberalsIndia for Good Governance. The bulk of the issue -- an editorial note, an excerpted A. B. Shah foreword, and essays by S. P. Aiyar, Aroon Tikekar, Sunil Gokhale (Gokhale's great-grandson), Prabha Ravi Shankar, Godrej N. Dotivala and R. Srinivasan, plus a student report on the symposium -- traces Gokhale's life, his mentorship under Mahadev Govind Ranade, his moderate constitutionalist politics against Tilak's extremism, his founding of the Servants of India Society, his influence on Gandhi as 'political guru', and tributes from contemporaries such as Pherozeshah Mehta.

The remainder of the issue turns to current affairs: M. R. Venkatesh assesses whether Budget 2015 can deliver 'Make in India'; Ashok Karnik's 'Point Counter Point' debates the Bengaluru ISIS-tweeter case, the Peshawar school massacre, Hindutva overreach, and the Coast Guard's sinking of a suspected Pakistani smuggling boat; B. Ramesh Babu analyses a shifting global balance of power towards renewed bipolarity; Firoze Hirjikaka and Bapu Satyanarayana both worry that Hindutva zealots within the BJP/RSS are undermining Narendra Modi's development agenda; Ashish Punthambekar considers the Peshawar attack's implications for Pakistan's stability and NATO strategy; and a book review by Dr. Ali Khwaja covers Raphael Susewind's study of Muslim peace workers in post-2002 Gujarat. The issue closes with symposium photographs and a back-cover 'Je Suis Charlie' tribute condemning the Charlie Hebdo killings.

### The Legacy of Gopal Krishna Gokhale - Introduction
*By A. B. Shah*

S. P. Aiyar's introduction to 'Gokhale and Modern India' surveys Gokhale's 49-year life as one of grandeur and tragedy: his physical collapse under the strain of the Islington Commission, his moral inheritance from Ranade's 'humanise, equalise, spiritualise' philosophy, his founding of the Servants of India Society, and his famous farewell address on leaving Fergusson College in 1902. It contrasts Gokhale's confidence in reasoned persuasion and constitutional method with Gandhi's and Tilak's more radical approaches, noting that Gokhale feared passive resistance would erode the 'foundations of public life' and habituate the masses to disobedience, while still being revered by Gandhi as a 'political guru' despite an enduring philosophical gulf between them.

- Gokhale's premature death in 1915 followed years of physical strain, including his work on the Islington Commission (1912).
- Ranade shaped Gokhale's belief that progress requires simultaneous political, social and economic reform ('a functioning synthesis').
- Gokhale's farewell address to Fergusson College (1902) is presented as one of his most moving personal statements.
- Gokhale and Gandhi diverged fundamentally on Western civilisation: Gokhale trusted Western science and institutions, Gandhi distrusted them.
- Gokhale feared that passive resistance/non-cooperation would teach the masses habits of disobedience and undermine constitutional government.

### Laying the Foundation for a Modern India
*By S. P. Aiyar*

Aroon Tikekar's essay, based on a speech at Fergusson College, argues for Gokhale's continuing relevance as the 'Gladstone of India' and the greatest creation of Ranade's liberal tradition. It surveys Gokhale's European-style liberalism (individualism, constitutionalism, laissez faire), his refusal to perform ritual purification after sea voyages unlike Tilak, his relationships with Sarla Ray and other Poona contemporaries, and his enduring but paradoxical bond with Gandhi and Jinnah, both of whom sought his mentorship. It closes by noting that Tilak, Gokhale's great rival, still eulogised him at his funeral as 'this diamond of India'.

- Gokhale is presented as a product of Ranade's liberal tradition, but of a purer European liberalism than Ranade's own Indian-inflected version.
- Gokhale refused to perform suddhikaran (ritual purification) after his sea voyages to Britain, unlike Tilak.
- Sarla Ray, grandmother of Satyajit Ray, challenged Gokhale over wearing the sacred thread; he reportedly cut it in response.
- Both Gandhi and Mohammed Ali Jinnah sought to be seen as Gokhale's political heirs, Jinnah aspiring to be the 'Muslim Gokhale'.
- Tilak's funeral tribute to Gokhale as 'this diamond of India' is presented as evidence of Gokhale's stature even among rivals.

It closes on Gokhale's insistence on unity in diversity and secularism, and his enduring but asymmetrical bond with Gandhi and Jinnah.

- Gokhale is remembered abroad: Mauritius has a Gokhale Hall and Fiji a Gokhale School.
- Despite deep mutual respect, Gandhi never joined Gokhale's Servants of India Society and ultimately rejected his faith in Western institutions.
