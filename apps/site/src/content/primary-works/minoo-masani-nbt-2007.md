summary: "S. V. Raju's short biography, issued in 2007 in the National Book Trust's National Biography series, tells the life of Minoo Masani (1905-1998) as the story of a principled politician who moved from the Congress Socialist Party to the Swatantra Party and spent most of his public life in opposition. Raju worked with Masani for nearly four decades from December 1959, first in the Swatantra Party's Central Office and then in the organisations Masani founded, and the book is openly an insider's tribute that also admits Masani's difficult, punctilious, 'purist' temperament. The Introduction sets Masani in the line of Naoroji and Gokhale, quotes R. Srinivasan on him as a vanishing species of liberal intellectual and J.R.D. Tata on a former Marxist who admitted he had been wrong, and lists the organisations he founded: the Democratic Research Service, the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, the Indian Liberal Group, the Leslie Sawhny Programme and the Project for Economic Education.\n\nThe first half follows Masani's political education. Born into a Parsi family in Bombay, the son of the knighted Sir Rustom Masani, he studied at the London School of Economics, became a Labour-inclined socialist, visited the Soviet Union in 1927 and returned to the Bombay Bar. Drawn into Civil Disobedience by Yusuf Meherally, he was jailed in 1932-33, at Nasik met Jayaprakash Narayan, Lohia and Patwardhan, and helped found the Congress Socialist Party, whose Joint Secretary he became under JP. Chapter 4 traces his turn against Soviet communism through the 1935 and later Soviet visits, the Great Purges, the Comintern's United Front tactic and the communists' infiltration of the CSP, ending in his resignation in November 1939. Chapter 5 covers his ten-day 1934 walking tour with Gandhi and the exchange of letters about the CSP programme, and sets out what Masani took from Gandhi: ends and means are linked, the State should not be glorified, and power should be decentralised. Chapter 6 presents Socialism Reconsidered (1944), which questions four Marxist assumptions, and his 1947 A Plea for a Mixed Economy, with a small nationalised sector, a larger new public sector and the largest sector left to free enterprise.\n\nThe middle chapters cover his working life and his legislative career. He spent sixteen years with the Tatas from 1941, working on workers' participation at TISCO and on the Declaration on the Social Responsibilities of Business of 1965, and set up his own management consultancy so that he need not live off politics. As a Congress member of the Indian Legislative Assembly and Constituent Assembly from 1945, he pressed for a uniform civil code, proportional representation instead of reserved seats, abolition of titles and planning on the States list, and he broke with Nehru over the Liaquat Ali budget, communism and the Cold War before leaving the Congress in 1952. Chapters 9 and 10 describe the Nagpur resolution on cooperative farming as the tipping point for the Swatantra Party, founded under Rajaji in 1959, with Masani as General Secretary and organiser: 25 Lok Sabha seats in 1962, 44 in 1967, the party's Statement of Policy 'To Prosperity Through Freedom', the 1971 Grand Alliance dispute with Rajaji that led to Masani's resignation, and the 1974 dissolution he opposed.\n\nThe last two chapters portray him as a model parliamentarian, who opened the Finance Bill debate for a decade, chaired the Public Accounts Committee in 1967-69, refused to oppose for opposition's sake, and kept faith with his Rajkot constituents. They then follow his retirement from politics in 1971, his role as chronicler and adviser during JP's Total Revolution, his stand against the Emergency through Freedom First (including the Bombay High Court case against pre-censorship), his refusal to join the Janata coordinating committees, and his resignation from the Minorities Commission in May 1978 when the government ignored it. The book ends with his later projects, his last children's book We Indians (1989), his blindness and his death on 27 May 1998, and a list of his books and pamphlets. The narrative leans heavily on Masani's own memoirs and quoted speeches and gives little independent critical evaluation of his judgements."

  key_points:
    - "Masani is presented as a rare principled politician who repeatedly resigned or retired rather than compromise: from the CSP in 1939, from the Congress in 1952, as Swatantra President in 1971, and as Minorities Commission chairman in 1978."
    - "His path ran from British Labour-style socialism and admiration of the Soviet Union to opposition to communism, helped by the Great Purges, the Comintern's United Front tactics and the communist capture of CSP-founded bodies such as the AITUC and the Kisan Sabha."
    - "Gandhi's 1934 walking tour and exchange of letters, and Socialism Reconsidered (1944), shifted Masani to a Mixed Economy with decentralisation, workers' control and a large free-enterprise sector; Raju stresses that he kept the social-justice aim of socialism."
    - "In the Constituent Assembly Masani argued for a uniform civil code, proportional representation in place of reserved seats, abolition of titles and planning on the States list, and lost most of these battles."
    - "The Nagpur resolution on joint cooperative farming (1959) is identified as the tipping point for the Swatantra Party, which Rajaji founded and Masani organised as General Secretary, winning 25 Lok Sabha seats in 1962 and 44 in 1967."
    - "The 1971 Grand Alliance dispute, when Masani wanted a common programme and not the negative 'Indira Hatao' slogan, is the first fundamental difference between Rajaji and Masani and leads to the party's fall and dissolution in 1974."
    - "As a parliamentarian Masani opened the Finance Bill debate for ten years, chaired the Public Accounts Committee, supported Indira Gandhi's devaluation of the rupee after consulting B.R. Shenoy, and said an alternative government must exist before the incumbent is removed."
    - "During JP's movement and the Emergency he acted as chronicler and adviser, called the Emergency a constitutional coup d'etat, fought censorship of Freedom First in court and doubted that the Janata victory was a 'second liberation'."
    - "Raju writes as a close associate, and the book relies heavily on Masani's memoirs and speeches, so its judgements are largely sympathetic and unargued against alternative views."

# Minoo Masani

## Summary

S. V. Raju's short biography, issued in 2007 in the National Book Trust's National Biography series, tells the life of Minoo Masani (1905-1998) as the story of a principled politician who moved from the Congress Socialist Party to the Swatantra Party and spent most of his public life in opposition. Raju worked with Masani for nearly four decades from December 1959, first in the Swatantra Party's Central Office and then in the organisations Masani founded, and the book is openly an insider's tribute that also admits Masani's difficult, punctilious, 'purist' temperament. The Introduction sets Masani in the line of Naoroji and Gokhale, quotes R. Srinivasan on him as a vanishing species of liberal intellectual and J.R.D. Tata on a former Marxist who admitted he had been wrong, and lists the organisations he founded: the Democratic Research Service, the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, the Indian Liberal Group, the Leslie Sawhny Programme and the Project for Economic Education.

The first half follows Masani's political education. Born into a Parsi family in Bombay, the son of the knighted Sir Rustom Masani, he studied at the London School of Economics, became a Labour-inclined socialist, visited the Soviet Union in 1927 and returned to the Bombay Bar. Drawn into Civil Disobedience by Yusuf Meherally, he was jailed in 1932-33, at Nasik met Jayaprakash Narayan, Lohia and Patwardhan, and helped found the Congress Socialist Party, whose Joint Secretary he became under JP. Chapter 4 traces his turn against Soviet communism through the 1935 and later Soviet visits, the Great Purges, the Comintern's United Front tactic and the communists' infiltration of the CSP, ending in his resignation in November 1939. Chapter 5 covers his ten-day 1934 walking tour with Gandhi and the exchange of letters about the CSP programme, and sets out what Masani took from Gandhi: ends and means are linked, the State should not be glorified, and power should be decentralised. Chapter 6 presents Socialism Reconsidered (1944), which questions four Marxist assumptions, and his 1947 A Plea for a Mixed Economy, with a small nationalised sector, a larger new public sector and the largest sector left to free enterprise.

The middle chapters cover his working life and his legislative career. He spent sixteen years with the Tatas from 1941, working on workers' participation at TISCO and on the Declaration on the Social Responsibilities of Business of 1965, and set up his own management consultancy so that he need not live off politics. As a Congress member of the Indian Legislative Assembly and Constituent Assembly from 1945, he pressed for a uniform civil code, proportional representation instead of reserved seats, abolition of titles and planning on the States list, and he broke with Nehru over the Liaquat Ali budget, communism and the Cold War before leaving the Congress in 1952. Chapters 9 and 10 describe the Nagpur resolution on cooperative farming as the tipping point for the Swatantra Party, founded under Rajaji in 1959, with Masani as General Secretary and organiser: 25 Lok Sabha seats in 1962, 44 in 1967, the party's Statement of Policy 'To Prosperity Through Freedom', the 1971 Grand Alliance dispute with Rajaji that led to Masani's resignation, and the 1974 dissolution he opposed.

The last two chapters portray him as a model parliamentarian, who opened the Finance Bill debate for a decade, chaired the Public Accounts Committee in 1967-69, refused to oppose for opposition's sake, and kept faith with his Rajkot constituents. They then follow his retirement from politics in 1971, his role as chronicler and adviser during JP's Total Revolution, his stand against the Emergency through Freedom First (including the Bombay High Court case against pre-censorship), his refusal to join the Janata coordinating committees, and his resignation from the Minorities Commission in May 1978 when the government ignored it. The book ends with his later projects, his last children's book We Indians (1989), his blindness and his death on 27 May 1998, and a list of his books and pamphlets. The narrative leans heavily on Masani's own memoirs and quoted speeches and gives little independent critical evaluation of his judgements.
