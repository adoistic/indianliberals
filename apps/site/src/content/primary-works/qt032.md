# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 32 (contents page: JAN./MAR. 1962; front cover: 'winter 1961-'62') is the Bombay quarterly of the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, edited by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta with Nirad C. Chaudhuri and Laxmanshastri Joshi as advisory editors, Nissim Ezekiel as Reviews Editor, Laeeq Futehally as assistant, and Sheela Singh as Business Manager. The contents page bills the magazine 'A Quarterly of Inquiry, Criticism and Ideas'; the cover calls it 'An Adventure of Ideas'. The issue's argumentative centre is the relation between cultural autonomy and political power. G. E. G. Catlin reads Rabindranath Tagore (in his centenary season) as the anti-nationalist prophet of cultural freedom; Ashin Das Gupta tells Indian historians that communal and regional history-writing is immoral in 1962 and that 'propaganda for the good cause has become a necessity'; Ram Joshi weighs Maharashtra's district-council scheme against the Balvantraya Mehta Committee's block model and against caste-bound village society; Harold A. Gould argues that colonialism arrested an indigenous Indian modernisation already under way, so that Western aid is restitution rather than charity; Pranab Bardhan marshals tax, wage and expenditure data to show that a decade of planning has widened inequality. Around this sit two 'Angles on the German Problem' — Salvador de Madariaga on the Berlin Wall as Khrushchev's closed shop-window, Martin S. Dworkin on the postwar Anglo-American film habit of separating decent Germans from dirty politics — plus David McCutchion on Stendhal, F. R. Stanley on Kabir, Jagannath Prasad Das on the scientific pretensions of psychoanalysis, three poems, a short story set in Shillong, a six-page books column, nine signed book reviews, six unsigned shorter notices, and two letters.

The issue is emphatically a Congress for Cultural Freedom product: the Berlin Wall, Soviet propaganda on Gagarin, the Eichmann trial, the Nazi past and the Soviet re-assessment of Gandhi are all handled from a liberal anti-totalitarian position, while the domestic pieces are sceptical of Indian planning and of what Bardhan calls 'that monument of platitudes and cliches, The Third Five Year Plan'. It also carries no artwork or photographic plate of any kind — every visual page in the issue is a commercial advertisement (Dunlop, Bengal Potteries, Mahindra, Mobil, Tata-Mercedes-Benz, Khatau, Wills' Gold Flake, Tinopal, a Government of India tourist-books page, Jamshedpur/Tata, Peps, Caltex, IISCO, Mafatlal, Indian Oil, Small Savings, Kohinoor, Hindustan Motors, Encounter, Hindustan Lever), the sole exception being a small comedy-and-tragedy masks vignette beside the 'BOOKS' section head on printed p.77. That absence is a genuine finding for this number of the run.

## Essays

### Rabindranath Tagore
*By G. E. G. Catlin*

Catlin's piece, abridged from an address printed in the Journal of the Royal Society of Arts, reads Tagore not as a poet who dabbled in politics but as a political thinker whose central category was the Nation — and whose central hostility was to it. Catlin traces four episodes of 'moral indignation': the 1900 patriotic Stories and Tales, the campaign against Curzon's partition of Bengal, the 1919 renunciation of the knighthood after Jallianwala Bagh, and the 1940 refusal (against Eleanor Rathbone's appeal) to bring India into the war on terms India had not been consulted about. Against Gandhi's swaraj he sets Tagore's fear of Bengali chauvinism and his descent from Rammohun Roy's Brahmo reformism.

The argument's core is Tagore's definition of the Nation as 'a machinery of commerce and politics', a hydraulic press of impersonal power which Catlin links to Hobhouse's 'metaphysical idea of the State' and to what 'we call alienation'. Tagore's quarrel with empire, Catlin insists, was cultural rather than economic: what outraged him was Macaulay's educational strait-waistcoat, and he would not necessarily have preferred a Hindi culture imposed on Bengal. A second movement of the essay defends Tagore's distrust of science and his Upanishadic metaphysics, glossed through Radhakrishnan and then — in a surprising turn — through Teilhard de Chardin's Milieu Divin, whose theme Catlin finds 'almost exactly the same pattern of thought' as Tagore's.

- Presents Tagore as political thinker and 'Great Sentinel', not as an occasional political poet; notes he attended a Congress annual meeting only once, to sing Bande Mataram.
- Four episodes of moral indignation anchor the reading: Stories and Tales (1900), the anti-partition campaign against Curzon, the 1919 resignation of the knighthood after Jallianwala Bagh, and the 1940 exchange with Eleanor Rathbone over the war.
- Tagore's 'Nation' is a machinery of impersonal power; Catlin ties it to Hobhouse's 'Metaphysical Idea of the State' and to modern 'alienation'.
- The indictment of empire is cultural, not economic: Macaulay's education system is the outrage, and Catlin adds pointedly that a Hindi culture imposed on Bengal would have fared no better.
- Closes by aligning Tagore's metaphysics with Radhakrishnan's Verstand/living-concrete distinction and with Teilhard de Chardin, arguing the verities Tagore expressed 'are not those either of India or of the East alone'.

### Democratic Decentralisation
*By Ram Joshi*

Joshi's article (printed under the fuller title 'Democratic Decentralisation and the Problem of Local Leadership') sets out the political problem behind panchayati raj. Independent India's 'modernist elite' inherited a tradition of leadership that was 'symbolic' rather than 'creative': habitual obedience to a superior personality, rights tied to caste function rather than to individuals. The result is a democracy of 'soulless formalism' in which governments announce large welfare schemes, find no popular co-operation, fall back on the bureaucracy, and thereby isolate themselves further. Decentralisation matters, Joshi argues, less as a constitutional ideal than as the only institutional device that can manufacture the local leadership the Plans need.

He then compares two models. The Balvantraya Mehta Committee recommended the block as the unit with an indirectly elected pyramid topped by the Zila Parishad; the Government of Maharashtra proposes the district, with directly elected District Councils, almost no ex-officio membership, and committees elected by proportional representation with a single transferable vote. Joshi prefers Maharashtra's: a district constituency of some thirty-five thousand is winnable by hard work rather than by purse, and the committee principle forces opposition parties to share in making and executing decisions instead of merely opposing. He is candid about the dangers — caste rigidities are hardening, not loosening, and 'village gangsterism' is a real risk — and equally candid that there is almost no popular demand for decentralisation, rural people preferring the Collector as protection against 'the predatory invasion on their power and purse by the local politicians'. Against the romantic appeal to ancient village republics he is blunt: self-sufficiency was a function of isolation, and the ancient village was 'the arrested village'.

- Diagnoses Indian leadership as 'symbolic' not 'creative' (citing Schmidt), producing a democracy of 'soulless formalism' in which mass expectations expand government while popular co-operation does not follow.
- Contrasts the Mehta Committee's block unit and indirectly elected Zila Parishad with Maharashtra's directly elected District Councils, and prefers the latter as 'distinctly more democratic'.
- Argues the committee principle plus proportional representation by single transferable vote will make opposition parties responsible partners rather than pure obstructors.
- Rejects the historical argument from ancient village republics: their sufficiency rested on isolation and fatalistic caste routine.
- Concedes the experiments respond to doctrine and to the fear of Plan failure rather than to any popular demand, and warns of casteism, parochialism and village gangsterism.

### Stendhal and Romanticism
*By David McCutchion*

McCutchion's essay — the longest in the issue, set in smaller type across eight pages — uses Stendhal to anatomise Romanticism. Following Irving Babbitt, he defines Romanticism as exalted idealism married to unrestrained egoism, deprived of moral discipline and therefore doomed to the mal du siecle. Stendhal is the exception who proves the type: divided between 'logique' (French analytical intellect) and 'espagnolisme' (sensibility nurtured on the Nouvelle Heloise), he is the first writer to accommodate himself to the failure of absolutes and 'to realise that relativity is the price of freedom'.

Much of the essay is close reading. Julien Sorel combines Romantic energy with Romantic sensibility but is set loose in a real, harsh world, so that 'every attempt to actualise an ideal betrays it'; Mathilde de la Mole is an overt satire of Romantic love; Lucien Leuwen is the satire of parliamentary democracy and of the bank that 'is at the head of the state'. McCutchion reads Stendhal's Restoration France as devitalised in every sphere — the lopped plane trees of Verrieres, the Besancon seminary that shuts out life, the Paris salon 'dying of boredom with its own existence' — and Julien's energy as the locus of value in a conflict not of good and evil but of life and death. The last pages turn the argument on the present: Stendhal's philosophy of 'the happy few' who accept the rules of the game to gain scope for their own gifts is, McCutchion suggests, one of 'the chief forces of disintegration in European society today', because in an affluent society everyone believes himself one of the happy few and the 'chase for happiness' becomes the aim of life. He closes with Kierkegaard's aesthetic/ethical/religious stages, wondering whether societies run them in reverse.

- Defines Romanticism via Irving Babbitt as idealism plus egoism minus moral discipline, producing the 'children of Werther' and the mal du siecle.
- Stendhal is the divided exception: 'logique' against 'espagnolisme', a realist who knows when he is happy and who accepts relativity as the price of freedom.
- Reads Scarlet and Black as the Romantic predicament staged in a real world, with Mathilde de la Mole as explicit satire of Romantic love and Julien's energy as the essay's locus of value.
- Reads Lucien Leuwen as satire of parliamentary democracy and of finance capital: 'the bank is the nobility of the bourgeois class'.
- Turns the analysis on 1962: the ethic of 'the happy few' has become mass aesthetic hedonism, 'the betrayal of the clerks' complete, with Durrell's Alexandria Quartet and Swami Agehananda cited as symptoms.

### The Historian in India, 1962
*By Ashin Das Gupta*

Das Gupta opens with Nelson at Trafalgar to say that in 1962 India needs more than the traditional duty, and then asks what the historian's traditional duty is. Section I argues that it is impossible to discharge: a historian who searches his sources with the best techniques still gives only 'some truths about the past', consoles himself with a segment and thereby distorts by omission. Even the detached antiquarian distorts, 'by robbing the past of its vitality'. The right conclusion is not despair but modesty: 'all history is false, but some history is less false than others.'

Section II makes the political turn. Das Gupta thinks it probable that 'in some years from now the Republic of India will break down in confusion', and that historians have a vested interest in the integrity of their country. The danger is not a faulty reading of the past but a vicious mental climate, to which a particular kind of history contributes: communal history and history that exalts one region at the expense of others. His striking move is to concede the factual accuracy of such work — historians who bring up 'stories of sustained Muslim atrocities against the Hindus' are not necessarily lying — and to condemn it on moral rather than evidential grounds: 'communal and regional preoccupations are immoral.' Traditional tests (are the facts ascertained? does the thesis account for most known facts?) will catch the worst of it, but they are not enough; the real work is to build new preoccupations, telling the story of the common people 'quietly underlining its totality and synthesis'. Section III answers three objections — that this denies historical objectivity, that it licenses private moralities, and that it is either dictatorial or futile — by distinguishing objective factual assertions from unavoidably non-objective generalisations, and by insisting that morality should shape the orientation of research, never the acceptance or rejection of a thesis.

- The historian's traditional duty — the truth about the past — is unperformable; all history distorts, whether by omission, by predilection, or by antiquarian lack of imagination.
- 'All history is false, but some history is less false than others' — work with diligence and circumspection to approximate an unattainable totality.
- Predicts a possible breakdown of the Republic and identifies communal and regionalist history-writing as a contributor to the 'vicious mental climate' behind it.
- Concedes that communal historians may be factually accurate and condemns them morally instead; the antidote is not refutation but a new social and economic history of the common people.
- Distinguishes two levels of historical statement (objective specific facts; inescapably less objective abstractions) so that moral considerations guide the choice of subject, never the validation of a thesis.

### West's Real Debt to East
*By Harold A. Gould*

Gould's article (headed 'The West's Real Debt to the East') begins from a Dutch scholar's account of Indonesia as a congeries of progress and stagnation, and from the justification of Dutch rule as better than 'the former feudal conditions'. Gould inverts the argument. Down to the mid-eighteenth century the East-West encounter was a commercial draw, often more profitable to Asia; Mughal India, Ming China and the Southeast Asian entrepots were richer and more venerable than Europe. What upset the balance was the Industrial Revolution, which turned Europe from a buyer of Asian handicrafts into a hunter of raw materials and captive markets, and which sent European populations up six hundred per cent, supplying both the motive and the manpower for expansion. Asia's simultaneous internal collapse — Aurangzeb's defeat of Dara Shikoh, the Maratha catastrophe at Panipat, the Manchu decay in China — meant the great civilisations were subdued not even by states but by trading companies.

Colonialism, on Gould's account, produced societies 'modern' in administration, transport and communication but 'backward' in production, with a Westernised elite alienated from peasants barred from the modern economy — hence the appearance of progress and non-progress together. Against Max Weber's verdict that the ritual law of caste could not give birth to economic revolutions from within, Gould offers the Bhakti mystics — Ramanand, Kabir, Nanak, Garibdas, Chaitanya — quoting D. P. Mukerjee on their revolt against caste and ritual, and argues that British policy to 'honour native custom' froze that reform current by re-empowering the high-caste factions Bhakti had been challenging. Japan, spared a colonial master, is his one concrete counter-case. He is careful not to demonise European individuals — quoting Redfield on judging earlier stages of civilisation, and conceding that Parliament and even part of Gandhi's outlook were Western gifts — but concludes that the East's involuntary 'aid programs' financed the West's industrialisation, and that repayment is a right rather than a charity.

- The 15th-18th century encounter was 'a draw from the standpoint of power', often more profitable to Asian than to Western societies.
- The Industrial Revolution, plus a 600 per cent European population rise, converted Asia into supplier of raw materials and receiver of finished goods, destroying its handicraft integration.
- Asia's near-simultaneous political collapse (Aurangzeb over Dara Shikoh, Panipat, the Manchu Ch'ing decay) let mere trading companies dominate whole civilisations.
- Contests Max Weber directly: the Bhakti reform current was already dissolving caste rigidity, and British 'honour native custom' policy reversed it by confirming high-caste authority.
- Concludes that colonies were involuntary one-way 'aid programmes' to the West, so Western capital transfer today is restitution, not generosity; Japan is the counterfactual.

### Income Distribution in India
*By Pranab Bardhan*

Bardhan's article (printed as 'Recent Trends in the Distribution of Income in India', under a Lewis Carroll epigraph about dyeing one's whiskers green and using a large fan so they cannot be seen) asks where a decade of planning has sent the national income. His frame is Kuznets's long secular swing in inequality, and the observation that developing economies, unlike Victorian England, cannot postpone egalitarian claims; India's professed answer was rapid growth plus equality, and it has delivered neither — a 3.5 per cent growth rate, half Turkey's and a third of Iraq's, and a Mahalanobis committee whose findings were still undisclosed more than a year on.

The body is an assembly of scattered evidence. The new direct taxes have made no dent: Wealth Tax receipts were 0.05 per cent of national income in 1960-61, Estate Duty, Gift, Expenditure and Wealth taxes together 0.08 per cent, while the share of direct taxes in total receipts fell from 32.6 to 29.5 per cent and the excise burden shifted towards 'wage-goods'. In agriculture, roughly 10 per cent of the population took more than half the total agricultural income in 1953-54, and over 30 per cent of the decade's income increase accrued to holdings of 30 acres and over — 3 per cent of rural people. Agricultural labour families' total income fell 11 per cent in absolute terms between 1950-51 and 1956-57. Industrial labour's share stayed flat while the profits index rose from 100 to 151.7; unemployment's backlog grew from 5 to 9 million. Consumer expenditure data show the lowest 75 per cent static and the top 10 per cent gaining. Bardhan then dismantles the standard defences — that early inequality is historically inevitable, that egalitarianism blunts incentives, that inequality finances savings (savings rose only from 6.7 to 7.7 per cent of national income), and that redistribution is politically unfeasible — and ends by asking whether India is 'having second thoughts on socialist ideals'.

- Growth of 3.5 per cent a year is half Turkey's and a third of Iraq's; the Mahalanobis committee on distribution had reported nothing after more than a year.
- The celebrated new direct taxes are negligible: Wealth Tax 0.05 per cent of national income in 1960-61, and Estate Duty plus Gift, Expenditure and Wealth taxes together 0.08 per cent — 'The mountain has evidently produced a poor little mouse.'
- Agricultural gains are concentrated: about 10 per cent of the agricultural population took over half of agricultural income in 1953-54; over 30 per cent of the decade's increase went to holdings of 30 acres and above, only 3 per cent of rural people.
- Agricultural labour families' total income fell 11 per cent in absolute terms 1950-51 to 1956-57, while industrial real earnings crept to 102.6 against a productivity index of 132.7 and a profits index of 151.7.
- Rebuts the inevitability, incentive, savings-financing and political-infeasibility defences of inequality, quoting Kuznets against the analogy with historical Western Europe.

### The True Disciple
*By F. R. Stanley*

Stanley's essay (printed as 'The True Disciple: An Essay on Kabir', under an epigraph from a letter of Plotinus to Flaccus) begins at Maghar, where a Hindu shrine tended by a sadhu of the Kabir Panth and a Muslim shrine held by a family of weavers stand divided by a high wall — two monuments, he says, to the Oneness that Kabir preached. He retells the legend of Kabir's discipleship to Ramananda, notes that the one authenticated biographical fact is his weaving, and reads the loom imagery of the 28th Ramaini of the Bijak as Kabir's image of God. The argument is that Kabir's rejection of Hindu and Muslim dogma alike — pilgrimage, fasting, sacred threads, circumcision, idolatry, asceticism — follows from a single conviction that the unity of God is manifested in a unity of love uniting all castes and creeds.

The second half places Kabir among the mystics. His God is neither abstraction nor impersonal infinite; the Bijak's 6th and 7th Ramainis stage the difficulty of describing the indescribable, and the Adi-Granth supplies the settled claim that purity of heart is what permits the vision of God. Stanley sets Kabir's period — the declining Delhi Sultanate, Timur's invasion of 1398, Lodi bigotry — as the political background against which the Bhakti movement rose, and traces the line Ramanuja to Ramananda to Kabir. He notes the wordplay on manaka/pher that carries the rosary couplet, the Sufi 'stations of the soul' that structure Kabir's spiritual pilgrimage, and the scale of Kabir's afterlife: a following of a million or more, thousands of sakhis still current by word of mouth in northern India, and Kabir Panth sects of both Hindus and Muslims claiming him.

- Opens at the twin shrines of Maghar, Hindu and Muslim, divided by a wall — offered as monuments to the unity Kabir preached.
- Kabir's weaving is 'the only authentic piece of information' about him; the loom supplies the Bijak's central image of God as weaver.
- His rejection of both Hindu and Muslim ritual follows from one premise: the unity of God manifested as a unity of love across caste and creed.
- Places Kabir historically in the declining Delhi Sultanate after Timur's 1398 invasion, and doctrinally in the Bhakti line from Ramanuja through Ramananda.
- Reads Kabir as a mystic in the Sufi 'stations of the soul' sense — dying to self, then awakening to God — whose posthumous following crossed both communities.

### Psychoanalysis Re-examined
*By Jagannath Prasad Das*

Das asks whether psychoanalysis is a science and answers no, while insisting that a theory can be useful without producing rigorous laws. He begins with the sociology of the movement: Freud's popularity rivals Marx's, some contemporary novels can hardly be told from psychiatric case reports, and to the man in the street psychology just is psychoanalysis. But Freud's habit of ascribing personal motives to theoretical disagreement — Jung's 'lofty ideas', Adler's will to power, Havelock Ellis's presumed impotence — sealed the movement off from criticism. The set-up is self-confirming: no one who has not been analysed may contribute, and the years of analysis wash out the complexes and the critical attitude together.

Das then sets out the theory fairly — id, ego, superego; Eros and Thanatos; repression, rationalisation, sublimation and regression; the psychopathology of everyday life — before locating the fallacy: 'regarding observation and explanation as inseparable', so that the truth of Freud's observations casts a halo over explanations with no experimental moorings. The heaviest charge is biological reductionism: Freud forgot that man is a social animal, and built the universality of the Oedipus complex on a sample of neurotic upper-middle-class Germans. Totem and Taboo is 'a woeful performance' by anthropological standards. Das surveys the neo-Freudian revolt (Kardiner, Fromm) to show that only the form of psychoanalysis survives in it, notes Freud's late conversion to occultism and his defence of lay analysis as evidence of an unscientific temper, and concludes on therapy: prolonged, expensive, with cure rates not significantly different from no treatment, though the analyst remains 'a great source of comfort and consolation' in impersonal big cities — which, Das says, proves little about id, ego or superego.

- Distinguishes psychoanalysis's cultural authority from its scientific standing, and allows that unverifiable theories can still contribute.
- Documents Freud's practice of converting theoretical dissent into diagnosis (Jung, Adler, Havelock Ellis), and the closed-circle rule that only the analysed may contribute.
- Names the core fallacy: treating observation and explanation as inseparable, so true observations lend a halo to unmoored explanations.
- Attacks the biological reductionism — man as biological rather than social unit, the Oedipus complex universalised from neurotic upper-middle-class German patients, Totem and Taboo dismissed by anthropologists.
- Finds the neo-Freudians (Kardiner, Fromm) retaining only the form of psychoanalysis, and reports that American analytic associations claim no statistically acceptable evidence of curative power.

### 1) A Chinese Wall in Europe
*By Salvador de Madariaga*

Madariaga's short piece opens with a reported incident: while the wall went up, an East German guard escaped west because his comrades, unwilling to be photographed 'serving the servant of Moscow', turned their backs on the cameramen. He reads it as the whole operation in reverse. Khrushchev, he argues, was cornered: on the eve of sending a settler to the Moon, his German colony was emptying itself westward at a rate of one a minute, with no bread in the Eastern Zone and no movement without police scrutiny. The zone was a double shame — proof that a free Germany could become a miracle of wealth and that a communist Germany could not compensate its subjects for their lack of freedom — and, worse, evidence that the Soviet Union could not remedy it, and so that the Soviet economy itself was 'parlous'.

Madariaga then ventriloquises 'the Red Tsar''s strategy in the first person: we cannot improve the Eastern Zone, but we can stop the leakage of the young and end the scandalous contrast between the two Berlins; the Powers have not reacted to broken arrangements before, so the risk is small; and in the meantime we will distract and frighten the West with carefully contradictory statements — imperious demands under nuclear threat while insisting it is they who threaten us, a peace treaty offered and its refusal made catastrophic — so that governments turn from the real problem to the formal ones of how to avoid nuclear war and how to negotiate. He concedes the Red Tsar got away with it: the Western press largely agreed that nothing in the record obliged the Powers to prevent Ulbricht from building a wall, and no one but a madman would risk nuclear war over it. - Opens with the guard who escaped because his comrades turned their backs on Western cameras, read as a symbol of the whole operation in reverse.
- Frames the Eastern Zone as the Soviet Union's shop-window, whose poverty proved both that communism does not deliver and that Moscow could not fix it.
- Reconstructs Khrushchev's strategy in his own imagined voice: seal the leakage, end the visible contrast, exploit Western non-reaction to earlier broken arrangements.
- Argues the contradictory nuclear rhetoric was designed to shift Western attention from the real problem to the formal questions of avoiding war and negotiating.
- Concludes that the wall was not a sufficient cause for anyone but a madman to risk nuclear war, and counts it the biggest cold-war victory since Budapest 1956.

### 2) Clean Germans and Dirty Politics
*By Martin S. Dworkin*

Dworkin's essay is a study of a film convention and its politics. Six years after Nuremberg, The Desert Fox (1951) gave Rundstedt and Rommel a subtle sympathy as chivalric enemies; by 1958 Variety could headline 'FILMS' NEW NICE-NASTY NAZIS'. Dworkin's thesis is that turning old enemies into allies on screen requires a renovation of memory, and that the device chosen was to separate Germans from politics: the soldiers are decent, the politicians are to blame, and the politicians happen to be Nazis. The pattern, he notes, marks almost all American and British films of the decade but very few French and even fewer Russian ones.

He then works through the corpus. The British specialise in triumphant ingenuity and the glorified German antagonist — Rommel, Canaris, Langsdorff, Von Werra in The One That Got Away. The Americans, under State Department hints about overseas audiences and commercial pressure, go further, making good Germans resemble Americans in appearance and mannerism, from John Wayne's captain in The Sea Chase to Curt Jurgens's U-boat commander in The Enemy Below, each supplied with a fanatic Nazi political officer 'apparently carried by all German ships as standard equipment'. Loyalty is displaced onto Imperial Germany rather than onto any pre-Hitler democracy. The German girl gets the same treatment in Fraulein (1958), rehabilitated by kindly Americans against a tourist-poster Rhineland. The culmination is I Aim At the Stars (1960) on Wernher von Braun, where a single critical American character lends a false air of public debate to a film that moves 'pointedly to exculpate' its subject: the good German of the past as outside politics, the good scientist of the present as outside responsibility. Against this Dworkin sets the documentary counter-wave — Erwin Leiser's Mein Kampf, The Valiant Years, the Eichmann trial coverage — whose propaganda, he argues, had also to be counterpropaganda 'against recurrent inclinations of the victors themselves to separate the Germans from their politics'.

- Traces the 'Nice-Nasty Nazi' convention from The Desert Fox (1951) to Variety's 1958 headline and the 1961 'NEW NAZI BEAST FILM CYCLE'.
- The device is to split soldiers from politicians: decent Germans despise ideology, guilt is assigned to Nazis-as-politicians, and the question of why the war was fought disappears.
- British films glorify the chivalric German antagonist; American films Americanise the good German's appearance and manner and attach a standard-issue fanatic Nazi officer.
- Loyalty is displaced onto Imperial rather than Weimar Germany, raising 'the ghosts of the gallant Count Von Luckners and Baron Richtofens' of an earlier romanticised enmity.
- I Aim At the Stars is the culmination: it fabricates an open debate about von Braun that never happened, and argues 'for a future of little meaning' by keeping the good German outside politics and the good scientist outside responsibility.
