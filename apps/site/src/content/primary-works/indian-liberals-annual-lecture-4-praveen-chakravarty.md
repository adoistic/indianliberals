summary: |-
  Praveen Chakravarty, a Congress politician and political economist, argues that Indian liberal elites are 'confused' because they conflate liberalism, properly a philosophy of liberty and freedom from domination, with the much narrower economic debate of state versus market. He opens with a 2019 anecdote about one of India's top ten industrialists who privately raged at government policy but was too afraid to say so publicly; Chakravarty asks 'who is richer, you or me?', arguing that formal wealth without freedom of expression is a poor kind of liberty. Recounting a conversation with his mentor Manmohan Singh, he invokes Karl Popper's 'The Open Society and Its Enemies': the 1991 reforms delivered only 'formal freedoms' (removing quotas, freeing prices and trade) but not an open society, so India remains 'a closed society with some formal freedoms,' where a climate of fear suppresses investment, a thesis Singh set out in his 2019 op-ed 'The Fountainhead of India's Economic Malaise.'

  His central worked example is the GST, widely hailed as a liberal reform but which he calls deeply illiberal: it centralises taxation, stripping elected state governments of their only exclusive taxing power, leaving states with 'representation without taxation' and killing the ability of states to innovate, as Tamil Nadu did under M.G. Ramachandran to fund midday meals. He contends both wings of liberalism betray genuine liberty: right liberals reduce it to laissez-faire and would tolerate a strong state that curbs freedoms so long as the economy is private, while progressive liberals conflate it with equality and pursue 'eat the rich' wealth taxes, which he calls an attack on the freedoms of the rich; he recounts blocking a wealth-tax proposal in the 2024 Congress manifesto with P. Chidambaram, arguing you can 'make the poor richer without making the rich poorer.' Drawing on Adam Smith's 'Theory of Moral Sentiments' (to be 'loved' and 'lovely'), Isaiah Berlin's negative and positive liberty, and Mill's tyranny of the majority, he reframes the goal as justice and fairness of process rather than equality of outcome. He concludes the deficit is on the demand side: there is not enough public demand for genuine liberalism, and for both camps liberalism is merely 'wrapping paper for the chocolate' of growth or equality.
