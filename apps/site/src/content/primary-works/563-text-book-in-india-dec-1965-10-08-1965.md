  In the rendered pages, M. R. Masani argues that “nationalisation of textbooks” is a misleading label for State monopolies over textbook publication. He presents such monopolies as dangerous in a democracy because they can restrict authors, publishers, school choice, and experimentation, while creating the possibility of government-controlled indoctrination. He also traces the policy to British imperial practice, contrasting it with the later development of a mixed system in which private publishers supplied books alongside government publications.
  
  In the rendered pages, Masani supports his case with committee warnings, court observations, press commentary, and examples from several Indian states. He claims that State-produced textbooks are often poor in quality, expensive, late, unavailable, and vulnerable to profiteering and counterfeiting. The Bihar example is used to show how shortages and high prices encouraged piracy, allegedly costing the government about Rs. 35 lakhs annually. Masani’s proposed remedy is competition: State publications should compete with private publishers, while the central Education Ministry should ensure that both remain available and that minimum standards are maintained. ## Key points

- The phrase “nationalisation of textbooks” is presented as a misnomer for State monopolies or partial State control of textbook publication.
- Masani argues that a textbook monopoly is especially dangerous in a democracy because it may enable official indoctrination and restrict intellectual choice.
- He maintains that State textbook systems reproduce aspects of British imperial publishing practice rather than representing a new or progressive policy.
- Committee findings and judicial and newspaper commentary are cited as warnings against monopoly, poor quality, restricted choice, and regimentation of thought.
- The rendered pages claim that State textbooks are frequently costly, badly written, unavailable on time, and effectively financed through disguised fees or profiteering.
- Counterfeiting is attributed to high prices, shortages, weak distribution, poor-quality originals, and unfavorable terms offered to the book trade.
- Masani recommends ending the monopoly and opening textbook publication to private competition, with government oversight limited to maintaining standards and access.
