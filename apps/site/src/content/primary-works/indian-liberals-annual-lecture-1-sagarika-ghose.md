summary: |-
  In the first Indian Liberals Annual Lecture, Sagarika Ghose argues that the case for individual liberty in India has never been more urgent, warning that freedom is being eroded across the board through sedition and terror laws (UAPA), Pegasus surveillance, banned books and films, and the detention of comedians, journalists, students and activists. Her central thesis is that freedom is indivisible: liberals cannot demand economic liberty and freedom from the licence raj while ignoring social, political and cultural freedom. She criticises the manner of the three farm laws' passage, invoking Gandhian means-over-ends to argue that reforms rammed through by ordinance and voice vote lack legitimacy and that reform must persuade people rather than be imposed by state coercion. She contends that liberalism is not a Western import but has deep roots in Indian traditions of questioning, from the Upanishads and Bhagavad Gita to the bhakti poets, and that Gandhi, with his fear of state power and defence of individuality, is the foremost liberal.

  Ghose maps how a highly centralised state, aided by identity-based vigilante mobs enjoying political impunity, snatches away personal freedoms, and how government control has tamed the media into an "undeclared emergency." Arguing that statism is common to all parties (Congress introduced it, the BJP took it to maximal heights), she proposes liberal solutions: abolishing the Censor Board and the Ministry of Information and Broadcasting, keeping politics competitive to avoid one-party rule, defending minority rights (the individual being the smallest minority), decentralisation, and grounding liberalism in "barefoot" grassroots and non-violent Gandhian political action rather than the trappings of state power. She calls herself a classical liberal rather than a libertarian, judges India now an "electoral autocracy," and frames the defence of liberty as a defence of constitutional freedoms by sovereign citizens.
