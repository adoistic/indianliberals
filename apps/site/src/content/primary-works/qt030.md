# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 30 (contents page dated JULY/SEPT. 1961, cover dated 'summer 1961') is a 112-page Bombay quarterly sponsored by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, edited by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta with Nirad C. Chaudhuri and Laxmanshastri Joshi as advisory editors, Nissim Ezekiel as Reviews Editor and Laeeq Futehally as his assistant. It describes itself on the contents page as 'A Quarterly of Inquiry, Criticism and Ideas' and on the cover as 'An Adventure of Ideas', and the issue lives up to both: twenty-one printed contents entries run from a Cambridge economic historian's argument about growth and liberty, through two essays reclaiming Tamil literature from Sanskritic accounts of Indian culture, a demolition of H. G. Wells the prophet, an Acton-based attack on the ethic of self-sacrifice in Indian politics, and the issue's longest piece, a post-mortem reading of Camus's morality, to three poems, two translated short stories, a documentary quarrel with a Soviet report on a Tagore paper, a review-chronicle of a London publishing season, five book reviews and one long letter.

Its argumentative centre is the standing of the individual against collective claims, approached from several directions at once. Postan refuses the dichotomy of freedom versus growth and attacks doctrinaire laissez-faire as crudely Marxist in the same breath as he indicts Stalinist forced saving; Ruthnaswamy traces the sacrificial ethic from the Essenes to Congress ministers recruited from the class of thiagis and proposes dedication of the self to service in its place; Doubrovsky locates Camus's break with Sartre and Marxism in the refusal to sacrifice the men of today for those of tomorrow; Neela D'Souza's essay on homosexuality argues for the Wolfenden position on consenting adults in private and notes that Indian law treats homosexuality on a par with rape; Ashok N. Desai reads Chinese thought reform through Orwell's Newspeak; and Robert Conquest's letter answers a Quest reviewer with the claim that the present is real and the future hypothetical. Against that, Jesudasan and Cope are doing something different - reading literatures, Sangam Tamil and Afrikaans, as unflattering records of the societies that produced them. Beyond the printed contents the issue carries three unsigned SHORT NOTICES, an 'OUR CONTRIBUTORS' page with nine biographies, four boxed filler epigraphs, and an unusually heavy load of full-page advertising (roughly the first sixteen and last four PDF pages). It carries no editorial plate, photograph or art portfolio of any kind: every image in the issue belongs to an advertisement, which for Quest is itself a finding.

## Essays

### Freedom and Growth
*By M. M. Postan*

Postan attacks the premise that the underdeveloped world must choose between growth and freedom. He concedes at the outset that totalitarian states really do enjoy two growth advantages over liberal economies: compulsory capital formation, achieved by state-directed allocation of national income and the forcing down of popular consumption, and the power to direct factors of production to strategically chosen sectors. He is blunt that the first advantage is bought with atrocity, naming Stalin's collectivisation and the famine that followed it and the crushing of worker revolts in East Germany and Hungary. Some other advertised facilities of dictatorship, notably the power of instantaneous decision, he dismisses as illusory or worse, arguing that the freedom to act without hesitation has cost Russia and China ruinous mistakes. The West, he says, has no economic reason to fear falling behind in the growth race, only military and reputational ones; the underdeveloped countries, held to a demographic timetable, cannot afford to lose it.

His answer is to dissolve the dilemma rather than accept either horn. Compulsory saving is not inescapable: what the private capital market cannot supply, Western capital-assistance schemes can, provided they come without political strings and without early and heavy repayment terms, which makes the choice between oppressive and humane growth a decision for the Western countries rather than for the poor ones. On planning, he insists on discriminating by level and by spirit. Direction at the level of the national budget of resources impairs nobody's liberty; direction of inanimate resources carries only economic penalties; it is the lowest level, the detailed control of people's employment and consumption, that requires authoritarian power, and even there he argues the Soviet case followed from communist ideology rather than from economic necessity, and may soften as consumption grows. Wartime Britain, he notes, planned more comprehensively than wartime Russia or Germany and yet bore less crushingly on individuals, because the planners were a body politic dedicated to freedom, subject to the rule of law, and inclined to treat regulation as a regrettable necessity. The essay closes by rejecting laissez-faire whole-hoggery as a mirror image of Marxism.

- Concedes two real totalitarian growth advantages - forced capital formation and the direction of productive factors - while treating the facility for instantaneous decision as illusory.
- Names Stalinist collectivisation, the ensuing famine, and the suppression of production-norm revolts in East Germany and Hungary as the price of forced saving.
- Argues Western capital-assistance schemes, if free of political strings and early repayment, remove the economic case for saving through hunger - so the moral choice lies with the West.
- Distinguishes three levels of state direction and insists the damage depends on purpose and political temper, not extent; wartime Britain planned harder than wartime Russia yet oppressed less.
- Attacks the 'road to serfdom' position as crudely Marxist for equating economic regulation with loss of personal liberty.

### 1) What Sangam Poetry Means to India
*By Hephzibah Jesudasan*

Jesudasan argues that 'Indian culture' is cheaply used to mean Varnasrama Dharma and 'Indian literature' to mean Sanskrit and Sanskritised literature, and that Sangam poetry - a body of work from roughly the second century A.D., independent of Sanskrit vocabulary, metre and ideals - is the standing refutation. She presents it as a secular literature divided into Aham (private life) and Puram (public life), worked against conventional landscapes, and as an unusually reliable historical record, because its four hundred and fifty odd poets range from kings to Kurava women and share an unfailing regard for fact. She is impatient with Tamil zealots who would push the Sangams back a hundred centuries and grace them with gods and saints, pointing out that the poets themselves never mention a Sangam or an Academy at Madura, and that the word Sangam is not even Tamil.

The centre of the essay is a reading of Sangam society against itself. The poetry shows an advanced, war-conscious, brilliantly cosmopolitan polity with vivid city life, honest trade, monogamy, widely honoured wifehood, women with freedom and status unthinkable later, and little religious pressure - the word 'Hindu' does not occur in the Sangam vocabulary. But it also shows the beginnings of what she calls the seeds of casteism in the Dravidian grouping of society by profession: poets call the Pulayas and Pariahs degraded, and a Pulaya cot-maker is shown economically oppressed. Her conclusion is deliberately uncomfortable for Tamil polemic - that regional and hereditary professional grouping produced unhealthy loyalties and exploitation before Brahminism watered those seeds, so that blaming religion alone is wrong. She is equally severe on the fondness for toddy and for the prostitute, which she reads as notes of decadence, and on the poets' snobbery toward their patrons. On quality she rates the Sangam poets for quiet dignity, brevity and exact observation rather than for effusion, singling out Nakkirar's Nedu-nal-vadai as one of the most finished little masterpieces in the world.

- Presents Sangam literature as secular, structurally independent of Sanskrit, and priceless as a historical record because its poets kept an unfailing regard for fact.
- Rejects the traditionalist chronology and the three-Sangam legend on the ground that the poets never mention a Sangam at all.
- Locates the seeds of caste in the Dravidian occupational grouping itself, with Brahminism as a later accelerant rather than the sole cause - printed in a magazine whose readers would expect the opposite.
- Reads toddy and prostitution in the poems as evidence of moral decadence and a grave lesson, and cites a queen's pre-sati poem as an eye-opener to conservatives.
- Praises the poetry for restraint, compact imagery and observation 'not less than Wordsworthian', rating Nakkirar's Nedu-nal-vadai against anything of its length in English.

### 2) The First Three Novels in the Tamil Language
*By Ka Naa Subramanyam*

Subramanyam traces the making of Tamil prose before turning to the first three Tamil novels. He credits the Jesuit Constantius Beschi, who followed Roberto di Nobili's practice of preaching in prose, with the language's first surviving prose work, the Tales of the Guru Who was a Simpleton, and calls him the father of modern Tamil prose because he used prose for a creative purpose independent of any poetic text. Ananda Rangam Pillai's diary had no literary impact because it was not known until the end of the nineteenth century; the missionary and Education Society translations, including a Tamil Panchatantra from a Marathi version, he dismisses as pedantic exercises rather than creative work.

The novels themselves are Vedanayakam Pillai's Prathapa Mudaliyar Charitram (1879), Rajam Iyer's Kamalambal Charithram and Madhaviah's Padmavathy, the last two from the middle and later nineties. He reads the first as episodic, preachy and extremely readable, inspired by both Beschi and the Muslim Ibrahim Shah Rowther's Vikramaditya tales, modern in its ideals of womanhood and in its language reform but out of date in its statecraft, since its author preferred monarchy to a republic. He condemns as vandalism a publisher's current attempt to purge Vedanayakam Pillai's text of allegedly un-Tamil elements. Rajam Iyer, who died at twenty-six, he judges a keen and impartial observer whose novel he still rates the greatest yet produced in Tamil, though static and increasingly absorbed in Vedanta; Madhaviah he calls the novelist of social protest par excellence, better constructed and showing real development in his heroine, but with bad characters who are types rather than persons. All three, he notes, wrote to uplift the Tamil public, and all three differ radically in style - racy spoken rhythm, ornate and swift, and an attempted golden mean that lost both.

- Credits Beschi, not a Tamil, with the first creative Tamil prose work and calls him the father of modern Tamil prose.
- Separates genuinely creative prose from the missionary and Education Society translation exercises of the early nineteenth century.
- Rates Kamalambal Charithram the greatest Tamil novel yet written while conceding it is static and lacks the motivation of growth.
- Calls Madhaviah's Padmavathy the Tamil novel of social protest par excellence and the better constructed of the two later books.
- Denounces a contemporary publisher for purging Vedanayakam Pillai's prose of 'alien and un-Tamil' elements, and regrets that no effective protest has been raised.

### H. G. Wells: The Prophet and the Artist
*By Gabriel Gersh*

Gersh opens with an anecdote of Wells puncturing Galsworthy's arithmetic of animal suffering, then argues that Wells, so realistic in conversation, was himself sentimental in most of his social and economic belief. The essay is a sustained indictment of Wells the prophet: he could settle on nothing except the need for a world State, contradicted himself and anyone who agreed with him, oscillated between salvation by patient effort and salvation by magic, and produced a different vision of society and of the family in almost every book. He ran about the earth looking for leaders - Balfour, Rockefeller, George V, the Chiang Kai-sheks, both Roosevelts - and found all wanting, and died in despair in Mind at the End of Its Tether, whose forecast of imminent cosmic extinction Gersh finds simply incoherent: if the universe is about to disappear, nothing anyone does, including adopting the single transferable vote, can matter.

Gersh then grounds Wells's convictions in his biography, arguing that his father's failure as a Bromley shopkeeper and his mother's incompetence as a housekeeper and cook were generalised into a false theory of society, and that the resemblance to Dickens's life only sharpens the difference: Dickens's work is pervaded by love of mankind, while Wells was almost destitute of love, loving only Ann Pornick without reserve and treating individuals as specimens. He assembles a catalogue of failed prophecies - the submarine that could only suffocate its crew, the ineffective aeroplane and the war-winning balloon, gas rendering the tank obsolete, cities of twenty to forty million people, a Radical victory in 1939 - and argues that the ones that came true were available to any sharp-witted man who talked to inventors. His verdict is that Wells was unimportant as politician, sociologist and seer and significant only as the artist he professed to despise: a potential great comic genius who chose instead to be a minor prophet. The sociological work is already dead; the novels, from The Island of Dr. Moreau to Tono-Bungay, will live.

- Reads Wells's indecision as constitutional: a world State was the only thing he never changed his mind about.
- Finds the despair of Mind at the End of Its Tether logically incoherent, since inexorable cosmic doom makes proportional representation irrelevant.
- Derives Wells's theory of society from his father's bankruptcy and his mother's cooking, and contrasts him with Dickens on the single axis of love.
- Catalogues the failed forecasts in Anticipations and The War That Will End War, including the dismissal of submarines and aeroplanes and the faith in balloons and gas.
- Concludes that Wells threw away the gift of genius, and that his novels, not his prophecies, will survive.

### Self-Sacrifice in Politics
*By M. Ruthnaswamy*

Ruthnaswamy asks what truth there is in Acton's warning that renovating society on the principle of self-sacrifice - the aspiration of the Essenes, the early Christians, the Fathers and Canonists and Friars, Erasmus, More and Fenelon - had become 'the most dangerous enemy lurking in our path'. He begins by explaining why the idea is native to India: sacrifice, first of things and then of the self, was practised from Dravidian times as a way of acquiring power, and the social and political authority of the Brahmin rested on self-denial, so that among high and low it was deep-seated that men who led lives of self-denial must have power and influence. He then works through Acton's list, qualifying it as he goes - the Essenes disappeared because they could not live like men in a world of men; monastic life sacrificed the individual to the community; Canon Law made sacrifice of self to the Church its informing principle; More recommended total submission in the Utopia but vindicated the rights of human personality on the scaffold; Fenelon's fault was relying on the consciences of kings and citizens instead of on the division and limitation of power in representative assemblies, independent judiciaries and local self-government.

The second half brings the argument to India. Ruthnaswamy is careful to say that Gandhi's Satyagraha is a far cry from the French Revolution and from the Nazi, Fascist and Communist nightmare, but insists the principle is the same, and that Gandhi's own humility, compassion and sense of humour were what kept the doctrine from being applied au pied de la lettre. Even in his lifetime, he says, the military discipline imposed on Congressmen, the treatment of nonconformists like Subhas Chandra Bose and Neriman, khadi, prohibition, hartal, picketing, social boycott and the moral coercion of the fast unto death were already challenging individual liberty; after his death the Congress still heeds threats of starvation unto death, as with Potti Sriramalu, and still recruits ministers from the class of thiagis with little regard for intellect or character. He then lists the liberty-reducing consequences of the developmental state - controls, permits, licences and quotas, education becoming a state monopoly with nationalised textbooks, private enterprise allowed only 45 per cent of new industries, prohibition, population control by administrative order, cooperative farming and land ceilings - and says that while the logical result has not yet reached Mussolini's maxim, India is on the road. His remedy is the opposite of the doctrine: dedication of the self to service rather than sacrifice of the self, and the intensification rather than the stemming of individualism, on the ground that it is self-reliant individuals who make institutions work, which is why cooperation succeeded in England, Germany and Denmark and relatively failed in India.

- Frames the whole essay on Acton's lecture on the history of freedom in Christianity and on Acton's record of vindicated predictions, including that power tends to corrupt.
- Explains the Indian purchase of the idea historically: sacrifice as a technique for acquiring power, and Brahmin authority resting on self-denial.
- Credits Gandhi's personal temperament, not the doctrine, with restraining Satyagraha, while listing the coercions of khadi, prohibition, hartal, social boycott and the fast unto death.
- Reads planning, controls, licences, textbook nationalisation and the 45 per cent cap on private new industry as calls for sacrifice of the self in the name of economic progress.
- Proposes dedication to service rather than sacrifice of self, and argues the cooperative movement failed in India for want of self-reliant individuals dominated instead by community and caste spirit.

### The Morality of Albert Camus
*By Serge Doubrovsky*

Doubrovsky's long essay, written after Camus's death, sets out to ask what moral principles Camus actually held. He clears the ground first by rejecting two labels: 'existentialist', which he calls a bugbear for imbeciles and merely an error of classification, and the more damaging picture of Camus the Just, the austere preacher, the school teacher of civics. His central claim is that Camus has no system: justice, happiness and revolt recur endlessly without ever being defined, and what the work yields is not rules for life but a human attitude, an existential tension, a passion for living. Camus, he says, is above all a poet who travesties his own intuitions whenever he presents them in the guise of reasoning.

The positive account is built on the sun. Where the sun is absent from Sartre's universe and plays a negative role in La Nausee, and where for Valery it is a self-contained mode of spherical existence, for Camus it is the vital element itself and the perfect symbol of the link between the natural and the human - so the fundamental category of his ontology is participation. Doubrovsky contrasts the Camusian body, in which muscular indolence and physical harmony reign and physical love is unison, with the Sartrean body that philosophy has cut in half and cannot reassemble, and reads Meursault swimming with Marie, and Rieux and Tarrou swimming to the same rhythm in La Peste, as the same truth recovered on contact with the water. Death, on this reading, does not make life absurd but gives it its full meaning. He then eliminates heroism, which he calls a retreat into oneself and an attempt to be God while still being oneself, and hedonism, contrasting Camus's poverty of natural imagery and singular world with Gide's plural fruits of the earth and category of having. The final movement is the dispute with existentialism over being and doing: Sartre's morality of historic action, oriented wholly to the future, opens the door to sacrificing the men of today for those of tomorrow, and every word of Camus's morality is aimed at slamming that door and rehabilitating the inalienable value of the present. Camus, he concludes, never denied that violence might be a weapon but always refused to grant it be a policy - a simple distinction that for millions of human beings is a vital one - and his work is less a system of morality than a preface for all future morality.

- Refuses both the 'existentialist' label and the moralising-saint portrait, and argues Camus never subjects justice, happiness or revolt to a vertical analysis.
- Makes the sun the key to Camus's ontology: participation, in explicit opposition to Sartrean nausea and to Valery's self-contained sun.
- Reads swimming scenes in L'Etranger and La Peste as the recovery of vital communion, and death as what gives life its full meaning rather than what makes it absurd.
- Eliminates heroism and hedonism as ontological rather than moral errors - heroism closes the circle, Gide's plural world belongs to the category of having.
- Locates the break with Sartre and Marxism in the refusal to sacrifice the present to the future, and in the distinction between violence as weapon and violence as policy.

### One Among Many-the Homosexual
*By Neela D'Souza*

D'Souza opens with Kinsey's proportion - one man in twenty-five - and extrapolates it to sixteen million homosexual men in India, four or five cities the size of Bombay, so that nearly all of us have one or more homosexuals among our acquaintances. She then surveys what was then known about causation without committing to any of it: the psychoanalytic claim that everyone passes through a homosexual phase, Donald West's view that homosexual tendencies are part of the natural biological make-up and that preference for the opposite sex is acquired, Clifford Allen's insistence that an abnormal childhood is the one sure fact, the pattern of dominant possessive mothers, and the situational factors of segregation in boarding schools, purdah societies and the South African mine compounds. She explicitly refuses the words 'normal' and 'abnormal' because of the approval and disapproval they carry, calling homosexuality a deviation from the usual, and she dismisses the seduction theory with an analogy: it is as valid as pleading that one fell into theft from having one's school books stolen.

The historical section answers whether homosexuality is a symptom of modern decadence by showing it is as old as mankind, running from Sodom and Gomorrah through New Guinea and Australian initiation, the berdaches and alyhas of American Indian tribes, African initiation and Basuto 'circumcision schools', the contempt of the Trobriand Islanders, the loss of caste and whipping and mutilation prescribed in ancient India, Incan vengeance on whole families, the idealisation of the passion in classical Greece and its degeneration into a luxurious vice in Rome, and the twenty thousand people known to the London police in the 1890s. The rest of the essay is an argument for tolerance and for law reform, built on the persecution of Radclyffe Hall's The Well of Loneliness and the destruction of Oscar Wilde, on the evidence that homosexuals are no more prone than heterosexuals to molest children, and above all on the Wolfenden Committee's recommendation that homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private be placed outside the sphere of the law. She notes the Committee's support from the Archbishops of Canterbury and York, church groups, much of the press and the Oxford and Cambridge Unions, records that Indian law treats homosexuality on a par with rape, argues that talk of 'cure' wrongly assumes a disease and compares it to forcing left-handed children to write with the right hand, and closes on the treatment of the awkward minority group as the yardstick of a civilised society.

- Opens with Kinsey's one-in-twenty-five figure and its extrapolation to sixteen million men in India, in order to make the subject unavoidable rather than exotic.
- Deliberately refuses the vocabulary of normal and abnormal, and rejects the seduction theory of causation with a theft analogy.
- Uses anthropology and history - Greece, Rome, New Guinea, the Basutos, ancient Indian penalties, Victorian London - to dismiss the claim that homosexuality is modern decadence.
- Argues for the Wolfenden recommendation on consenting adults in private, noting Indian law treats homosexuality on a par with rape while Italy and France do not punish it as such.
- Treats 'cure' as a category error, comparing it to making all left-handed people right-handed, and notes the homosexual may not want to be cured.

### A Quick Look at South African Literature
*By Jack Cope*

Written fifty years after the Union of South Africa was established, Cope's survey argues that the least expected outcome of 1910 has been the rise of an autonomous and often mature literature. He notes that creative South African writing has been almost entirely in Afrikaans and English, that at least eight other languages are spoken and written in the country, and that it is too early to speak of their literature in the larger sense - the African languages having hardly evolved beyond classroom and church, and most younger Indians having adopted English or Afrikaans. Afrikaans literature, he says, has followed the destiny of the Afrikaner nationality through defeat, subjection, liberation and triumph: despised and unrecognised as a language fifty years earlier, it found its strength in the Second Language Movement after the Boer War, and the 1905 publication of 'Winternag' by Eugene Marais marks the dawn of a new era. The early poets - Marais, Leipoldt, Jan Celliers, Totius - gave a defeated nationality the identity their conquerors denied them, and Cope is careful to add that except for the ironical fact that their skins were white, the Afrikaners' position was not much better than that of other groups under the Pax Britannica.

The critical burden of the essay falls on realistic prose, which he says is hardly at its beginning even in 1960, and on why. His explanation is social: the transition from farm to city is only partly accomplished, the cities remain alien and dominated by British, Jewish and Indian commercial interests, Afrikaans suburbs are parochial, and the demand for books comes largely through schools and the politically aligned Press, so a critical social novel has little chance. The consequences he calls baffling - the educated turn to books in English, with library surveys showing up to ninety-five per cent of Afrikaans readers in typical country areas taking out English books, while a trade in salacious and semi-pornographic magazines flourishes; and a censorship Bill is before Parliament so drastic that any other system east or west of the Iron Curtain seems mild in comparison. He quotes the publisher J. Human that few Afrikaners read Afrikaans books because the quality does not satisfy them, and Alan Paton that Afrikaans fiction is an infant that never grew up, that the policy of the country's rulers is killing the novel, and that fear of hostile social criticism may be as inhibitory as fear of hostile literary criticism. Poetry, by contrast, he finds technically advanced and still widely read and loved - he works through Uys Krige, the first important writer to reject the national myths, Van Wyk Louw with his profound and ignored pessimism, the academic Opperman, Elizabeth Eybers, Olga Kirsch (discouraged into silence and now living in Israel) and the coloured poets S. V. Petersen and P. J. Philander - quoting several poems in Afrikaans with his own English renderings.

- Treats Afrikaans literature as the literature of a defeated nationality that found in poetry the identity its conquerors denied it.
- Argues realistic prose has lagged because the Afrikaner move to the cities is incomplete and the book market runs through schools and a politically aligned press.
- Reports library evidence that up to ninety-five per cent of Afrikaans readers in country areas borrow English books, alongside a boom in semi-pornographic magazines.
- Warns that a censorship Bill before Parliament would be more drastic than any system east or west of the Iron Curtain.
- Finds the rejection of Afrikaner national myth mainly in poetry - Krige, Van Wyk Louw, Opperman - and in Jan Rabie's 'Ons die Afgod', and quotes the verse bilingually.

*By Santosh Kumar Ghosh*

### "Soviet Deportation of Nationalities"

Conquest writes to answer Siddhartha Sen's Quest review of his book The Soviet Deportation of Nationalities, and treats the disagreement as revealing an important division in the world about the standards to be adopted on moral and political issues.
