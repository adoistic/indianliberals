## Summary

In the rendered pages, Homer A. Jack presents a critical memorandum on India's Emergency, proclaimed on 25–26 June 1975 under Prime Minister Indira Gandhi. The opening sections place the Emergency in the context of post-independence political history, economic stagnation, corruption, the Allahabad High Court judgment against Gandhi's 1971 election, and the government's subsequent constitutional and parliamentary measures. Jack argues that India is moving behind a “Khadi (homespun) Curtain”: censorship, preventive detention, bans on organisations, and restrictions on judicial review have narrowed the space for democratic accountability, even though some freedom remains between the official strands of repression and the censored press.

The report also examines the government's 20-Point Program and the claimed early benefits of the Emergency, including improved punctuality and discipline, anti-corruption measures, action against smuggling and tax evasion, reduced inflation, and lower prices. Jack distinguishes these reported gains from the Emergency's negative effects, which, in the rendered pages, include legal erosion, political imprisonment, press censorship, fear and harassment, restrictions on assembly, weakened Parliament, uncertainty over elections, and curbs on trade unions. The final rendered pages turn to opposition activity, especially Jayaprakash Narayan's leadership, the detention of opposition figures, non-violent satyagraha, and the emergence of regional and underground resistance. The report is dedicated to Narayan and repeatedly contrasts the government's claims of restored order with the suppression of civil and political rights.

## Key points

- The report situates the 1975 Emergency within India's political history and the crisis surrounding Indira Gandhi's election judgment.
- It describes constitutional amendments, ordinances, preventive detention, bans on organisations, and the suspension of access to fundamental-rights courts.
- It records the government's economic and administrative claims under the 20-Point Program, including lower inflation, anti-corruption action, and increased discipline.
- It argues that these claimed gains coexist with censorship, political imprisonment, intimidation, restrictions on assembly, and weakened parliamentary scrutiny.
- It discusses the effects of the Emergency on elections, Parliament, trade unions, and the press.
- It presents Jayaprakash Narayan, opposition parties, Sarvodaya workers, and satyagraha campaigns as the principal sources of active or potential resistance.
- It treats the Emergency as a constitutional and political threat while acknowledging that India had not, in the pages seen, yet become a fully fledged police or fascist state.
