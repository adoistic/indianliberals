# Quest

## Summary

Quest No. 35 (cover 'autumn 1962'; contents page 'OCT./DEC. 1962') is a 120-page Bombay quarterly sponsored by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, edited by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta with Nirad C. Chaudhuri and Laxmanshastri Joshi as advisory editors, Nissim Ezekiel as Reviews Editor and Mrs. Laeeq Futehally as his assistant. The issue's argumentative centre is the university and the conditions of free intellectual work. Co-editor Amlan Datta opens with a case for retaining English as the common academic language of Indian universities against a switch to Hindi or the regional languages; Troy Wilson Organ, an Ohio philosopher recently at Santiniketan, argues that religion can only be liberal if it adopts the self-correcting method of science; Kastur Chand Lalwani turns a year of American observation into an indictment of lay government, loyalty oaths and 'sponsored research', which the editors append a signed rejoinder to; Pranab Bardhan asks whether post-war American capitalism has changed in kind or only in degree; and N. K. Devaraja's Rajasthan Philosophy Conference presidential address asks Indian philosophers to stop choosing between Sanskritist self-congratulation and imported linguistic analysis. Amit Kumar Nag supplies a long sociological account of the Nagas and the 'civilizing agencies' that unsettled them, and Mirza Nasir Ali a critical portrait of the Urdu poet Josh Maleehabadi.

The second half of the issue is literary and critical. Tibor Meray, the exiled Hungarian journalist, remembers the Korean poet and Shakespeare translator Chung-sik Sul, whom he watched being tried and executed as an 'American spy'; four poems by Adil Jussawala, Pradip Sen and G. C. S. Marar precede Buddhadeva Bose's long Bengali story 'We Three' in Jyotirmoy Datta's translation. An ARTS section stages a two-sided controversy over Satyajit Ray's Kanchanjangha - flagged on the cover as 'CONTROVERSY ON "KANCHANJANGHA"' - with Jyotirmoy Datta defending the film as a mirror Indian audiences refuse to look into and P. Lal reading it, more sceptically, as India's first 'anti-film'; four unnumbered plate pages of film stills, credited to Miss Marie Seton and listed nowhere in the contents, sit between them. Ten signed book reviews and four unsigned-on-the-contents-page SHORT NOTICES by Laeeq Futehally close the issue, ranging over Tutuola, Pasternak, Romila Thapar's Asoka, the Cold War, ex-Nazis in Bonn and Aldous Huxley, followed by a single letter from H. R. Pasricha continuing a quarrel from the Monsoon number.

## Essays

### A Common Language for Indian Universities
*By Amlan Datta*

Amlan Datta argues that Indian universities are dangerously isolated from one another and that adopting the regional language as the sole medium of higher education would complete their separation. He wants permanent residential posts for guest professors, systematic exchange of teachers between universities, and above all a single common academic language. Conceding that the mother tongue cannot be replaced for spontaneity of thought at lower levels, he insists the common binding language of higher education must be either English or Hindi, and that it should remain English: more books, more business and more international traffic pass through it, Bengali or Marathi or Tamil has more to learn from English than from Hindi, and translation introduces a time lag that instantaneous national debate cannot afford.

His case is explicitly political rather than merely pedagogic. Academic Balkanisation, he writes, would strengthen cultural and ultimately political fragmentation at a moment when India has 'formidable antagonists across our borders'. He anticipates the objection that one need not care about national integration and answers it from the European example, from the needs of development planning and from the constitution's silence on secession. He also answers the charge that English held back the regional languages: contact with English enriched them, and the gulf between Bharatchandra and Rabindranath is inexplicable except through the widening of horizons. His sharpest warning is reserved for Hindi: favoured by political patronage and close kin to the other Indian languages, it could displace them at home in a way English never did, reducing them to 'dialects spoken by women at home' and provoking resistance led by second-rank leaders.

- All-India contact between universities amounts to little more than annual Christmas conferences and occasional memorial lectures; Datta wants guest-professor residencies and planned teacher exchange instead.
- Regional language as sole medium of higher education would 'Balkanise' the country academically and feed political fragmentation.
- The mother tongue must be retained and enriched, but higher education needs a second, common medium; the real choice is only between English and Hindi.
- He chooses English on practical grounds - volume of books, international intercourse, and the fact that Indian languages learn more from English than from each other.
- A proposed enforcement mechanism: after a trial period, students might be required to answer at least one-third of examination questions in English or the mother tongue.
- Hindi, favoured with political patronage and closely akin to other Indian languages, is the greater threat to the regional languages than English ever was.

### Liberalism in Religion
*By Troy Wilson Organ*

Troy Wilson Organ separates two things usually confused: liberalism as a way of holding beliefs and radicalism or conservatism as the content of belief. Crossing 'methodological' against 'terminal' liberalism he generates eight positions, then uses the distinction historically. Post-Reformation Christianity, he argues, allowed methodological liberalism - reason, revelation, mysticism, shared experience - while forbidding any conclusion but the orthodox one, which is no liberalism at all: 'To tell a man that he is free to think so long as he arrives at a specified conclusion is to mock him.' Modern science did the reverse, permitting any conclusion while insisting on one method, and its authority rests not on deduction or sense experience or usefulness but on intersubjective verifiability - the standing invitation to repeat the experiment.

He then sets five principles for a religion that would be liberal in the scientific way: no knowledge is certain; no method other than the scientific method of knowing is possible; the method implies no metaphysics; nothing contradicting an empirically verifiable proposition may be supported; and no belief may be held that is not pragmatically justified. The consequences are drawn deliberately: the liberal religionist will not pray for rain, but may believe in a deity, because absence of proof is not proof of the alternative, and may hold beliefs about survival after death on pragmatic grounds - while stopping short of inventing heavens of bliss. The closing section compares prospects in the two countries he knows. America, he says, is in retreat - fundamentalist sects, Billy Graham, neo-orthodoxy, the John Birch Society on campus, the middle-aged professor as the only liberal in the room - whereas India, standing 'with one foot in the Stone Age and one foot in the Atomic Age', may yet produce a liberalism in religion unlike the Western kind.

- Liberal/dogmatic describes how beliefs are held; radical/conservative describes what they are about - crossing these with methodological/terminal yields eight positions.
- Western religion since the sixteenth century granted method but denied conclusions; science granted conclusions but fixed the method.
- Science's warrant is intersubjective verifiability, not deduction, sense experience or pragmatic fruitfulness.
- Five principles are laid down for a cognitively serious liberal religion, including that no belief contradicting an empirically verifiable proposition can be held.
- Absence of scientific proof of deity is not proof of non-existence, so belief in deity remains open to the liberal religionist.
- Organ reads mid-century America as growing illiberal and thinks India, with its tradition of tolerance, the more likely site of methodological liberalism in religion.

### Academic Freedom
*By Kastur Chand Lalwani*

Kastur Chand Lalwani, a teacher formed in the British academic tradition, opens by doubting that Indian teachers and administrators enjoy the freedom of thought they had before 1947, and says that if so 'our intellectuals have paid a heavy price to obtain the country's liberty'. Wanting to know which model post-1947 Indian educational reconstruction was copying, he rules out the West European and the Russian and concludes India is going 'the American way' - so he spends his study leave in the United States reading the literature of American academic freedom. Ray Ginger's account of the Scopes 'monkey trial' leads him back to Henry Dunster's forced resignation from Harvard in 1654, through eighteenth-century religious conformity, the post-Darwin Fundamentalist campaigns, and then to the twentieth-century displacement of the threat from the church to 'big business' and private fortune, documented from the 1915 Report on Academic Freedom, Veblen, Hofstadter and Metzger.

The substantive charge is that American universities are governed by laymen - trustees who in law are the university and who hire and fire - with the president as a businessman in academic garb presiding over 'a planetarium of deans'. He piles on the machinery of nervousness: the Feinberg law, the loyalty oath, the janitor Hjalmar Anderson's refusal to sign one, the House Committee on un-American activities, a poll in which 53 per cent of Los Angeles social-science teachers felt less free than five years before and over 20 per cent thought it dangerous to discuss the Bill of Rights. His final target is 'sponsored research', which he thinks narrows the horizon of knowledge and subordinates universities to money-earning, quoting Whyte, Gengerelli and Norbert Wiener. Throughout he draws the Indian parallel - the UGC-chaired committee drafting a Universities Act for the older universities, victimisation of Bengal teachers on secret police reports, the flight of talent from teaching - and ends by contrasting India's 'vogue of a big laboratory', built with imported money and technology, with the stick-and-string improvisation of the Bose-Ray-Raman generation.

- Lalwani's premise: freedom of thought for Indian teachers and administrators may be narrower after 1947 than before.
- He identifies the American, not the British or Soviet, model as the template for post-Independence Indian university administration.
- The American history he assembles moves the chief menace to academic freedom from ecclesiastical control to business-dominated lay government.
- Loyalty oaths, the un-American activities committee and 'suspect' proscriptions are treated as a measurable chill, with survey data on teachers' self-censorship.
- 'Sponsored research' is his central worry: foundation and government money channels enquiry towards organisation-favoured projects and away from individual work.
- The Indian application is explicit - a drafted Universities Act, police-report victimisation in West Bengal, and big laboratories built on imported money in a bureaucratic setting.
- The editors print a rejoinder at the foot of p.27 noting that Lalwani's criticism of America rests almost wholly on American sources.

### Capitalism Today
*By Pranab Bardhan*

Pranab Bardhan takes the American economy as capitalism 'par excellence' and asks whether the changes of the previous few decades amount to an epochal transformation. He works through the components of effective demand. Personal consumption has fallen as a share of demand but shifted decisively to durables - 'Not tea, but TV' - and consumer asset formation, financed by instalment credit that rose three times as fast as personal income in the fifties, is widely read as a stabiliser; Bardhan doubts it, noting consumer inventory cycles and an insurance survey finding the average American family three months from bankruptcy. Business spending is steadier but its technological dynamism need not generate investment, since innovations that cut depreciation lower capital requirements and amortisation reserves let big corporations innovate at a low rate of net investment. Wasteful expenditure - 551 brands of coffee, tail fins, twelve billion dollars of advertising - props up demand at the cost of productivity. Public expenditure, above a quarter of national output, is dominated by defence, and he cites the estimate that one heavy bomber equals a modern brick school in more than thirty cities.

The second half turns to politics and social relations. Against the Marxist claim that a capitalist state cannot regulate capital, he substitutes a 'multiple pressure-group polity' in which organised labour, farmers and consumers also count - no American government will enter private housing, but none can impose draconic wage cuts either. Against the 'income revolution' he sets Sweezy on corporate savings and unrealised capital gains, and Denison on the constancy of labour's functional share. He accepts the atrophy of competition and the oligopolisation of society, rejects Burnham's managerial revolution on the empirical evidence, and endorses C. Wright Mills's power elite. His conclusion is that what is central to capitalism is not the method of surplus appropriation but the role of the individual, and that de-individualisation through giant organisation is common to capitalist and communist societies alike.

- Post-war American prosperity is steadier but not faster than the 1918-29 record: 2.2% average annual real GNP growth 1945-56 against 2.9% in 1918-29.
- Consumer durables and instalment credit are read by others as stabilisers; Bardhan treats debt-financed consumption as potentially as volatile as debt-financed investment.
- Advertising and planned obsolescence are structural, not incidental: about 12 billion dollars by the end of the fifties, over 15 billion with related activities.
- The Marxist theory of the capitalist state is replaced by a multiple pressure-group polity that constrains capital as well as labour.
- Claims of an 'income revolution' are discounted by corporate savings, capital gains and expense accounts, and by the constancy of labour's functional share.
- Burnham's managerial revolution is rejected empirically; Mills's tightly-knit 'power elite' is accepted.
- The real transformation is the disappearance of the inner-directed entrepreneur - and that de-individualisation is shared by communist societies, so it cannot be charged to capitalism alone.

### The Tasks before Indian Philosophers
*By N. K. Devaraja*

N. K. Devaraja's presidential address to the Rajasthan Philosophy Conference of January 1962 describes philosophy as facing an unprecedented crisis in both subject matter and method: the demand for evidence has unsettled traditional philosophy and linguistic analysis threatens to cut it off from the questions that gave it its aura. In India the positivist challenge has produced a stand-off. At one end are scholars who rehearse the glories of the ancient Rishis; at the other, admirers of Wittgenstein, Carnap and perhaps Sartre who will not tolerate the metaphysics of the Mahayanists and Vedantists. Devaraja rejects both. The traditionalists fail to meet a demand for evidence that he takes as a sign of intellectual maturity and ignore values made possible by science and by the new volume of cultural traffic; the analysts have merely exchanged one inferiority complex for another, producing expository papers or pale imitations of Western discussion where the previous generation at least had the patriotic mission of interpreting ancient wisdom.

He then names the historic imbalance he wants corrected. Ancient India produced the Astadhyayi, the Arthasastra and the Kamasutra and scaled spiritual heights, but none of its great philosophers took an interest in mathematics or the sciences of his time, and none produced a major treatise on ethics, socio-political philosophy or aesthetics; measured against Plato and Aristotle or Kant and Hegel, even Nagarjuna, Dharmakirti, Sankara and Ramanuja look partial. He blames two factors: the repetitive character of Indian socio-political and economic experience, and the preoccupation of the best minds with the transcendent, which left moral and political norms to dogmatic law-givers like Manu and Yajnavalkya. Since the old dogmas underwrite the four varnas and four thousand castes as much as the values we want to keep, they cannot be appealed to now. The constructive task he sets is a philosophy of values equal to the new experience, and he offers Gandhi - punctual, exact in his accounts, scientific in his passion for experiment, morally indignant as Socrates, spiritually ardent as Tukaram - as the phenomenon Indian philosophers of value ought to be analysing.

- Delivered as a presidential address at the Rajasthan Philosophy Conference, January 1962 (footnoted on the first page).
- Indian philosophy is split between traditionalist exhortation and imitative analytic philosophy; Devaraja rejects both as failing the new nation's intellectual needs.
- The positivist demand for evidence is welcomed as a sign of maturity; the positivist restriction of philosophy to clarifying scientific statements is not.
- Philosophy's proper task is analysing and appraising the value-bearing consciousness of man, not promoting scientific research.
- Ancient Indian philosophy is faulted for producing no major work on ethics, socio-political philosophy or aesthetics, and for ignoring mathematics and the sciences.
- Older dogmas cannot ground today's values because they equally support the caste order we wish to demolish.
- Gandhi is proposed as the model subject for a new Indian philosophy of moral and religious values.

### The Naga Sociological Background and the Impact of Civilization
*By Amit Kumar Nag*

Amit Kumar Nag, a journalist specialising in Assam tribal life, surveys Naga society and then the agencies that changed it, reaching finally for an explanation of what he calls the Naga impasse. The first half is descriptive: some forty tribes on the Indian side and four principal ones in Burma, sharing broad cultural similarity but differing in dialect, custom and religious practice; contested and probably diverse origins, traced by J. H. Hutton's collection of migration legends to almost every point of the compass; the early notices in Ptolemy, the Moghul chronicler Saiab-ud-Din Talish and Tavernier; political forms ranging from hereditary feudal chieftainship among the Semas to 'extreme democracy' among the Angami and Lhota; the morung or bachelors' dormitory as the central disciplinary and educational institution; headhunting explained by the belief that soul-substance can be transferred; the two forms of marriage, formal and informal, with no stigma attaching to either; and a qualified account of women's freedom before marriage and hard work after it.

The second half is a list of 'factors of change' - government, Christian missions, modern education, medicine, the two World Wars, and other contacts. Suppression of headhunting made cultivation safe but removed the discipline that filled the morung; missions abolished rather than remodelled the Feasts of Merit and the sacrificial feasts and weakened the system of social control; mission schooling is judged, on J. P. Mills's 1931 Census testimony, 'an evil rather than a good' where it taught boys to expect a government 'sitting-and-eating' job; medicine displaced the medicine-man; the Second World War threw the Nagas into world politics, brought Japanese occupation of some nine hundred square miles and, with the INA's victories over better-equipped white troops, destroyed the notion of Anglo-Saxon invincibility. Nag closes with Toynbee's 'consciousness of being disinherited from one's ancestral place in society', the collapse of the old balance between reality culture and value culture, the decline of wood-carving and song, and an insistence that the Nagaland problem is neither headhunters' revenge nor one adventurer's day-dream but a malaise that needs tackling at its own depth.

- About forty Naga tribes on the Indian side, with wide internal variation in language, custom and polity, and no single origin.
- The morung (bachelors' dormitory) is treated as the pivotal institution of training, discipline and defence; its decay is the recurring index of social change.
- Headhunting is explained as a fertility practice grounded in beliefs about transferable soul-substance, and was still practised in unadministered areas.
- Six agencies of change are itemised: government, Christian missions, modern education, medicine, the two World Wars, and non-tribal penetration of the hills.
- Missions are charged with abolishing rather than adapting valuable customs and with destroying a working system of social control.
- Colonial-era testimony is quoted at length, including a Deputy Commissioner's plan to suppress girls' sleeping houses by taxing them as dwellings.
- The Second World War, Japanese occupation and the Indian National Army are given decisive weight in politicising the Nagas.
- The conclusion refuses both the 'revenge of headhunters' and the 'single adventurer' explanations of the Naga conflict.

### Josh Maleehabadi
*By Mirza Nasir Ali*

Mirza Nasir Ali gives a four-part critical biography of the Urdu poet Shabbir Hasan Josh Maleehabadi, born 1898 near Lucknow into a landed Afghan family of poets. Part I is life: tutors including Mirza Mohummad Hadi Ruswa instead of school, an unfinished Cambridge School Certificate, six months at Santiniketan, then wide private reading in Dante, Shakespeare, Milton, the Romantics, Goethe and Tolstoy alongside Hafiz, Khayyam, Sadi, Ghalib, Kalidas, Tulsidas, Kabir and Tagore; a father who disinherited him for wanting to be a poet and then relented; ten years of literary office in Hyderabad ending in dismissal, officially for a slander on the Nizam; the journal 'Kaleem' in Delhi, the government monthly 'Aajkal' from 1948, and removal to Karachi in 1958 to supervise an Urdu encyclopaedia. Ali is candid about the man - unkeepable appointments, practical jokes, bawdy talk in religious gatherings, letters printable only after expurgation - and equally about his generosity.

Part II traces the poetry's movement from conventional ghazal love-lyric to the nazm, which permits sequence and development, and explains the ghazal form for readers without a European analogue, offering Pope's couplets and Mir as the nearest comparison. Josh's turn to Hindu-Muslim unity, anti-imperialism and social inequality is placed in the nationalist twenties and thirties, with the judgment that like Shelley his fervour stayed emotional: without the constructive faculty of a political philosopher he could not trace a course of action. Ali reads him as a sceptic in polemic and a believer in poetry, a pyrrhonist who still venerates the selfless of every religion, and notes bluntly that 'Josh is least revolutionary of all when it comes to women'. Parts III and IV praise his command of words and his quatrains while cataloguing the defects: unequal and sometimes jejune verse, didacticism, and a stock imagery of rose-gardens, nightingales and cup-bearers that makes him read like the catalogue-poetry of Kipling and Osbert Sitwell. The defence is that Urdu poetry is not to be judged by Western canons, and that Josh's simplicity - no symbolism, no imagism, no surrealism - is closer to Milton's sine qua non than to a defect.

- Josh (Shabbir Hasan, b. 1898, Maleehabad) is placed in a landed Afghan family with a three-generation poetic tradition; his output is claimed at over two hundred thousand lines.
- The Tagore parallel is used four times over - birth, family literary tradition, landed aristocracy, prolificacy - and later inverted when Josh pities Tagore for being born among people who did not understand him.
- His career turns on the ten Hyderabad years (1925-34), which politicised his verse and ended in dismissal.
- The technical heart of the essay is the shift from ghazal to nazm, with an extended explanation of the ghazal's non-sequential unity for readers who have no European equivalent.
- Ali judges the revolutionary fervour genuine but unconstructive - emotional like Shelley's, with no remedy proposed.
- Josh's religious position is read as scepticism in argument and belief in poetic practice, with Voltairean derision reserved for priests.
- The essay states without dissent that Josh confines women to mistress, housewife or mother.
- Faults named: unequal verse, didacticism, stock phraseology, weak structural unity - outweighed, Ali says, by an indefinable appeal.

### Remembering a Poet
*By Tibor Meray*

Tibor Meray's memoir opens on the night of 22 August 1951 at Kaesong, where explosions had interrupted the Korean armistice negotiations and the Korean-Chinese and American liaison officers were disputing whether the town had been bombed. Standing beside Colonel Chang, Meray was struck by the interpreter's voice rendering the Colonel's fury into English - 'If you are a true military man and a man of conscience, you cannot deny these adamantine, rocklike proofs' - and only later learned that the major was also the Korean translator of Shakespeare and one of his country's foremost poets. The two became friends over rice-wine and arguments about the Macbeth text and whether 'Ode to the West Wind' can be translated. When Chung-sik Sul wrote an epic poem thanking the Hungarian-donated hospital that had treated his heart, Meray had it translated with the help of Wilfred Burchett and Alan Winnington and published in Budapest, and took down Sul's life story to introduce him: born 1912 at Tan Chun, expelled from school in Seoul in 1929 for the Kwang-ju strike, studies at Mukden and Peking, a degree at a Protestant university, Mount Union College in Ohio and Columbia, work for the US Military Government's Public Information Department, then the underground Communist Party out of disappointment with the Americans and their support for Syngman Rhee.

The second half is the betrayal. When Meray's Korea book was to appear, the Korean Embassy supplied a list of names to be deleted and Sul's was among them; no explanation was given. Returning for the armistice signing in 1953, he found Sul gone from the delegation and then saw him in a Pyungyang courtroom as the fourteenth accused in a show trial of 'American spies and conspirators', charged with concealing his US Military Government employment - an accusation Meray thought proved his innocence, since the fact was too widely known to conceal. Meray, still a believer, searched his memory for something suspicious and found only a look in Sul's eyes when told that Korean suffering was defending world peace: 'when the fighting parties are great powers ... why do they have to shed in their conflict the blood of my small people?' Sul was executed. After the 1954 rehabilitations Meray proposed to Burchett that they petition Kim Il Song for Sul's rehabilitation; Burchett disagreed and nothing came of it. The piece ends as an act of duty to the memory of a poet and a friend.

- A first-hand account by a Hungarian Communist Party correspondent of the Kaesong armistice talks and of the poet-interpreter Chung-sik Sul.
- Sul's biography is given in his own words: South Korean birth, expulsion for anti-Japanese student action, American university education, employment by the US Military Government, then the underground Communist Party.
- He translated Hamlet, Romeo and Juliet and Macbeth into Korean; the Macbeth translation remained in manuscript.
- Censorship precedes the trial: the Korean Embassy has Sul's name struck from Meray's Hungarian book with no reason given.
- The 1953 Pyungyang trial is read as the local application of the Rajk-type purge - liquidation of 'native' communists by the Moscow-trained group.
- Meray argues the concealment charge is self-refuting because Sul's US employment was common knowledge.
- The only 'crime' he can find is Sul's unspoken question about why a small people should bleed for great powers' conflicts.
- A written appeal to Kim Il Song for posthumous rehabilitation was abandoned when Wilfred Burchett refused to join it.

### Memory
*By Adil Jussawala*

Three seven-line stanzas built on a single figure: memory as a starved crow beating its horned beak against a stone weathered smooth by wind, frost and primeval lava, glancing sunlight like a mirror. - A single extended metaphor: memory as a starved crow working at an unyielding, weather-smoothed stone.

### Come with me to the water's edge
*By Pradip Sen*

### Dusk
*By G. C. S. Marar*

An eight-line poem in two quatrains, wholly descriptive and aural. - Metallic and funerary imagery: light that sleeps in steel, hills pealing hollow as sepulchres.
- The final line makes silence, not sound, the devouring force.
- The shortest item in the issue's poetry section.

### We Three
*By Buddhadeva Bose*

A long story told by Bikash, looking back from after the Second World War to Dacca's Purana Paltan in 1927, when he and his two neighbours Asit and Hitangshu - the only three households in an open field of toads and wet grass - fell in love with the same girl. She is Antara Dey, daughter of the family renting the ground floor of Hitangshu's father's Tara Kutir; her family calls her Taru, and the boys, after Bikash sees a print of the Gioconda, secretly rename her Mona Lisa, a private possession of a name. The comedy of the first movement is all social terror: whether to accept Mr Dey's casual invitation, sandals leaving dirty patches on a polished floor, the disgrace of failing to return her folded-hands greeting, Bikash unable to raise his eyes or speak for fear of betraying his Dacca brogue while the Calcuttan Asit produces 'mashima' effortlessly.

The story's centre is her typhoid. For a month and a half she is 'ours': Asit biking through rain for ice, Hitangshu waking the depot by the river at two in the morning, Bikash - who cannot ride a bicycle - shadowing her mother, measuring medicine, carrying the doctor's bag, and keeping the night vigil in which she wakes, asks who he is, and asks him not to slip away while she sleeps. That secret he tells no one. She recovers; then the wedding to the handsome, perfumed Hiren Babu from Calcutta, whom all three promptly begin to imitate; then pregnancy, misread by the boys as a spitting sickness; then, after a night and a day of screaming that the narrator hears as 'the primeval cry of Earth the Mother', Mr Dey's trembling lips forming the words 'Come in and see'. The coda disperses everyone in a few sentences - Hiren Babu remarried within a year, the Deys transferred, Asit dead of some disease at Tinsukia, Hitangshu married and settled in Germany - leaving the narrator alone with a dream that breaks through his working hours. Translated from the Bengali by Jyotirmoy Datta, with footnotes explaining mashima, Ashwin, Aghran, Vijaya, the shanai, the turmeric bath, the dhoti and the Bengali forms of address the boys agonised over when drafting their letter.

- Framed as retrospect from after the war to Purana Paltan, Dacca, in 1927-28, with the place and date incanted throughout.
- Three friends share one unspoken love; the secret name 'Mona Lisa' is the mechanism by which they privately possess her.
- Class and provincial anxiety drive the comic first half - the Calcuttan's ease against the Dacca narrator's terror of his own accent.
- Her typhoid gives the three an 'occupation'; the narrator's night vigil is the story's emotional centre and stays his secret.
- The unsendable letter, drafted to avoid choosing between 'you' and 'thou', is a set-piece on Bengali forms of address.
- Pregnancy and death in childbirth are narrated entirely from outside the room, as sound; the boys are never allowed a glimpse of her.
- The coda kills or scatters every character in a paragraph, leaving memory as the only survivor.
- Translated from the Bengali by Jyotirmoy Datta, with thirteen explanatory footnotes; the translator is credited only at the end of the story, not on the contents page.

### Kanchanjangha, A Disquieting Mirror
*By Jyotirmoy Datta*

Jyotirmoy Datta opens the ARTS section by quoting, at length and without comment, hostile letters about Satyajit Ray's Kanchanjangha published in an English daily - 'new in form but hollow in content', 'a minute quantity of food served on a beautiful golden plate before a famished man', 'too bizarrely intellectual to be called art for the people'. He reads the complaints as self-revealing. The correspondents concede the film's formal beauty and then deny it content because they wanted 'life-force' and a tale of the heart: 'The Indian viewer is sentimental; he considers his money well spent only if his endocrine glands are squeezed so mercilessly that his eyes sprout tears as fast as artesian wells.' Ray refuses those satisfactions - the anglophile Rai Bahadur is not knocked on the nose, Manisha may yet end up as Mrs Bannerji, and the reconciliation of Anima and her husband is a domestic compromise of petty sacrifices rather than a triumph of love. Worse for the 'good Bengali' is the dialogue, whose English words sit in the Bengali 'like too many raisins in a cake, or rather, flies in milk', exposing the khichry language a language-proud Bengali uses even while in love.

Against the charge that the beauty is separable from the content, Datta argues that unlike Pather Panchali or Aparajita almost nothing in this film survives abstraction from its flow - the shot of Manisha pressing her face to a tree against a blurred Gurkha band works only as a composite of colour, sound and the contrast between a lonely girl and a crowd in the most public spot in Darjeeling. He defends the accidental structure: by moving the characters to a place where social restraints loosen, Ray makes the accidental seem almost inevitable. He also concedes a cultural verdict - there has been no classical period in Bengali culture, no Kalidasa or Racine to teach the poetry of form - and notes in a footnote that the stills chosen for Quest were picked for their bearing on his theme rather than as photographic gems. The essay ends on the Rai Bahadur turning his back on the peak at the moment the cloudbank shifts, and on the Nepalese boy's song that neither he nor we understand.

- Built out of quoted hostile newspaper letters, which Datta treats as evidence about the audience rather than about the film.
- Ray's refusals - no defeat of the Rai Bahadur, no clear romantic victory, a marriage patched up by commonsense - are read as deliberate ambiguity.
- The English-studded Bengali dialogue is identified as the deepest source of offence, because it holds a mirror to the bilingual viewer.
- Unlike the Apu films, Kanchanjangha yields almost no shot that keeps its power outside the film's flow.
- Darjeeling as setting is what licenses the accidental: social restraints loosen and chance encounters acquire the look of inevitability.
- Datta argues Bengali culture lacks a classical period and therefore a training in form, generalising the point cautiously to the rest of India in a footnote.
- A footnote records his own part in selecting the stills that accompany the article - the plate section the contents page never lists.

### Kanchanjangha, An Experiment in Anti-Film
*By P. Lal*

P. Lal answers Datta from the other side of the controversy by taking Kanchanjangha seriously as a specimen of a genre. He begins with John Grierson's 1930s prediction that the French avant-garde would give way to the propagandist film, notes how wrong it was, and places Ray's film in a lineage running through Resnais' Marienbad, which critics call the classic anti-film. Kanchanjangha, he says, 'is not quite as experimental and successful as that, but it is an anti-film nonetheless' - a serious spoof, an elaborate doodle of film and feeling, announced by the Nepalese boy winking from an oversize 'K' on Ray's own poster. He then tests the film against the requirements of the form: a story allowed to meander with no real resolution, unity imposed by directorial skill rather than arising from the material; the avoidance of physical conflict, with the clatter of ponies drowning Mr Banerjee's proposal; the refusal of chief characters, so that the cast is a clutter of vignettes with Anil Chatterjee a 'main' character on three minutes of screen time and Lila on about three seconds; and the refusal of moral commitment.

It is on that last requirement that Lal turns critical. Quoting Ray's Arun - 'Scratch us and see: we are both good' - he objects that 'the answer to a disintegrated civilisation is not a disintegrated poem, and the presentation of confused and amoral people is not to be achieved through a confused and amoral film', and notes that none of Ray's earlier films lacks responsible moral comment. He catalogues the imposed order (the shooting script's numbered map of Observatory Hill) against the improvisation (English and American tourists recruited into shot, dahlias added because Marie Seton pointed at them), and then itemises the cliche symbols with open mockery: mist for mental confusion, tortuous roads for complicated relationships, amorous pigeons behind a courting couple, a triangular slip-chute for the triangle, saffron and white for the virginal girl and glaring striped purple for the adulterous one. He finds the blind beggar and the boy's song fuzzy, Ashok's transformation implausible, the uncle crudely drawn, and the loss of voice perspective in long shots - which Marie Seton called subtle - merely repetitive and irritating. The essay ends on a question that doubles as a verdict: 'How does one assess an anti-film except by anti-criticism?'

- Locates Kanchanjangha in an international anti-film lineage (Resnais, Cocteau) and in the wider anti-art of Robbe-Grillet, Aragon, Benn and Pasolini.
- Tests the film against four generic requirements: meandering story, no physical conflict, no chief characters, no moral commitment.
- Screen-time data is used as evidence of deliberate diffusion: Anil Chatterjee three minutes as a 'main' character, Lila about three seconds.
- Lal's central objection is moral, not formal - a confused subject does not license a confused film.
- The tension between the numbered shooting-script map of Observatory Hill and on-the-spot improvisation is treated as essential to the form.
- A long mocking inventory of cliche symbols: mist, tortuous roads, pigeons, the triangular slip-chute, colour-coded saris.
- Marie Seton is cited twice as the on-set source, and once contradicted on the loss of voice perspective.
- Closes by claiming Kanchanjangha as the first Indian anti-film and the first colour anti-film anywhere.

### Feather Woman of the Jungle
*By S. Melluish*

S. Melluish reviews Amos Tutuola's fifth book, Feather Woman of the Jungle (Faber and Faber, 132 pp. 15s.), opening the BOOKS section. He begins sceptically with the reception of The Palm-Wine Drinkard, greeted with 'an inebriated clamour of praise' as the first important literary work in English by a West African published in London, and suggests that English critics, made fashionable in their sympathy for African national aspirations, were quick to seize on Tutuola as the portent of an indigenous Nigerian literature. - Reviews Tutuola's fifth book and reads its English reception as partly political fashion rather than literary judgment.
- Describes the form precisely: ten framed tales told by an old chieftain about six journeys in search of 'wealths'.

- Rejects the prose on grounds of cumulative strain rather than of incorrectness - the idiom is 'apt' but exhausting.

### Courage of Genius
*By C. L. Nahal*

Printed under the running title 'Pasternak's Ordeal', C. L. Nahal reviews Robert Conquest's Courage of Genius: The story of Boris Pasternak (Collins and Harvill, 191 pp. 18s.). He notes the book's significance as written by a poet about a poet and dedicated to a poet, Philip Larkin, and says that what is on trial in it is not only the Russian dictatorship but every totalitarian system where the artist cannot breathe freely. His most unusual move is to concede that much of the Russian anger in 1958 was genuine and was provoked less by Doctor Zhivago than by the Nobel award, and to blame the Nobel Committee for bad timing: the Russians have a real grievance that Chekhov, Gorky, Tolstoy and, in our own times, Sholokhov were passed over, and 'it does look queer ... that the Committee should think Winston Churchill good enough for the highest honour in the field of literature but not Leo Tolstoy'. Nahal quotes Pasternak's letter to Khrushchev at length - 'I am linked with Russia by my birth, life, and work', 'A departure beyond the borders of my country would for me be equivalent to death' - and describes Conquest's method as documentary rather than interpretative, resurrected from material already in the press but held together by an artist's visible annoyance at the bullying of a brother artist. - Frames the Pasternak affair as a test of every totalitarian system, not of Russia alone.
- Concedes that Soviet anger was genuine and partly provoked by the Nobel award rather than by the novel.
- Blames the Nobel Committee for bad timing and for a record of passing over Tolstoy, Chekhov, Gorky and Sholokhov.
- Objects that the West read Doctor Zhivago as a political novel, which was unfair to Pasternak in a different way.
- Notes that the book is documentary rather than interpretative, with nearly half of it appendices of the campaign texts.
- Ends on the claim that not killing Pasternak may have been crueller than killing him.

### Asoka and the Decline of the Mauryas
*By Ashin Das Gupta*

Under the running title 'The Good King Asoka', Ashin Das Gupta reviews Romila Thapar's Asoka and the Decline of the Mauryas (Oxford University Press, 276 pp. 45s.) at unusual length and with unusual method: he describes the book chapter by chapter with page references and then argues with its thesis point by point. The opening is disarming - ancient history as 'a closed preserve of respectability where sedate antiquarians browse', a world lost in trivialities for which the historians are hard to blame because they have so little to build on - and that is what makes Thapar praiseworthy, her gifted pen assisting an intrepid imagination. He identifies her governing attitude in the claim that Asoka was not a visionary but representative of his time, understood his age and realised in Indian terms what it demanded, and says this is a point a reader may reject but cannot ignore. He works through the sources chapter, the narrative from Asoka's birth to death with its frustrating chronological digressions, the treatment of Buddhist accounts that are narrated and then discarded as monkish, the economic chapter based on Megasthenes and the Arthashastra, the administrative chapter, the religious-policy chapter, the reconstruction of the fifty years after Asoka that scarcely rises above a king-list, and the final chapter's rejection of military weakness, Brahmanic reaction, Kosambi's economic crisis and Nihar Ranjan Ray's popular uprising in favour of a top-heavy administration and absent national consciousness.

The review then turns adversarial. He shows that her own paragraph on economic change forcing the political pattern in fact proves the reverse - that centralised empire helped trade - and mounts five objections to the central thesis that Asoka adopted Dhamma to conciliate a rising commercial class chafing at low social status: there is little concrete evidence of a spectacular expansion of Maurya trade; there is nothing to show the merchants felt humiliated, which is an assumption others may not share; no one has connected sixth-century-BC philosophical disputation with the commercial classes; she herself concedes that Asoka's Dhamma was not Buddhism; and it is hard to see how preaching a new faith in a land of ancient beliefs could have helped the central power at all. His own conclusion is that Asoka preached Dhamma because he thought it right.

- A chapter-by-chapter account with page citations, unusually detailed for a periodical review.
- Identifies Thapar's governing thesis: Asoka as representative of his age rather than in opposition to it.
- Praises her linguistic equipment and bibliography while calling some chapters plodding and the post-Asoka chapter little more than a king-list.
- Shows that her own example of economic change forcing political change actually demonstrates the reverse causation.
- Mounts five specific objections to the commercial-class explanation of Asoka's Dhamma.
- Notes her own concession that Asoka's Dhamma was not Buddhism, which weakens the political reading.
- Concludes that Asoka preached Dhamma because he believed it right, not to woo merchants.

### Culture: An Introduction
*By K. D. Valicha*

Under the running title 'Culture Analysed', K. D. Valicha reviews B. S. Sanyal's Culture: An Introduction (Asia Publishing House, 314 pp. Rs. 16.75) and begins with a compressed classification of it: pragmatic in intention, metaphysical in sensibility, analytic in methodology, holistic in outlook, missionary in aim and, as the author would like to think, harmonious in ideal. Sanyal's individual is not a particular but a systematic unity of reflexes, instincts, percepts, scientific categories, philosophical valuation, practical activities and educational activities, analysable in those terms but not reducible to any of them; culture is ontologically the realisation of values, in which value has a regulative function and hedonic feeling is its constituent; ideal culture is non-fixational, since any fixation of feeling at a lower level of experience checks development - and Communist culture is fixational because efficient feeling is fixed at the level of social theory and practice.

Valicha lays out the three linked processes Sanyal defines - acculturation, humanization and individualization, each preventing a different fixation and the last superseding the second - and reports that religion is what lends individuality to culture while history is the next means after education. He gives space to the distinction between writing history and making history, and to the claim that historical explanation involves 'laws' of history as major premises. He judges the chapter on gregational ethics the most brilliant part of the book, including its broad redefinition of genocide as 'a revolutionary method of alienation and conversion' of which Communism is accused. His verdict is cautious rather than dismissive: a compilation of a lifetime's study that brings scattered approaches together and catches a major characteristic of the historical situation, difficult to judge simply because its abstractions are synthesised into a system, and best allowed to grow on the reader, since a cursory reading invites a rejection that would be 'too easy and gratifying'.

- Opens with a six-term classification of the book's intention, sensibility, method, outlook, aim and ideal.
- States the book's purpose as anti-communist and culture-saving, which sets up its problems of ends and means.
- Reports Sanyal's definition of the individual as a systematic unity that is analysable but not reducible.
- Explains the key concept of non-fixational culture and the charge that Communist culture is fixational.
- Sets out the triad of acculturation, humanization and individualization with page references.
- Singles out the chapter on gregational ethics, including a broadened definition of genocide, as the book's best.

### The Writer's Dilemma
*By Uma Vasudev*

Printed under the running title 'Introspection', Uma Vasudev reviews The Writer's Dilemma (Oxford University Press, 88 pp. 12s. 6d.), a collection of essays introduced by Stephen Spender. She reads the volume as a series of reactions: against the depressive fabric of proof, 'the real cloth-of-gold fabric of a possible Way'; against conditioning and conformity, a 'mule-like, camel-like individuality'; against God, no-God or vice-versa, and against all ills, the creative genius. Although the contributors include a historian, novelists, a philosopher, a poet and journalists, they all represent a creative order, so each winds up his reasoning with an affirmation of faith in himself and in the public; the miraculous is always round the corner and, as Spender says in his introduction, the situation is 'pregnant with impossibilities'. Her account of the balance is exact: the pessimism flows from the discussion of averages and generalities, the optimism from faith in a creative spirit that is born tough and a rebel. She quotes Richard Wollheim's essay, The Consequences of Communication, for the fundamental point that it is lightly assumed that the age before mass culture was a period of diffused eccentricity and happy anarchy.

The review's own contribution is to transpose the problem to India. Where Western writers fear that their public's creative response will be killed by cheap mass media - brainwashing, subliminal advertising, commercial television, Westerns, comics - India has superstition, prejudice and dogmatic religion, and the effect is the same; the added danger is that the modern substitutes will replace the older ones without even an interval of possible awakening, while 'the writers themselves are sunk in pitiful platitude'. So essays that at first seem of merely academic interest to countries like ours turn out to describe a similar problem: a cocoon of cliches and ready-made ideas here, matched by women's serials and somatic drugs there. Her conclusion takes the book's own line - only genius is transcendental, only the unpredictable has solutions to offer - and identifies the recurrent and most hopeful theme as the need for faith and the courage to wait.

- Reviews an OUP symposium introduced by Stephen Spender, whose contributors span history, fiction, philosophy, poetry and journalism.
- Characterises the book as a set of reactions, each ending in an affirmation of faith in the writer and in the public.
- Locates the pessimism in generalisation and the optimism in the toughness of the creative spirit.
- Transposes the mass-culture argument to India, substituting superstition, prejudice and dogmatic religion for commercial television and comics.
