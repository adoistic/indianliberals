# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 28 (Jan/Mar 1961, cover-dated 'winter 1960-61') opens with three essays on the shape of post-war modernity -- Sushil Dey on the moral costs of Galbraith-style 'affluence', Georges Friedmann's Congress-for-Cultural-Freedom conference paper reassessing technical civilization as both consumer and producer, and Jyotirmoy Datta's polemic (printed as 'On Caged Chaffinches and Polyglot Parrots') defending Indian-language writers against the claims of Indo-English literature. Raymond Aron then delivers a pointed retrospective, 'Presumption', judging de Gaulle's Algerian policy two years on. V. A. Shahane profiles the controversial Marathi poet B. S. Mardhekar; Aamir Ali surveys shared motifs across Asian folk tales; C. L. Miglani and D. L. Pham-xuan-Luong contribute shorter pieces on essential-services strikes and the Vietnamese Tet festival respectively; and William Phillips files a 'Letter from New York' on the Kennedy-era mood in American politics and letters. A poetry section (two Nissim Ezekiel sonnets, and single poems by Pradip Sen, K. Raghavendra Rao and F. R. Stanley), an essay on Indian architecture's search for a post-Independence idiom by Madhao Achwal, Anita Desai's ghost-narrated short story 'Ghost House', Herbert Luthy's long open-letter rebuttal of a Marxist theory of imperialism, and Punya Sloka Ray's review-article on Michael Polanyi's 'Personal Knowledge' round out the front half. The back half is entirely criticism and correspondence: Sadhan Kumar Ghosh's books column, a substantial, contentious review by P. Lal of Dom Moraes's 'Gone Away', further reviews of art, theatre, poetry, language, politics, Soviet nationality policy and current fiction, an 'Our Reviewers'/'Our Contributors' pair of biographical notes, and a correspondence section carrying a rebuttal of Edward Shils on the Indian intellectual, a correction from Nicolas Nabokov, and an unlisted letter from a Mauritian correspondent. Throughout, the magazine's Cold-War liberal, anti-Communist and anti-doctrinaire orientation (it is sponsored by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom) is explicit, most sharply in Luthy's dismantling of 'Marxist' imperialism theory and in the reviews of books on Hungary and the Soviet deportations.

## Essays

### Affluence and Growth
*By Sushil Dey*

Dey argues that the 'affluent society' identified by Galbraith afflicts capitalist and socialist economies alike, since both have converged on the same goal of maximizing output through modern technological apparatus, leaving individuals as interchangeable cogs rather than originators of production. He surveys the psychological costs of undirected material growth (advertising-driven consumerism, teenage 'beatnik' revolt) and argues economic planning must be subordinated to a moral purpose of fostering human growth, not mere output. He proposes that newly developing countries, precisely because their technology has not yet outrun ordinary people's comprehension, have a chance to build democratic, rural-centred development that avoids the affluent world's mistake, and that such a course would also benefit the rich countries by reorienting demand away from 'frills and gadgets'.

- Capitalism and socialism have converged on the same goal of maximizing productive output, making their old ideological opposition mostly historical.
- Modern production strips ordinary workers of initiative, concentrating judgment in a 'small managerial elite'.
- Galbraith's 'affluence' produces psychological and cultural damage (consumerism, loss of thrift, teenage revolt) even as it solves distribution.
- The liberal case for redirecting resources toward public goods (education, health, culture) requires large-scale central control, which is itself in tension with liberal premises.
- Newly developing countries can choose a rural, democratically-paced development model that keeps pace with people's own capacity to understand and direct it.
- A healthier 'affluence', geared to capacity-building rather than consumption, would change the character of demand from the developing world (books and ballet rather than colour television).

### The Reassessment of Modern Societies
*By Georges Friedmann*

Friedmann's conference paper (delivered at the Congress for Cultural Freedom's Berlin general conference, June 1960) argues that 19th- and 20th-century social theory -- Saint-Simon, Comte, Durkheim, Marx, Lenin among others -- no longer suffices to interpret the 'technical civilization' now emerging worldwide. He distinguishes a society's 'technical environment' from its 'technical civilization' and assesses modern society from the standpoint of consumer and producer: material consumption has improved broadly, even for the poorest, but the mass media are double-edged, capable of educating or of manipulating; industrial work has been physically eased but drained of personal meaning through repetitive specialization, even as automation creates new problems of enforced, empty leisure (the 'double job' phenomenon). He closes by calling for the social sciences to keep pace with the exact sciences in restoring an ethical equilibrium to technical civilization.

- Classical social theory (Marx, Comte, Durkheim, etc.) cannot fully explain contemporary technical/industrial society.
- A 'technical environment' (machines, transport, mass media) is now near-universal, reaching even 'underdeveloped countries' and provoking rural-to-urban migration into slums.
- Material consumption has improved across social classes, but cultural consumption via mass media is 'equivocal' -- capable of enlightenment or of manipulation and even totalitarian control.
- Industrial specialization has reduced physical drudgery but intensified nervous fatigue and destroyed workers' sense of personal achievement -- the 'New Sisyphus'.
- Reduced working hours have not produced the hoped-for flowering of leisure culture; instead workers often take a 'double job'.
- Friedmann calls for the social sciences to progress alongside, and morally temper, the exact sciences.

### Indian Creative Writers and English
*By Jyotirmoy Datta*

Under the printed heading 'On Caged Chaffinches and Polyglot Parrots', Datta mounts a personal, often satirical defence of writing in one's own Indian language against the claims of Indo-English writers. He argues language is not chosen but 'dictates' itself to the writer, that no language is too 'underdeveloped' for great poetry, that the Indo-English writer's claim to a uniquely all-India, cosmopolitan voice is unfounded (comparing it to a mistaken belief that English is today what Sanskrit was in its living heyday), and that the real, unstated motive for many is financial reward from Western publishers. He singles out P. Lal's Writers Workshop manifesto for particular rebuttal, while praising Madhusudan Datta and Rabindranath Tagore as writers whose greatness came through, not despite, their own language.

- Datta rejects the idea that writers 'choose' a language; for him the language of composition is involuntary, 'dictated' rather than selected.
- He disputes the claim that English is the only language that can express an all-India, pan-regional reality, comparing it unfavourably to Sanskrit's actual living literary history.
- He specifically challenges P. Lal's claim (quoted from Writers Workshop No. 2) that 'only in English can real Indian poetry be written'.
- He argues no language is inherently 'underdeveloped' for poetry; early masters (Homer, Dante, Pushkin) achieved greatness in languages at their infancy.
- He names money -- high payments from Western publishers and magazines -- as the unstated but 'overwhelming' reason many Indians write in English.
- He holds up Madhusudan Datta and Rabindranath Tagore as writers whose universality came from, not despite, writing in Bengali.

### De Gaulle's Algerian Policy
*By Raymond Aron*

Printed under the title 'Presumption', Aron revisits forecasts he made in June 1958 about de Gaulle's likely Algerian policy and judges them against two years of events. He concludes de Gaulle has pursued a hybrid of unconditional-surrender demands and a promised 'Algerian Algeria', refusing to negotiate with the F.L.N. as a legitimate interlocutor while simultaneously conceding its core claim (Algerian self-determination) -- a contradiction Aron calls illogical and traces to Gaullist pride rather than strategy. He compares the trajectory to French policy in Vietnam (Bao-Daism), argues secret negotiation remains possible even if public negotiation is politically closed to de Gaulle, and extends the critique to de Gaulle's wider foreign policy failures (the tripartite-directorate memorandum, NATO integration, the 'Europe of fatherlands') as instances of the same gap between grandiose ambition and diminishing French capacity.

- Aron checks his own 1958 forecasts about three possible French policies (continued pacification, negotiation with the F.L.N., or an intermediate compromise) against two years of subsequent events.
- De Gaulle's actual policy is a hybrid: demanding F.L.N. surrender while working toward an 'Algerian Algeria' -- logically inconsistent, in Aron's view.
- Aron attributes the impasse to Gaullist pride ('France is a great lady who grants favours, but does not permit them to be wrested from her') rather than to strategic necessity.
- He draws an explicit parallel with French policy toward Vietnam and Bao Dai, warning of a similar hollow transfer of nominal independence.
- The essay broadens into a wider indictment of de Gaulle's foreign policy overreach -- the tripartite-directorate memorandum, the Atlantic Alliance, and NATO integration -- as symptoms of the same disproportion between ambition and resources.
- Aron ends on the phrase that gives the piece its title: the 'presumption' of 1958 is now plainly visible in 1960's outcomes.

### B. S. Mardhekar: Modern Marathi Poet
*By V. A. Shahane*

Shahane profiles B. S. Mardhekar (1909-1956) as the pivotal, controversial figure of modern Marathi poetry, tracing Marathi verse from its devotional origins through the individualist and nationalist reform movements of Keshav-sut, Ravi Kiran Mandal and Kusumagraj to Mardhekar's post-war 'widening' of individual consciousness into social awareness, comparable in Shahane's account to T. S. Eliot's impact on English poetry. He recounts Mardhekar's Bombay obscenity prosecution over his poetry (successfully defended on I. A. Richards's 'context theorem of meaning'), quotes extensively from Mardhekar's poems illustrating his deliberately unornamented, semantically inventive diction and parody of devotional convention, and credits him as a pioneer of stream-of-consciousness technique in the Marathi novel.

- Mardhekar is presented as simultaneously an 'event' and a 'problem' in modern Marathi literature, provoking both worship and condemnation.
- Shahane traces a lineage from devotional Marathi saint-poets through Keshav-sut's individualism to the post-1945 disillusionment Mardhekar shares with T. S. Eliot's 'The Wasteland'.
- Mardhekar was prosecuted by the Government of Bombay for obscenity over 'A Few Poems' (1947) and 'A Few More Poems' (1950), and won using I. A. Richards's context theory of meaning.
- His diction is described as deliberately unornamented and semantically inventive, coining words like 'chakrapani' to describe an industrial mill-worker rather than Krishna.
- He pioneered the stream-of-consciousness technique in the Marathi novel with 'Night Turned into Day'.
- Shahane predicts Mardhekar a permanent, if minority, place in Marathi literary history.

### Notes on Some Asian Folk Tales
*By Aamir Ali*

Aamir Ali surveys recurring motifs across Asian folk tales -- from Bengal, Kashmir, Cambodia, Japan, the Philippines and Western India -- noting shared elements (rakshasas whose life resides in some hidden object; a heroine reborn through successive forms after death; the tortoise-and-hare race retold with a snail or buffalo) alongside genuine regional variation, and traces how such stories seem to travel and adapt between countries while keeping a common narrative grammar rooted in the Ramayana, Mahabharata and Jataka tales.

- Notable published collections of Asian folk tales (Bengal, Kashmir, Ceylon, Japan, Thailand) are largely out of print and hard to find.
- Common motifs recur across the region: demons (rakshasas/Yakas) whose 'life' is hidden in an external object that must be destroyed to kill them.
- A recurring 'reincarnation' convention has a wronged heroine reborn successively as animal, plant and finally human again (illustrated via a Cambodian and a Western Indian tale).
- The hare-and-tortoise race motif recurs with local substitutions (a snail challenging a hare in Cambodia; a buffalo in the Philippines).
- Ali attributes the shared grammar of these tales partly to the common heritage of the Ramayana, Mahabharata and Jataka tales across the region.
- Some tales show clear signs of travelling between countries and acquiring local variation en route (e.g. a rakshasi-becomes-queen story found in both Kashmir and Cambodia).

### Strike in Essential Services
*By C. L. Miglani*

Miglani argues that India's lack of a clear, consistent government policy on strikes in essential public services risks serious economic and administrative damage, contrasting India's ad hoc use of special ordinances with the more decisive legal bans on such strikes in the United States (Taft-Hartley Act), Japan and Indonesia. He contends that a government employee's responsibility to the public overrides any claimed 'equal right' to strike, warns that the expanding public sector risks blurring 'essential services' into an ever-widening category that could edge socialism toward outright state control, and calls for clearer thinking rather than mere suppression.

- India has no settled, coherent definition of 'essential services' where strikes can be banned by ordinance, unlike the U.S., Japan or (post-1957) Indonesia.
- Miglani argues a public-utility employee's responsibility to the community overrides any 'equal right' to strike using the strongest bargaining tactics.
- He surveys the Essential Services Maintenance Act (1957) and subsequent presidential ordinances widening the banned-strike list.
- He warns that an open-ended 'any other service' clause could let government label almost any public-sector industry 'essential', narrowing the gap between socialism and outright state control.
- Recent strikes (State Bank employees, Bhilai Steel, Central Government employees) are cited as evidence of the stakes.
- He calls for 'clearer thinking and attempts at resolution rather than suppression of differences'.

### The Meaning of Tet
*By D. L. Pham-xuan-Luong*

Framed as an open letter to editor Abu Sayeed Ayyub, the Saigon-based Pham-xuan-Luong explains the meaning and rites of Tet, the Vietnamese lunar New Year, tracing its Chinese-antiquity origins, its role as a Confucian family-reunion and ancestor-cult occasion, and its symbolic function of wiping away the old year's grievances -- contrasting the Lunar Calendar's extended New Year holiday, evolved from an agricultural society without a weekly Sabbath, against the West's single-day, Sunday-anchored calendar.

- Tet's origin is traced to Chinese antiquity, though its rites have since become distinctively Vietnamese.
- Confucius's Five Classical Books are cited as treating Tet's observance as a compulsory periodic celebration for the King.
- Tet functions as a family-reunion and ancestor-cult occasion binding 'the living with the dead'.
- The New Year is meant to wipe out the old year's 'hatred and rancours' entirely.
- The extended, month-long character of the Lunar New Year holiday is explained as compensation for an agricultural society that historically had no weekly day of rest.
- The piece closes framing itself explicitly as a bridge of 'mutual understanding between our two cultures', per both the author's covering note and the editors' reply.

### Letter from New York
*By William Phillips*

Writing just after Kennedy's election victory, Phillips reads the vote as the merger of long-separate political and intellectual discontents against post-war conservatism and philistinism, surveys a 'new radicalism' in American letters that is more attitude than program (the Beat movement, Paul Goodman, Harold Rosenberg, Norman Mailer, Robert Lowell), and closes by noting the sudden cultural permissiveness around sex in American fiction and film, which he attributes less to any change in sexual behaviour than to a broader historical dislocation following the era's scientific and political shocks.

- Phillips reads Kennedy's election as symbolizing the convergence of separately-brewing political and intellectual discontent in America.
- He distinguishes the new mood from 1930s-style political radicalism: it is 'literary' -- more attitude and stance than program.
- The Beat movement is assessed as more a social than literary phenomenon, its lasting effect confined to a few genuinely talented poets (Ginsberg, Corso, Kerouac).
- New books by Paul Goodman, Harold Rosenberg, Norman Mailer and Robert Lowell are singled out as striking a genuinely new note after a decade of 'gentility, respectability and smugness'.
- He notes the sudden prominence of explicit sex in American fiction and film, attributing it to historical dislocation rather than any real increase in sexual activity itself.
- He credits Kenneth Galbraith's review of Paul Goodman's 'Growing Up Absurd' as recognizing Goodman's originality against the grain of most social criticism.

### Perspective
*By Nissim Ezekiel*

A sonnet-sequence meditation on the tension between visionary calling and self-doubt, moving from saints whose inner voices were dismissed by 'priests and lawyers' as diabolical, through the dancer's and pilgrim's discipline, to a closing injunction to persist despite illusion and leave ultimate judgement to darkness or God.

- The poem opens by likening the poet's/mystic's inner call to the discredited 'voices' of saints, dismissed by institutional authority.
- It moves through images of dance, pilgrimage and quest as figures for creative or spiritual vocation.
- It closes with an injunction to accept one's fate ('turn the sod / With deeper thrust') and leave final judgement to forces beyond human control.

### Poet, Lover, Birdwatcher
*By Nissim Ezekiel*

A short poem comparing the discipline of the birdwatcher's patience to that of the poet and the lover, arguing all three succeed not by force of will but by attentive waiting for the right, unforced moment.

- The poem draws an extended analogy between birdwatching, courtship and poetic composition as arts of patient waiting rather than active pursuit.
- It closes on images of transformation and revelation ('The deaf can hear, the blind recover sight') achieved through such patience.

### Men At Work
*By Pradip Sen*

A short free-verse poem set among labouring men under a punishing sun, contrasting the indifferent natural world ('the sun, that unthinking / Searing flame') with the men's own embodied suffering and their refusal of easy consolation ('This is no time to sing / Or praise the goodness of the Earth').

- The poem juxtaposes an indifferent, 'unthinking' sun against the physical suffering of manual labourers.
- The labourers themselves reject any romanticised 'praise' of the earth, insisting instead on the felt reality of sweat and fear of death.

### The Vultures
*By K. Raghavendra Rao*

A poem (dedicated 'for friend Lal') imagining vultures as both natural scavengers and figures for the corrupting, treasonous forces of contemporary political life, who 'trade in small fears' and profit from decay while performing their grim office with 'winged modesty'.

- Vultures are figured simultaneously as literal scavenger birds and as a metaphor for political betrayal and moral scavenging.
- The poem's tone moves between disgust and a kind of admiration for the vultures' unashamed, uncomplaining discharge of their function.

### A Valediction
*By F. R. Stanley*

A meditative poem on parting, arguing that the 'true valediction' is not the sentimental gesture of a single farewell but the deeper, ongoing knowledge that living in time is itself a continuous separation from every moment once loved.

- The poem redefines 'valediction' away from a single dramatic farewell scene toward an ongoing philosophical condition of loss inherent in time itself.
- It closes by comparing the aftermath of parting to either 'a life just ended, or a life just begun'.

### Indian Tradition and Contemporary Architecture
*By Madhao B. Achwal*

Achwal diagnoses contemporary Indian architecture as caught between two failed responses -- an official, government-favoured pastiche of 'Glorious Tradition' motifs (Moghul domes, Ajanta arches, Gopurams) grafted onto modern buildings, and a younger generation's reactive rejection of any symbolic/traditional content in favour of bare craftsmanship -- and argues both mistake surface architectural forms for the deeper cultural forces that produced them. He traces the pastiche tendency to the psychological insecurity of a newly independent state needing to loudly assert both national identity and international standing, and calls for architects to be freed from both nostalgic and anti-traditionalist dogma to pursue their own honest creative expression.

- Contemporary Indian architecture shows two dominant, opposed trends: official pastiche-'Indian' revivalism, and a younger reactive modernism that denies symbolic/traditional content altogether.
- Achwal frames architecture as fundamentally a symbolic language of communication, not merely a functional-technical exercise.
- He traces the post-Independence hunger for monumental 'Indian' architecture to psychological insecurity -- a newly sovereign state needing to loudly assert both nationality and internationalism at once.
- He rejects the equation of architectural 'honesty' with exposed structure/materials, arguing artistic integrity is about fidelity to one's own aesthetic concept, not literal transparency.
- He compares the situation to revolutionary Russia's abandonment of constructivism for Tsarist-style monumentalism, and to Nazi Germany's parallel classicism.
- He calls for the 'Authority' (state patrons) to leave architects alone to experiment and create rather than judging buildings by conformity to an official idea of tradition.

### Ghost House
*By Anita Desai*

Narrated collectively by the ghosts inhabiting an old two-hundred-year-old family house in 'Chanderanagore' (Chandernagore), the story recounts the family's fear of the monsoon storms the ghosts actually love, the accident of young Khokon (left an 'idiot' after glimpsing a ghost while kite-flying), the drunken young heir who is terrified by a phantom carriage, the lonely old grandfather Thakur-babu's grief over a spectral dancing-girl, and the sweeper woman's superstitious dread -- the ghosts closing by insisting they mean the family no harm and simply wish to 'entertain' and comfort their 'dear haunted friends'.

- The story is narrated in the first-person plural by a band of household ghosts who love the monsoon season the frightened human family dreads.
- A central incident: the boy Khokon is left permanently traumatised ('idiot-faced') after apparently glimpsing one of the ghosts while flying a kite.
- The ghosts stage a phantom-carriage apparition to frighten the family's dissolute young heir returning drunk from a former tavern.
- The aged Thakur-babu is haunted by grief for a spectral dancing-girl from the house's more glamorous past, tied to memories of a colonial-era English tutor.
- The ghosts insist, in a plaintive final register, that they mean the family no harm and only wish to comfort their 'poor haunted' human companions.

### Marx's Theory of Imperialism
*By Herbert Luthy*

Printed as an open letter ('Imperialism, or Marx's Posthumous Martyrdoms') addressed to 'my dear Sternberg' (Fritz Sternberg, author of 'The Conflict of the Century'), Luthy mounts a sustained rebuttal of the Marxist theory that imperialism is a late, necessary phase of capitalist crisis-avoidance. He argues historically that imperialism and world-spanning trade preceded industrial capitalism by centuries (citing the 'India stuffs' cotton trade and the East India Company), that classical liberal economists including Marx himself largely ignored colonial empire as a pre-capitalist survival, that the causes of both the First World War and the 1929 crisis cannot be reduced to capitalist economic contradictions, and that genuine post-war scholarship on development economics (Furnivall, Colin Clark, Rostow, Kuznets) has left 'Marxist science' decades behind. He closes conceding Marx's genius while insisting his followers' scholastic dogma has ossified rather than extended his thought.

- Luthy rebuts the Leninist/Sternberg thesis that 'imperialism' is capitalism's necessary late-stage escape from its own contradictions, arguing colonial expansion long preceded industrial capitalism.
- He cites the pre-industrial global cotton and slave trade, and Britain's East India Company, as evidence imperialism was 'planetary before becoming national'.
- He argues classical liberal economists, Marx included, largely treated colonial empire and national rivalry as pre-capitalist survivals irrelevant to their economic models.
- He rejects Sternberg's account of the causes of the First World War as Anglo-German capitalist rivalry, and of the 1929 Depression as a confirmation of the 'law of the aggravation of capitalist crisis'.
- He credits modern development economics (Furnivall, Colin Clark, Rostow, Kuznetz, Perroux) with real advances that Marxist theory, wedded to 'opponents' confessions' as evidence, has failed to match.
- He closes acknowledging Marx's genius while arguing Marxist orthodoxy has calcified into an unfalsifiable scholasticism -- and pointedly notes that 'Nehru the statesman' has himself moved well beyond his own earlier revolutionary simplifications.

### Personality and Science
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

Ray reviews Michael Polanyi's 'Personal Knowledge' (1958) as a major, if flawed, statement of the 'integralist' pole in a perennial split within scientific thought between mechanistic/objectivist and holistic/personalist approaches. He credits Polanyi with a genuinely important argument -- that science is transmitted as an unformalisable craft-tradition through personal apprenticeship, not merely through written propositions -- while criticising Polanyi's reliance on loosely-verified secondary facts and, more seriously, his implicit suggestion that accepting science requires accepting the whole inherited tradition of 'Europe'.
