# Quest

## Summary

Quest Vol. III No. 1 (August-September 1957), edited by Nissim Ezekiel for the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, is a Cold-War-aligned Bombay journal of arts, literature and ideas rather than a political-economy periodical. Its non-fiction spine runs from C. R. M. Rao's essay on the 'nationalisation of culture' under Nehruvian planning, through Eliezer Livneh's balance-sheet of postwar American aid, to R. B. Joshi's travel piece on the ruined Peshwa capital of Bithur; it also carries unattributed pre-publication excerpts from Milovan Djilas's The New Class. Its literary and arts content -- the bulk of the issue -- includes the two prize-winning stories from Quest's short-story competition for Indian writers (S. J. Joshi's Marathi 'The Devil' and M. S. Kalyanasundaram's Tamil 'The Message'), two A. K. Ramanujan poems, Ka Naa Subramanyam's survey of the Tamil novel, reviews of Bengali theatre and of Soviet and Films Division cinema, and ten book reviews covering subjects from V. P. Menon's account of the transfer of power to Jacques Maritain's philosophy of religion and a hostile notice of Soviet propaganda fiction from Chukotka.

Five unnumbered photographic plates -- none listed on the printed contents page -- are tipped into the issue: a domestic photograph titled 'The Cradle (Ahmedabad)' by R. J. Chinwalla; a tribal-folk portrait from Bastar by Shyam Manohar; two film stills illustrating the Tilak documentary review; two photographs of the Bithur memorial and Aurangzeb's mosque illustrating the Bithur travel piece; and a standalone photo-essay on the fort of Jaisalmer by Dhruva N. Chaudhuri. These plates, plus the Djilas excerpt and the short-story-competition verdict note, are the issue's substantive unlisted content.

## Essays

### Nationalisation of Culture
*By C. R. M. RAO*

C. R. M. Rao argues that the classical-liberal doctrine of minimal government has, within a generation, given way in Indian political thought to an all-encompassing Welfare State ideal, and that even culture -- 'least amenable to planned reform' -- has now become state business and thereby the handmaid of politics. He surveys the post-Independence 'glut' of state-sponsored cultural activity (mushairas, exhibitions, National Laboratories, film awards) and argues this quantity masks an intellectual vacuum rather than a genuine renaissance, since neither literature, drama nor science shows a corresponding rise in creative or critical quality. He traces the deeper cause to a confusion of incompatible national ideals pursued simultaneously (democracy and the Welfare State, individual freedom and the Socialist Pattern, non-violence and armed pride, centralised planning and village self-sufficiency), compounded by a nationalised, utilitarian education system. He closes by warning that the fusion of a permanently-ruling party with an ever-expanding state, executed partly through cultural patronage, points not to a Welfare State or a Socialist Pattern but to a 'Total State' and indefinite one-party rule.

- Argues the Welfare State ideal has displaced classical liberal minimal government as India's reigning political idealism.
- Diagnoses a state-driven 'glut' of cultural activity (exhibitions, mushairas, film awards, National Laboratories) as quantity without a corresponding rise in creative quality.
- Identifies simultaneously pursued but incompatible national ideals (democracy/Welfare State, individual freedom/Socialist Pattern, non-violence/armed strength).
- Criticizes state nationalisation of textbooks and the frankly utilitarian aims stated in the Second Five Year Plan's education chapter.
- Warns that state control of culture, layered on economic centralisation and single-party dominance, risks producing a 'Total State' rather than a Welfare State.

### "Imperialist" Philanthropy
*By ELIEZER LIVNEH*

Eliezer Livneh draws a tentative 'balance sheet' of roughly a decade of American-led foreign aid (UNRRA, the Marshall Plan, Point Four) to some thirty-five countries. He credits the aid with successfully immunising recipient states against Communism or economic collapse in the immediate postwar reconstruction period, noting that former enemies Japan, Germany and Italy became its chief beneficiaries and that even Yugoslavia's independence from Moscow depended on it. But he argues the calculus changes once aid continues flowing into an already-normal economy: using Greece and Turkey as case studies, he shows how uninterrupted, non-commercial aid inflates bureaucracies, subsidises uncompetitive industry, and feeds chronic inflation, becoming a 'refined sweet poison' that erodes national self-reliance rather than a straightforward blessing. He predicts that as Cold War tensions ease, 'imperialist philanthropy' will give way to ordinary international investment and trade on a more businesslike footing.

- Estimates total postwar US-led foreign aid at close to 100 million dollars over roughly a decade (the text's own figure, likely an underestimate of the true billions).
- Argues aid was decisive in anti-Communist reconstruction in Europe and Asia, citing Japan, Germany, Italy and Yugoslavia.
- Distinguishes 'rebuilding after the war' aid (broadly beneficial) from continued aid to already-normal economies (Greece, Turkey), which bred bureaucratic bloat and uncompetitive industry.
- Argues continuous non-business assistance is psychologically corrosive: it breeds ingratitude in recipients and 'rottenness in the leading classes.'
- Predicts a shift from philanthropic aid toward ordinary international trade and investment as Cold War tensions relax.

### The Tamil Novel
*By KA NAA SUBRAMANYAM*

Ka Naa Subramanyam surveys roughly eighty years of the Tamil novel, from Vedanayakam Pillai's pioneering Prathapa Mudaliar (c.1877) through the 'serious' school of Rajam Iyer (Kamalambal Charithram) and A. Madhaviah (Padmavathy Charithram), whom he ranks alongside Flaubert and Turgenev, to the melodrama-driven mass tradition founded by Ponnusamy Pillai (modelled on G. W. M. Reynolds) and refined by prolific adapters like Vaduvoor Duraisamy Iyengar and, above all, the journalist-novelist R. Krishnamurthy ('Kalki'), whose serialised historical romances set the template most working Tamil novelists still follow. He also surveys a 'purposive' school (Mu Varadarajanar and followers) and a small surviving line of serious writers, including his own novels and Janakiraman's then-unpublished Moha Mul. His diagnosis is a 'stalemate': the reading public and the magazines that supply it reward melodrama and communal fiction, serious fiction finds little institutional support (no literary trusts, weak little-magazine culture, few sympathetic publishers), and 'it will be the lot of the serious novelist in Tamil to flounder as best he can for some more time.'

- Traces the Tamil novel from Vedanayakam Pillai's 1870s Prathapa Mudaliar through the 'serious' novelists Rajam Iyer and Madhaviah of the 1890s.
- Identifies Ponnusamy Pillai (modelled on G. W. M. Reynolds) as founder of a still-dominant melodramatic tradition.
- Credits/blames Kalki (R. Krishnamurthy) as the most influential and widely imitated modern Tamil novelist, prizing plot mechanics over depth.
- Surveys a 'purposive'/communal school (Mu Varadarajanar and followers) alongside a small surviving group of serious writers.
- Concludes that magazine economics and weak institutional support (no trusts, few little magazines, unsympathetic publishers) leave serious Tamil fiction in a stalemate.

### A Day in Bithur
*By R. B. JOSHI*

R. B. Joshi recounts a day-trip by public bus from Kanpur to Bithur on 11 May 1957, the day after the centenary unveiling of a bust to Peshwa Nanasaheb, opening with a comic account of the overcrowded bus (which at one point also carries two handcuffed 'dacoits' and a portly police Darogha Saheb on its roof). In Bithur a Maharashtrian Brahmin priest guides him through the razed grounds of the Peshwa's palace -- reduced to a well and rubble by the British after 1857 -- to the misspelled memorial bust ('Dhu Dhu Pant' for 'Dhondo Pant'), to Aurangzeb's mosque (said to mark the spot where Lakshman left Sita), and finally to the house of Tatya Tope's descendants, who still keep the family's portraits and gods. A postscript identifies Bithur/Brahmawarta as Baji Rao II's place of retirement, Nanasaheb's launching-point for the 1857 uprising, and, in Hindu myth, the site of Brahma's creation and horse-sacrifice.

- Describes an overcrowded Kanpur-Bithur bus journey, including two shackled prisoners and a police officer riding on the bus roof.
- Finds the site of the Peshwa's palace reduced entirely to rubble and a well, with only ruined dependents' houses nearby.
- Notes the memorial bust to Nanasaheb is inscribed with the misspelling 'Dhu Dhu Pant' instead of his actual alias 'Dhondo Pant.'
- Visits Aurangzeb's mosque, said locally to mark where Lakshman left Sita, and the house of Tatya Tope's surviving family.
- Postscript situates Bithur historically (Baji Rao II's retirement, Nanasaheb's 1857 revolt) and mythologically (site of Brahma's creation).

### The Devil
*By S. J. JOSHI*

S. J. Joshi's Marathi prize story (translated by Mangesh Padki) follows a Bombay chawl-dwelling office clerk and his wife who take in the narrator's friend Ram after Ram is retrenched, first advising him to send his wife and child to her father's while he alone lodges with them and job-hunts. As weeks of fruitless searching pass, Ram sinks into a mute, immobile, ravenously hungry passivity that the narrator's wife likens to the mythical demon of Marathi folklore who must be endlessly fed; the couple's sympathy curdles into disgust and secret cruelty (denying him a second cup of tea, mocking him behind his back) even as they cannot bring themselves to ask him to leave. One night they catch him furtively pilfering cigarettes and biscuits; by the next morning he has vanished with his belongings, and the narrator is left with sad recognition that 'the devil has departed... to become a human being.'

- A retrenched clerk, Ram, is taken in by the narrator and his wife after losing his job.
- Ram's prolonged unemployment turns him into a silent, passive, voraciously hungry presence the wife compares to a folkloric demon.
- The couple's hospitality curdles into resentment, secret cruelty, and eventual estrangement from their guest.
- Ram is caught secretly taking cigarettes and biscuits at night, underscoring his reduced, furtive state.
- Ram disappears overnight with his belongings; the narrator reflects that 'the devil' in him has departed to let him become human again.

### The Message
*By M. S. KALYANASUNDARAM*

M. S. Kalyanasundaram's Tamil prize story (translated by the author) follows Mohan Chetty, a South Indian government telegraphist whose hoped-for transfer home to Tamil Nadu is instead a posting to remote Bannu on the North-West Frontier. Furious and homesick, he almost resigns rather than go, but is talked out of it by his wife Veda Valli's patient invocation of duty and the saint Avvaiyar. In Bannu his misery deepens until, asked by a poor Pathan woman to draft a difficult telegram home -- a message urging her sons to bear the pain of separation because their earnings are lifting the family and village out of poverty -- Chetty is transformed by the act of composing her words. He writes home in a new spirit of acceptance, quoting Avvaiyar on braving 'tumultuous oceans' for one's family, leaving Veda Valli moved to grateful tears and prayer.

- Telegraphist Mohan Chetty is devastated to be posted to Bannu on the North-West Frontier instead of home to Tamil Nadu.
- His wife Veda Valli persuades him, through patient appeals to duty and tradition, not to resign his General Service post.
- A poor Pathan woman asks Chetty to draft a telegram to her migrant sons, urging patience despite the pain of separation.
- Composing her message -- which mirrors his own situation -- transforms Chetty's outlook on his own exile.
- Chetty writes home embracing his posting, quoting the Tamil saint Avvaiyar, and his wife is moved to tears of relief.

### No Dream, No Symbol
*By A. K. RAMANUJAN*

A short poem that catalogues nightmares and dreams drawn from literature and myth -- Dostoevsky's flogged-horse dream, Caesar bathed in Caesar's blood, a girl's nightmare of her father's severed head -- before turning to the speaker's own recurring vision of 'drowning softly' in the world's flesh, insisting that unlike the literary dreams it lists, his own vision is 'no dream, no symbol / but truth the ultimate nightmare.'

- Catalogues literary and mythic dreams and nightmares (Dostoevsky, Caesar/Calpurnia, an unnamed girl's nightmare).
- Turns from these borrowed images to the speaker's own recurring dream of drowning in 'the beating world.'
- Insists the speaker's own vision is not symbolic or literary but literal 'truth' and 'the ultimate nightmare.'

### Madura: two movements
*By A. K. RAMANUJAN*

A short two-part poem set in Madurai: the first movement observes the 'clench of a leper's crab-like claw,' moving as 'nothing in life can move'; the second turns to a temple dancer carved in stone, whose frozen gesture is described, in mirrored language, as moving precisely because it is unmoving.

- First movement: a leper's diseased hand described in the corrosive imagery of disease.
- Second movement: a temple dancer-statuette caught mid-gesture in stone.
- Both movements are built on the paradox of motion within stillness, using near-identical closing lines.

### Stagnation in the Bengali Theatre
*By P. C.*

A survey of Calcutta's professional Bengali stage arguing it has declined from the era of great character-actors (Sisir Bhaduri, Ahindra Chowdhury) into formulaic novel-adaptations padded with melodrama and misplaced song-and-dance, illustrated at length by a dissection of Tara Shankar Banerji's Arogya Niketan. The one bright exception is the amateur troupe Bohurupee under Shambu Mitra, whose disciplined productions of Tagore's Raktakarobi and Dak Ghar are praised as artistic achievements of the highest order -- though even Mitra's work meets with Bengali intellectuals' 'reserve,' and the professional stage remains hostage to the same commercial values, and recycled talent, as the Bengali film industry.

- Argues the Calcutta professional stage has declined from great character-actors to formula melodrama.
- Dissects Tara Shankar Banerji's novel-adaptation Arogya Niketan as typical of the confusion of ideas and lack of dramatic economy on the professional stage.
- Singles out the amateur troupe Bohurupee, led by Shambu Mitra, for disciplined, powerful productions of Tagore's Raktakarobi and Dak Ghar.
- Notes Bengali intellectuals treat even Mitra's acclaimed productions with reserve, calling his interpretation of Tagore 'heretical.'
- Attributes the professional stage's stagnation partly to its capture by film-industry personnel and economics.

### Tilak-A film biography
*By NISSIM EZEKIEL*

Nissim Ezekiel reviews Vishram Bedekar's forty-minute Films Division documentary on Bal Gangadhar Tilak as 'honest, simple and mediocre' -- competent enough to avoid the outright fraudulence of typical official documentaries, but banal in showing Tilak only as the conventional virtuous nationalist without artistic interrogation of contradiction. He faults Bedekar's own defensive framing of Tilak as an 'elemental force' like nature as false poetry masking an intellectual failure to grapple with genuinely conflicting historical interpretations, and criticizes the film's visual poverty: no memorable image, over-literal illustration of the narration (flames filling the screen exactly when the commentator says the country was 'ablaze'), and an actor used inconsistently to portray Tilak. He closes with a dig at Vasant Desai's 'carefully conventional' score.

- Judges the Films Division's Tilak documentary competent but banal: 'not incompetent, not meretricious, not vulgar' yet without artistic depth.
- Faults director Vishram Bedekar's own press-release rhetoric describing Tilak as an 'elemental force' as evasive false poetry.
- Criticizes the film's total lack of visual style and its over-literal pairing of commentary and image (flames on screen for 'ablaze').
- Notes an actor is used inconsistently to depict Tilak, undermining visual coherence.
- Closes by criticizing Vasant Desai's musical score as 'carefully conventional.'

### The Soviet Othello
*By D. G. NADKARNI*

D. G. Nadkarni compares Sergei Youtkevich's Soviet colour Othello with Orson Welles' earlier black-and-white version, judging Youtkevich's the more successful and coherent film. He praises its clarifying opening (told through Desdemona's view of Othello's exploits), its inventive visual symbolism (Iago glimpsed in a sword's reflection; ruined pillars, fishing nets and a wandering lamb used to evoke tragic atmosphere), and a virile, convincing Emilia, in contrast to Welles' more erratic, expressionistic 'camera-eye' style. He concludes that Bondarchuk's Othello achieves genuine tragic stature and that Youtkevich's stylistic consistency -- even if his inspiration is 'more Lamb than Shakespeare' -- surpasses the more subjective Welles.

- Compares Youtkevich's Soviet colour Othello favourably against Orson Welles' earlier black-and-white version.
- Praises Youtkevich's clarifying narrative device of opening through Desdemona's perspective on Othello's exploits.
- Highlights inventive visual symbolism: Iago seen in a sword's reflection, ruined pillars and fishing nets, a wandering lamb.
- Finds the Soviet Emilia (contrasted with Welles' Fay Compton) more virile and convincing.
- Judges Bondarchuk's final performance as achieving real tragic stature, giving the Soviet film the edge over Welles'.

### The Transfer of Power in India by V. P. Menon
*By S. NATARAJAN*

S. Natarajan reviews V. P. Menon's The Transfer of Power in India as a mainly factual record that withholds key documents -- notably the original Mountbatten partition plan and the British Cabinet's amendments to it -- leaving readers unable to judge the alternatives for themselves. He argues Menon, absorbed in official adjustments, fails to connect the transfer of power to the deeper trend of linguistic nationalism, which the reviewer considers the real threat to Indian unity (more than provincial autonomy ever was). He traces a 'Dutch auction' of ever-worsening constitutional offers (Simon Commission, Round Table Conference, Cripps, Cabinet Mission, Mountbatten) and criticizes Menon's uncritical admiration for the British-brokered partition, arguing the settlement merely deferred communal violence rather than resolving it, with troubling parallels to India's later dealings with the United States.

- Faults Menon's book for withholding key documents (the original Mountbatten plan and Cabinet's changes to it) rather than letting readers judge for themselves.
- Argues Menon neglects the role of linguistic nationalism, which the reviewer sees as the deeper threat to Indian unity.
- Describes a 'Dutch auction' of progressively worse constitutional offers culminating in partition.
- Criticizes Menon's uncritical admiration for the British settlement, given continuing India-Pakistan violence.
- Draws a parallel between Congress's earlier dealings with Britain and independent India's later dealings with the United States.

### The Ninth Year of Freedom
*By H. SOMJEE*

H. Somjee reviews the Indian National Congress's annual The Ninth Year of Freedom, 1956 as a dry catalogue of government achievement, useful mainly as a reference compendium of Union and State activity and statistics but hobbled by its refusal to evaluate what it reports, particularly on Foreign Affairs, Education and States Reorganisation. He argues a serious history of the ruling party's record requires three distinct tasks -- stating its objectives, assessing achievement against them, and analysing impact on people and rival parties -- none of which this exercise in 'mere reporting' attempts, regardless of unavoidable party bias.

- Reviews the Congress party's annual The Ninth Year of Freedom, 1956 as informative but purely descriptive, not evaluative.
- Argues the ninth year of freedom is historically significant for major economic-policy decisions (heavy industry investment, progressive taxation, community projects) rather than immediate achievements.
- Faults the book's silence on evaluation especially in Foreign Affairs, Education and States Reorganisation chapters.
- Outlines a three-part task -- objectives, achievement-assessment, impact-analysis -- that a genuine history of the ruling party would require.
- Notes such analysis is left undone by 'an overburdened Prime Minister and an equally preoccupied president of the party.'

### India, Democracy and Education by Jossleyn Hennessy
*By N. L. K.*

N. L. K. reviews Jossleyn Hennessy's India, Democracy and Education, a study built around fieldwork at the Birla Education Trust's Pilani campus, prefaced by Dr. Radhakrishnan. The reviewer approves Hennessy's practical prescriptions for good schooling -- small teacher-student ratios, boarding-community life, well-equipped campuses, room for independent thought -- but rejects the book's central thesis that democracy has flourished chiefly where peoples inherited Protestant Christian tradition and that such tradition is 'alien to India,' calling this philosophically shallow given, for instance, the rise of National Socialism in a Christian Germany and the living Indian institution of the panchayat.

- Reviews Jossleyn Hennessy's India, Democracy and Education, based on fieldwork at the Birla Education Trust's Pilani campus.
- Approves the book's practical educational prescriptions: low teacher-student ratios, boarding-community schooling, well-equipped campuses.
- Rejects Hennessy's thesis that democracy depends on inherited Protestant-Christian tradition, calling it 'flimsy.'
- Counters with the example of National Socialism's rise in Christian Germany and India's own panchayat tradition.
- Cites Pilani statistics: 6,238 students in 1952 across the Trust's institutions, 65% fee-funded, 35% Trust-funded.

### Approaches to God by Jacques Maritain
*By SANTOSH SEN GUPTA*

Santosh Sen Gupta gives a close philosophical account of Jacques Maritain's Approaches to God, summarising Maritain's 'sixth way' argument (from the self's simultaneous temporal and eternal self-awareness in thought to the necessity of an eternal thinking self, God), his restatement of St. Thomas's five proofs, his treatment of artistic and moral experience as further paths to God, and his concluding 'qualified agnosticism' -- that God's essence, though naturally desired, is knowable only by an act of grace. The reviewer judges it an able exposition but faults Maritain for never logically establishing the nature of God prior to arguing his existence, leaving the book's 'natural knowledge' of God weaker in certitude than ordinary self-knowledge, evident (if at all) only in mystical experience.

- Summarises Maritain's distinction between the 'way of proof' and the 'way of experience' (mysticism) as paths to knowing God.
- Explains Maritain's original 'sixth way': the eternal thinking self implied by acts of thought points to God.
- Covers Maritain's account of artistic experience (beauty as a 'mirror of God') and moral experience (right choice implying an objective good, i.e. God).
- Notes Maritain's concluding position of 'qualified agnosticism': God's essence is knowable only by grace, not by human faculties.
- Faults the book for not first logically determining the nature of God before arguing for his existence.

### Work and its Discontents by Daniel Bell
*By A. D. GORWALA*

A. D. Gorwala praises Daniel Bell's Work and its Discontents as a clear, engaging short study of alienation in factory work, noting Bell's prediction that automation will soon make the factory worker himself unnecessary, without offering an answer to what will then occupy human life. Gorwala contrasts the West's coming 'release' from joyless labour with conditions in 'the darker East,' where religion (quoting Luther on the housemaid, and comparing it to Hindu teaching on duty) may make monotonous work more bearable, and recommends the book to management, labour, civil servants and social scientists alike.

- Praises Daniel Bell's Work and its Discontents as clear, well-written, and stimulating.
- Summarises Bell's prediction that automation will soon eliminate the necessity of the factory worker himself.
- Contrasts the West's coming 'release' from joyless labour with the continuing necessity of monotonous work in 'underdeveloped lands.'
- Notes religion (Luther's dictum on the housemaid; Hindu teaching on duty/karma yoga) may make such work more tolerable in India.
- Recommends the book to management, labour, civil servants and social scientists alike.

### Atomic Weapons and East-West Relations by P.M.S. Blackett
*By P. C.*

P. C. reviews P. M. S. Blackett's Atomic Weapons and East-West Relations, endorsing its core argument that the efficiency of attack and retaliation has reached saturation point, making a further technological arms race pointless -- the real need being dispersal and concealment, not better weapons. The reviewer disputes Blackett's claim that Soviet nuclear parity, rather than politics, caused the post-Stalin thaw, but agrees the West should declare an economically and politically sustainable policy of 'graduated deterrence' rather than continuing to over-invest in superweapons at the expense of resources needed for aid to Asia.

- Endorses Blackett's central claim that the 'efficiency of means of attack and retaliation' has reached saturation, making further weapons races pointless.
- Argues the real strategic need is better concealment ('places to hide') rather than better weapons.
