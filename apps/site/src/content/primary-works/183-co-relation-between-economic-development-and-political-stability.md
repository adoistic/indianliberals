## Summary

M.R. Masani examines whether economic development and political stability are necessarily connected. He argues that neither term is unambiguous: stability can describe free democratic government or oppressive regimes, while development can mean broad economic welfare or growth achieved through exploitation. The examples of the Soviet Union, Communist China, and rapidly developing Asian countries are used to show that prosperity, poverty, freedom, and authoritarianism do not align in one simple pattern. The essay rejects any essential two-way causal relationship, but identifies political stability as a necessary precondition for sustained economic progress.

## Key points

- Political stability is valuable when grounded in freedom and democracy, but stability under tyranny or slavery is not desirable.
- Economic development should be distinguished from prosperity and from growth that impoverishes or coerces ordinary people.
- Prosperity may support political stability, but non-economic factors such as history, race, personality, and emotion can be more politically decisive.
- The cases of Kashmir, India's language disputes, Sumatra, East and West Pakistan, and Ceylon are presented as evidence that political conflict is often not reducible to economics.
- Masani finds one clear causal relationship: political instability prevents economic growth and progress.
- Both political stability and economic progress are treated as ends in themselves rather than merely means to one another.
- The essay advocates a free competitive economy, peasant proprietorship, limited state control, and a state that acts as an impartial referee.
- Masani calls for a flexible, popular liberalism drawing on both indigenous Indian traditions and international liberalism, with Gandhi as a central source of inspiration.
