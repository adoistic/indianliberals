summary: "Liberalism and Less Developed Countries (Gujarat University, Ahmedabad, 1982) is a memorial volume for the economist Bellicoth Raghunath Shenoy (1905-1978), edited by Mahesh P. Bhatt and Mukund S. Trivedi. It opens with a 14-page biographical sketch by the editors and then gathers fourteen essays: contributions from Milton Friedman, Henry Hazlitt, Gottfried Haberler, G. C. Wiegand, Antony Fisher, C. Lowell Harriss, George C. Roche III, Leonard E. Read and David D. Friedman, and from the Indian economists Subramanian Swamy, Gautam Mathur, M. H. Gopal and the two editors. The sketch, which the rendered pages show in full through page xii, presents Shenoy as an Austrian-trained monetary theorist, former Reserve Bank of India and IMF official, and first Director of Gujarat University's School of Social Sciences who, from 1955, became the lone dissenter against deficit-financed Indian planning, exchange controls, PL 480 food imports and the transfer of resources out of agriculture, and who was largely ignored by his profession in his lifetime. The surveyed text of the whole volume (OCR, not the rendered images) shows that the essays divide into three groups: general statements of the liberal case against planning, inflation and protection (Friedman, Hazlitt, Haberler, Harriss, Wiegand), tributes that borrow Shenoy's position (Fisher, Roche, Read, partly Wiegand), and technical pieces on Indian and theoretical economics (Swamy, D. Friedman, Mathur, Bhatt, Gopal, Trivedi).\n\nThe argumentative centre, read from the OCR survey, is the claim that market freedom, sound money and free trade serve poor countries better than central planning, deficit finance and controls. Friedman contrasts Meiji Japan with independent India to argue that ideas, not facts, govern policy; Hazlitt and Haberler treat inflation, protectionism and stagflation as products of government policy and market rigidity rather than of the liberal order; Gopal audits three decades of Indian budgets and recommends fewer objectives, an expenditure freeze and more room for the private sector; Swamy finds from 106 years of price data that agricultural prices lead the general price level in India. Other essays are further from the volume's title: D. Friedman locates a stock-flow error in Adam Smith, Mathur attacks the kinked-demand price-rigidity hypothesis, Bhatt weighs the case for an expenditure tax, and Trivedi tests inflation variability in the Indian demand for money. Pull quotes below are taken only from the rendered pages (the editors' biographical sketch), and per-essay summaries for the other essays rest on OCR text."

It traces his jailing in 1923 in Nagpur Central Jail, where he served Madan Mohan Malaviya, his studies at Benares Hindu University and then the London School of Economics, where Hayek's Austrian capital theory and the controversy over Keynes's Treatise on Money shaped him; two papers on the price level of investment goods in the Quarterly Journal of Economics (1931, 1933); teaching and currency work in Ceylon, followed by the Principalship of L. The core of the sketch is Shenoy's 'minority of one' stance after 1955: his note of dissent on the Second Plan panel against deficit financing, his 1957 presidential address to the Indian Economic Association urging a floating rupee, his analysis of overvalued exchange rates, import licences and foreign aid, his PL 480 studies (which, the editors say, forced the 1966 commission), and his argument that resources should flow to agriculture through the market rather than through planners' 'priorities'.     summary: "Dated 11 September 1975 (printed pp. 1-7, PDF 24-30). Friedman recalls a year of travel through Eastern Europe, the Middle East and the Far East and the contrast he saw between the facts (freedom and rising living standards wherever private markets prevailed, misery where central planning reigned) and the ideas of local intellectuals, who took planning to be the wave of the future. He pairs West and East Germany, Russia and Yugoslavia, Israel and Egypt, and Malaysia and Singapore with India and Indonesia, then builds a longer comparison of Meiji Japan and India after 1948. Both had able, patriotic leaders and an opportunity to borrow Western technique, but Japan followed a free-market policy modelled on Britain while India, led by Fabian-schooled men devoted to democracy, adopted five-year plans and controls and slid into stagnation and, recently, open dictatorial rule. He concludes that the difference reflects the intellectual climate of each era (liberalism in the 1860s, collectivism in the 1940s) and that ideas can live a life of their own only until they meet the test of evidence.     summary: "Swamy (printed pp. 47-73, PDF 70-96) applies spectral and cross-spectral analysis for the first time to 106 years of Indian wholesale price data (1861-1967, spliced from four official series) to ask which sector leads price movements. He sets out the Fourier-based method in some detail (Bartlett lag window, coherence and phase) and reports that Indian prices show the 'typical spectral shape' Granger found in other economic series, that more than 95 per cent of price variance lies in cycles longer than about 6 and 2/3 years (so the 'three-year monsoon cycle' is not significant), and that agricultural prices lead the general price level while the general level leads non-agricultural prices. His policy conclusion is that an anti-inflation policy that stabilises agricultural prices can control all prices, and that planners who dispute this will remain unpersuaded, since 'the last 30 years of Indian planning and inflation experiences have failed to persuade the Government of India of its errors'.     summary: "A footnote says the paper originated as lectures at Punjab University (printed pp. 81-106, PDF 104-129). Mathur critiques the price-rigidity hypothesis derived from the Sweezy-Stigler kinked oligopoly demand curve. He argues that the kink supports price stability only in a short period of general excess capacity with unchanged macroeconomic conditions; in a boom, wartime or an underdeveloped economy (limited capacity), the kink becomes a profit minimum and price stability is ruled out, and over a trade cycle the kink moves with the current price so no rigidity follows. He rejects attempts to force marginal cost to meet marginal revenue in the gap and proposes an alternative profit-maximisation condition, with an appendix deriving a critical value for choosing between two profit maxima. He faults Stigler, Sweezy and Haley for implying rigidity across the cycle and suggests that observed rigidities should be explained by other analysis, perhaps Rothschild's price-stability objective. # LIBERALISM AND LESS DEVELOPED COUNTRIES

## Summary

Liberalism and Less Developed Countries (Gujarat University, Ahmedabad, 1982) is a memorial volume for the economist Bellicoth Raghunath Shenoy (1905-1978), edited by Mahesh P. Bhatt and Mukund S. Trivedi. It opens with a 14-page biographical sketch by the editors and then gathers fourteen essays: contributions from Milton Friedman, Henry Hazlitt, Gottfried Haberler, G. C. Wiegand, Antony Fisher, C. Lowell Harriss, George C. Roche III, Leonard E. Read and David D. Friedman, and from the Indian economists Subramanian Swamy, Gautam Mathur, M. H. Gopal and the two editors. The sketch, which the rendered pages show in full through page xii, presents Shenoy as an Austrian-trained monetary theorist, former Reserve Bank of India and IMF official, and first Director of Gujarat University's School of Social Sciences who, from 1955, became the lone dissenter against deficit-financed Indian planning, exchange controls, PL 480 food imports and the transfer of resources out of agriculture, and who was largely ignored by his profession in his lifetime. The surveyed text of the whole volume (OCR, not the rendered images) shows that the essays divide into three groups: general statements of the liberal case against planning, inflation and protection (Friedman, Hazlitt, Haberler, Harriss, Wiegand), tributes that borrow Shenoy's position (Fisher, Roche, Read, partly Wiegand), and technical pieces on Indian and theoretical economics (Swamy, D. Friedman, Mathur, Bhatt, Gopal, Trivedi).

The argumentative centre, read from the OCR survey, is the claim that market freedom, sound money and free trade serve poor countries better than central planning, deficit finance and controls. Friedman contrasts Meiji Japan with independent India to argue that ideas, not facts, govern policy; Hazlitt and Haberler treat inflation, protectionism and stagflation as products of government policy and market rigidity rather than of the liberal order; Gopal audits three decades of Indian budgets and recommends fewer objectives, an expenditure freeze and more room for the private sector; Swamy finds from 106 years of price data that agricultural prices lead the general price level in India. Other essays are further from the volume's title: D. Friedman locates a stock-flow error in Adam Smith, Mathur attacks the kinked-demand price-rigidity hypothesis, Bhatt weighs the case for an expenditure tax, and Trivedi tests inflation variability in the Indian demand for money. Pull quotes below are taken only from the rendered pages (the editors' biographical sketch), and per-essay summaries for the other essays rest on OCR text.
