# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 44 (JAN./MAR. 1965; 'winter 1965' on the cover), edited from Calcutta by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta for the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom in Bombay, is built around a single argumentative centre: what happens to Marxism, and to the anti-colonial habits of mind Marxism bequeathed, once the theory is tested against either evidence or its own texts. Hans Daalder's long article dismantles the Hobson-Hilferding-Luxemburg-Lenin account of imperialism with trade and investment tables, then argues that the discredited theory still governs how the West, the Soviet bloc and the new states behave; Prabhakar Padhye reads the 1844 Manuscripts against Engels, Lenin and Stalin to show how a doctrine of the active, creative person became an apparatus of 'partinost' and denunciation; and two letters in the correspondence pages — from Richard Pipes and from Sidney Hook, both addressed personally to Padhye — dispute his reading of alienation on the spot, in the same issue that prints his article. Around that spine the issue sets M. P. Rege's diagnosis of why Indian philosophy never produced a scientific method, R. M. Pal's defence of Hamlet against T. S. Eliot, B. Mukerji's monist case that mind is simply a name for the functioning of the brain, Agehananda Bharati's field report on pandits who still regard Hitler as an avatar, and Lokenath Bhattacharya's admiring account of James Baldwin's anger.

The issue also carries three poems (Gieve Patel's 'On Killing a Tree' and two Vaisnava songs translated by Edward Dimock), two short stories, eight book reviews and four letters. Its reviewing is unusually inward-looking: A. G. Noorani reviews a study of Indian policy in South-East Asia and has his own book on Kashmir reviewed a page later by A. A. A. Fyzee; S. Natarajan reviews A National University, a book published by the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, which is also Quest's own publisher, without the relationship being disclosed; and a full-page advertisement on printed p.105 lists forthcoming ICCF volumes whose contributors include the journal's own co-editor and three people with bylines in this very number. The editorial voice appears once, unsigned but initialled '-EDITORS', to concede that a story printed in the previous issue was plagiarised and to ask readers to police the magazine's contributors for it.

## Essays

### The Indian Philosophical Tradition
*By M. P. Rege*

Rege opens from Sir Henry Maine's boast that nothing moves in the world which is not Greek in origin, concedes most of it, and then asks what use the Indian philosophical tradition can be to Indians who want a share in a universal intellectual inheritance. He sketches what philosophy has done historically — asking after the nature of the universe, criticising the presuppositions of the sciences, making concepts explicit, giving rational form to a conception of the good life — and measures Indian thought against each task. Early Indian speculation, he argues, is monistic and hylozoistic in exactly the way early Greek speculation is, and much of it is simply obsolete science; the Samkhya theory of gunas belongs in the same line of business as modern physics and psychology and must take the same test. What survives that deduction is real and considerable: the conceptual analysis of the Buddhist, Nyaya-Vaishesika and Vedanta critiques, and the moral portraits in the Gita and Buddhist literature.

The diagnostic half of the paper identifies three linked failures. First, method: Indian philosophy proceeds by commenting on a commentary on the sutras, which reduces thought to the explication of a truth already given, and a philosopher holds views because he belongs to a school rather than belonging to a school because he holds views. Second, epistemology: revelation, not reason, is treated as the primary means of truth, so perception swells to absorb the Yogin's direct apprehension of transcendental entities and the deductive ideal never forms — 'There is much fine critical philosophy in the Indian tradition but no rational metaphysics.' Third, ethics: the questions 'what is the natural or rational way of life?' and 'what is the rational constitution of the State?' were never asked, so a refined moral sensibility coexisted with barbaric customs it never had the rational leverage to criticise. Rege ends on the four resulting habits — conservatism, revivalism, eclecticism and anti-intellectualism — and notes, pointedly, that India produced no philosopher-scientist.

- Concedes Maine's claim that modern intellectual and institutional life is Greek in descent, while insisting the Indian tradition is the only other authentic and original philosophical tradition.
- Argues that much traditional Indian philosophy is superseded science and must be rejected on the same terms as Anaxagoras or the Epicureans, not preserved as simultaneously historical and still-valid.
- Identifies the commentary-on-commentary method as fatal to free inquiry: thought becomes expounder rather than discoverer of truth.
- Traces the absence of rational metaphysics and of a deductive ideal to the primacy of revelation, which also licenses a distinction between adhikaris and the rest.
- Holds that Indian ethical thought never generated universalisable rational principles, so moral intuitions never revalued established codes; Shankara defends ritual rather than discarding it.
- Names the resulting intellectual dispositions — conservatism, revivalism, eclecticism, anti-intellectualism — and observes there is no philosopher-scientist in India.

### Capitalism, Colonialism and the Underdeveloped Areas
*By Hans Daalder*

Daalder's article — sub-titled on the page, though not on the contents page, 'The Political Economy of ( Anti-)Imperialism' — is in two sections. Section I takes the economic theories of imperialism in turn and argues each fails as theory and as history. Rosa Luxemburg's claim that capitalism could only expand parasitically on non-capitalist economies founders on her own solution: if non-capitalist sellers are paid in goods, the effective demand she says is missing has still to come from somewhere, and her position collapses into Tugan Baranowsky's, whom she derided. Hobson's and Hilferding's investment-outlet theories mistake the lure of profit for its fact — returns on British colonial investment ran barely above domestic rates, and French investors lost two-thirds of their foreign holdings across the imperialist period. The business-cycle-regulator argument is tautological, since Zimmerman and Grumbach never separate colonial trade from extra-European trade generally. The Bauer-Hilferding theory of imperialism as the policy of cartelised finance capital is, he says, a typically German theory projected onto Britain and France, whose banking and industrial structures were quite different. Three tables anchor the case: colonial trade as a share of total foreign trade for five powers 1894-1934; foreign indebtedness of the main debtor nations in 1929-30; and per capita long-term foreign investment in 1929, headed by neutral Switzerland.

Section II is the part Daalder plainly cares about most: the theories are dead as explanations and alive as causes. Their legacy confuses economic with political factors, so political independence is over-credited with economic consequences and the social preconditions of industrialisation are lost sight of; it sapped Western self-confidence until guardianship felt like an imposition and Munich became possible; it let Lenin weld proletarian and national-liberation struggles into one formula and then left communists unable to explain Western prosperity after empire; and in the new states it supplies a scapegoat for the gap between expectation and fulfilment, making private foreign capital more suspect than state-to-state aid with strings attached. He notes a paradox: the Marxist diagnosis is coming true in reverse, since the withdrawal of imperial flags is what now makes capital fly and trade flounder.

- Rejects Rosa Luxemburg's underconsumptionist theory on three grounds and shows her extra-capitalist buyers merely relocate the problem she set out to solve.
- Uses returns data to argue capital export was 'not an economic necessity, but a psychological deception', with Marxists, imperial propagandists and investors equally wrong about colonial profits.
- Three tables show the main imperialist powers traded and lent more outside their empires than inside, and that the top per capita capital exporter was neutral Switzerland.
- Treats Hilferding's Finance Capital as a German theory wrongly generalised, noting Bismarck knew German colonies would be hostages to a British enemy in war.
- Endorses Robinson and Gallagher's documentary finding that the regions of Africa which interested British investors least concerned ministers most.
- Argues the discredited theory still shapes conduct: Western guilt, communist misjudgement of Western resilience, and a new-state suspicion of foreign capital that deprives those states of investment.

### The Marxian Image of Man
*By Prabhakar Padhye*

Padhye reconstructs Marx's picture of the human being from the 1844 Economic and Philosophical Manuscripts and then follows what became of it. Man for Marx is the working animal, distinguished from the bee and the spider by raising the structure in imagination before erecting it in reality; and it is precisely in the externalisation of that conscious activity that he suffers his fall. Padhye is careful about the order of causes: private property is the consequence of alienated labour, not its source, which lies in 'the external relation of the worker to nature and to himself'. He illustrates primary alienation with the poet who loses the effervescent experience of creation the moment the poem is written and can then be tempted to make use of it — an explanation he concedes is his own rather than Marx's. He then maps the Marxological quarrel over whether the later Marx continues or liquidates the earlier, noting that the dividing line cuts across the line between critics and defenders of Soviet communism: Daniel Bell and Adam Schaff agree that the young Marx is not the mature Marx, while Fromm, Tucker and Tomkins argue for continuity. Soviet and Polish apologists play the Manuscripts down, Padhye says, because read properly they indict present-day communism, whose 'community of work and of equality of wages paid out by the communal capital' Marx had already described as crude communism.

The second half is a history of a degradation. Marx used 'condition' and 'determine' interchangeably where the difference between possibility and necessity is philosophically decisive; Engels turned dialectics into a science of the general laws of motion and history into 'the conscious reflex of the dialectical motion of the real world'; Lenin read each into the other; and Soviet debate split into determinist and activist Marxisms, arguing over Pavlov, Freud and the withering away of law and of the school. Stalin's originality, in Padhye's reading, was to combine the activist view of the psyche with the party ethic of 'partinost', making the individual responsible for his own thoughts and therefore punishable for them — writers who keep silent must explain their silence, children must denounce defecting parents, spying becomes a socialist duty. The result was a population divided into the faceless and those with protruding faces, and a personality cult grown out of the worship of the mass. The young intellectuals of the Soviet bloc, he closes, are drawn to existentialism and to the newly published Manuscripts because both name the alienation their own official philosophy cannot.

- Recovers Marx's definition of man as the working animal and locates alienation in the externalisation of conscious productive activity, prior to private property.
- Insists private property is the consequence, not the cause, of alienated labour, quoting the Manuscripts against readings that reverse the order.
- Shows the debate over the young and mature Marx cutting across the pro- and anti-Soviet divide, with Bell and Schaff on one side, Fromm and Tucker on the other.
- Isolates Marx's interchangeable use of bedingt and bestimmt as the philosophical crack through which Engels's positivism entered.
- Reads Stalin's 'partinost' as the point at which a doctrine of the active person became an instrument for punishing thought, silence and even unconscious motive.
- Concludes that the Marxian conception of man 'lost itself in a morass' and that the revisionist potential of that question mark should not be underrated.

### Hamlet: The Culmination of a Tradition
*By R. M. Pal*

Pal's essay is a sustained rebuttal of T. S. Eliot's verdict that Hamlet is 'most certainly an artistic failure', conducted through a reading of the play's inheritance from the Kyd prototype. He begins with the first scene, apparently useless to the action, and argues it is where Shakespeare's method is visible: the wind-swept battlements, the unrecognisable comrades, Horatio's voiced disbelief are the machinery by which an audience's perception is lifted to a plane on which a trite revenge story can carry new values. He then attacks the critical habit of treating a dramatic character as a being with an existence outside the plot — Bradley's Hamlet who 'at any other time and in any other circumstances' would have been equal to his task, the biographical curiosity about the girlhood of Shakespeare's heroines. Eliot's charge that Shakespeare failed to impose the mother's-guilt motive on intractable material is turned round: if consonance between the hero's will and the plot's outcome were the test of dramatic excellence, Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides would all be exiled from Parnassus.

The positive argument is historical. Hamlet's procrastination is not pathology but the friction of a refined sensibility against a convention that had outlasted its social warrant. Pal rejects Harrison's defence of Hamlet as 'normal' by twentieth-century standards, because the play is not a twentieth-century plot and revenge in its most violent form was a moral obligation in the world Shakespeare built. Hamlet never questions the rectitude of what he is commanded to do; his difficulty is in bringing himself to do it, and the final killing is forced on him by accident, by the Queen's cry and Laertes' accusation, so that the aesthete conforms to the avenging pattern without betraying his own nature. Shakespeare, Pal concludes, was doing two things the tradition had never done: showing that the avenger destroys his own soul, and asserting the integrity of the human spirit against Fate — which is why Hamlet is the epilogue to the revenge theme rather than a failed specimen of it.

- Treats the Kyd prototype and ten years of stage popularity as limitations Shakespeare had to work inside, making the first scene the key to his method.
- Rejects the critical convention of granting a dramatic character dimensions beyond its representation, naming Bradley and Eliot as offenders.
- Argues that consonance of the hero's will with the plot's outcome is not a test of dramatic excellence, since no classical tragic hero willed his own retribution.
- Denies that Hamlet's delay is a depressive mania or a modern normality, reading it instead as a subtler response to a revenge motive that had lost its social validity.
- Reads Hamlet's final act as thrust upon him by accident, preserving his character while satisfying the tradition.
- Concludes that Hamlet is the deliberate close of the revenge tradition, in which Shakespeare examines rather than endorses revenge as a motive.

### The 'Natural Philosophy' of Man
*By B. Mukerji*

Mukerji, a cancer-research director writing from an address to the International Academy of Philosophy, argues that philosophy has held itself aloof from medical investigation and can no longer afford to. He surveys what the collaboration of chemists, physicists and mathematicians with biologists has produced: the DNA helix as the 'agent of destiny', whose permutations of base pairs account for the variety of the living world; the cell as a complex organism whose totipotential form, the ovum, already contains the pattern for the colour of eyes and hair, the four-chambered heart and the distribution of acid-secreting cells; and the brain, where ten billion nerve cells in a three-pound mass constitute what he calls the greatest enigma in modern science. On the brain he reports the then-new hypothesis that the highest level of integration happens not in the cortex but in the evolutionarily ancient reticular system, which filters the bombardment of stimuli and decides on one thing at a time.

The philosophical payload is monist and deflationary. He runs through the history of the soul's 'animal spirits' — Aristotle's heart, Galen's ventricles, Willis's grey matter, Malpighi's juice-like fluid, Galvani's twitching frog — and through the dualisms of Descartes and Plato, the Chinese denial of a surviving soul, and the Hindu and Buddhist doctrine of transmigration, in order to say that the mind-body problem and free will are now treated as semantic rather than real questions. He endorses Bailey's formula that mind is simply a name for the functioning of the brain, and Sherrington on the scientific unthinkability of an unconditioned event in the brain's series. He concedes that philosophers doubt whether compassion, love and 'soul' can be localised, and that the human capacity to say 'NO' may involve some 'extra-corporeal' attribute — but ends by grounding compassion in a million human experiences drawn from the environment rather than in soul or stars, and by declaring that there is hardly any need to place ourselves at the mercy of unknown spiritual forces.

- Presents molecular biology, cell biology and neurophysiology as findings philosophy must absorb, and dates biology's shift from cataloguing to measurement.
- Uses DNA's base-pair permutations and the totipotential ovum to argue that pattern and destiny are materially specified from the one-cell stage.
- Reports the reticular-system hypothesis of highest-level integration, displacing the cortex as the seat of decision.
- Narrates the retreat of the soul's 'animal spirits' from Aristotle and Galen through Willis, Malpighi and Galvani to their disappearance after 1800.
- Adopts a monist position — mind as a name for the functioning of the brain — and treats free will and the mind-body problem as semantic questions.
- Concedes an unexplained residue in the human capacity to refuse, and in extra-sensory perception, while denying any need for supernatural forces.

### Hindu Scholars and the Third Reich
*By A. Bharati*

Bharati, a monk-turned-sociologist writing from Syracuse, reports and explains a pattern he encountered repeatedly at Kumbha Melas and Vedanta conferences: orthodox Sanskrit pandits and monastic scholars hold Germany, and specifically Nazi Germany, in naive and outspoken admiration. He traces it not to the reformist channel — Vivekananda, Dayananda, Ranade and Devendranath addressed westernised audiences with whom learned orthodoxy had no communication — but to the diffusion of the news, as early as 1895-96, that Max Mueller had published the Rgveda after removing it from India. From that, he says, grew a durable belief-cluster: that the Germans 'somehow gotten hold of' texts unpublished in India, that they built their weapons on Vedic recipes, that 'Germany' means crest-jewel in Sanskrit, that Sanskrit is compulsory in German high schools, and that Germany leads the world in Indian studies. He notes that almost no Indian scholar, Narasimha Bharati included, knew Max Mueller never set foot in India.

Onto that, Bharati argues, Hitler's Aryan doctrine and the swastika fell as grist to the mill. He records Swami Svatantrananda at Allahabad in 1954 calling Hitler a mahatma and an avatar because he ate no meat and never married; Swami Hari Giri Mahar aj, a dasanami monk and active Congress Party member, telling a Vedanta assembly at Amritsar that the speaker had no right to abuse 'Sri Hitler and the pandits of Germany'; a 1942 Banaras Hindu University play, Duskrtavijaya, in which Berlin, Hitler and Goering are rendered into Sanskrit puns and London burns like Lanka; and a Tibetan lama at the University of Washington admiring a photograph of Rockwell's American Nazis as proof that 'the greatness of the symbol' had spread. Accounts of the camps and the exterminations, he reports, are met either with the svadharma-adhikarabheda argument that a perfected soul does not really kill, or with a flat refusal to believe Anglo-American propaganda. Bharati is explicit that modern Brahmin scholars of the calibre of V. Raghavan or P. V. Kane share none of this, but also that he has never heard one of them contradict it in a learned orthodox assembly. NOTE FOR EDITORS: the article transcribes admiring statements about Hitler and dismissals of the Holocaust; these are the reported views of the pandits Bharati is criticising, not the author's own, and his framing is consistently critical.

- Distinguishes learned Hindu orthodoxy from westernised reformist Hinduism and argues the German enthusiasm reached the former by a separate route.
- Dates the founding rumour to Narasimha Bharati's 1896 Allahabad announcement that a German scholar had published the Rgveda after removing it from India.
- Lists the resulting articles of belief, including the Sanskrit etymology of 'Germany' as siromani and the claim that Sanskrit is a compulsory German school subject.
- Reports that neither Hitler's dismissive remarks on India in Mein Kampf nor his refusal to shake hands with the Indian hockey team was known to Indian nationalists.
- Documents specific responses from named monks, an RSS flag bearing the SS Siegrune, and a Hindi play staging the destruction of the British Raj by Hitler.
- Notes two standard orthodox replies to the camps: a scriptural argument that the perfected soul does not really kill, and dismissal as Anglo-American propaganda.
- Observes that westernised Sanskritists are free of the pattern but do not contradict it in orthodox assemblies.

### James Baldwin
*By Lokenath Bhattacharya*

Bhattacharya's essay is a portrait of Baldwin as a writer whose subject is the political reality of colour and whose method is to begin from the awareness of suffering. He assembles it largely out of Baldwin's own sentences — the demon under the skin of his castle, the 'species of postnatal depression' about being from New York, the name Baldwin as a 'fragile handkerchief' hiding an irrefutable anonymity, the boast about hoeing cotton and damming rivers, the white policemen who left a ten-year-old flat on his back in a Harlem lot. He sets Baldwin against Richard Wright, who expatriated himself in Paris and lost first his country and then his sympathy for American Negroes; Baldwin also found Paris a city of refuge and spent nine years there but came back, on the view that the atmosphere at home had to be faced on the spot.

Bhattacharya then gives the biographical outline — born 2 August 1924 in New York, eldest of nine children of David and Emma Baldwin, a despised clergyman father, DeWitt Clinton High School, a profound religious crisis at fourteen — and a brisk account of the six books, treating Go Tell It on the Mountain as a novel of a Negro boy's lost innocence, Giovanni's Room as using homosexuality to approach a definition of the American character, and Another Country as crossing and re-crossing national, class, sexual and colour lines. He notes Baldwin's own judgement that he is a novelist primarily, and that the essays, which may one day seem dated, are where the resources he draws on as a novelist are most sharply visible. The essay closes on the passage about the relatively conscious whites and blacks who must insist on, or create, the consciousness of the others, and on the prophecy of the fire next time — with the remark that the hope lingers it will not come to that.

- Builds the portrait out of Baldwin's own formulations, treating fear and the 'strange monster' as equally important to his writing.
- Frames colour, in Baldwin's phrase, as a political rather than a personal reality, and suffering as the first step to the end of suffering.
- Contrasts Baldwin's return from Paris with Richard Wright's expatriation and loss of sympathy for American Negroes.
- Notes that Baldwin is not a pacifist like Martin Luther King and finds in struggle an element of terrifying beauty.
- Records Baldwin's insistence that Negroes must not do to others what has been done to them, since whoever debases others debases himself.
- Supplies dates, family and bibliography, and weighs the essays against the novels in Baldwin's own estimation of himself.

### On Killing a Tree
*By Gieve Patel*

A single-stanza free-verse poem in which the destruction of a tree is described as a procedure requiring patience and method. Hacking and chopping will not do it, because the bleeding bark heals and curled green twigs rise from close to the ground and expand again to former size; the root has to be roped, tied and snapped out of the anchoring earth so that the source, white and wet and hidden for years, is exposed. Only then does the scorching, choking, browning, hardening, twisting and withering finish the work. The poem's flat procedural tone, and its interest in what is most sensitive and longest concealed, carry an unstated analogy that the poem never explains; printed on p.59 at the foot of Padhye's page of footnotes on Marx, Engels and Stalin, it acquires a context Quest does not comment on.

- Presents the killing of a tree as a technical problem requiring more than violence to the visible parts.
- Turns on the distinction between the wound that heals and the root that cannot regenerate once pulled from the earth-cave.
- Ends in a sequence of participles — browning, hardening, twisting, withering — that enact the drying-out.
- Printed out of sequence with the issue's other two poems, on the same page as the closing footnotes to Padhye's essay on Stalinism.

### Aptaduti
*By Vidyapati*

The first of 'TWO VAISNAVA SONGS', attributed to Vidyapati and translated by Edward Dimock. Eight lines in the voice of an ageing woman who decorates her once-full cheeks with beauty-spots, paints her dying eyes with kohl and puts flowers in her burned-white hair, calling her own vanity absurd; the years have slipped away, her breasts hang empty and the fullness of her hips is gone, and on her withered body a drunken Kama wallows. The unlisted translator's note printed beneath it (catalogued separately as an unlisted item) explains that the classification of the poem as an aptaduti — a speech by Radha's go-between urging her not to waste her new youth — is the translator's speculation, since the collection carries no section identification.

- A short lyric of ageing and persistent desire, in the first person.
- Attributed by signature convention rather than by heading: the collection's standard signature line is 'Thus Vidyapati says'.
- Placed under a group head, 'TWO VAISNAVA SONGS', that the contents page does not print.
- Carries a translator credit to Edward Dimock that the contents page also omits.

### Aksepanuraga
*By Jagadananda Das*

The second Vaisnava song, attributed to Jagadananda Das and translated by Edward Dimock. A woman recounts her undoing at the Yamuna: the moon-apple of Nanda's eye lay waiting under the kadamba tree, and the honey of his look and the radiance of his body were bait and snare, so that her eyes lit there like birds and were trapped and her heart leapt like a doe into his nets, leaving the cage of her breast empty. ### The Electric Saw
*By Murli Das Melwani*

A short story set in a timber district in the north-east. U Sibmon Roy, the biggest timber merchant in the district, walks up through the pines on the morning his new electric saw is to start operating, and the walk becomes a review of how he got there. He had come from a distant village as a day labourer to the contractor U Rabin Singh, made himself indispensable, then deliberately went slack — telling his employer 'I know a lot about the world, but when it comes to business I'm just not fit for it' — so that no one would suspect the ambition underneath. The reputation for goodness, wisdom and thrift that the district credits him with is the surface of a long deception; behind his back they joke about his pumpkin face and his habit of wearing out one set of clothes before buying another.

The memory he keeps circling back to is the evening he drove back with U Rabin Singh and a few thousand rupees through a mist that closed over five miles of hill road. Within a few minutes the jeep and the body were at the bottom of the valley, the money was under a tree on the opposite slope, and U Sibmon Roy was lying on the road with self-inflicted bruises. The police believed the highway-robbery story, the case was closed after perfunctory investigation, the business collapsed under the dead man's brother-in-law because no accounts had ever been kept, and superstition kept other buyers away until U Sibmon Roy could buy it cheap. Melwani ends not on exposure but on rapture: the blade turns, whirrs and becomes a stationary blur, and the murderer's delicate tingling feelings burst and engulf him until he loses sense of everything and wants only to sink deeper into the ecstasy. The story's irony is entirely structural — it is the successful man's happiness, not his guilt, that the narration dwells on.

- A study of a self-made timber merchant whose reputation for goodness and thrift is the instrument of a long deception.
- The rise is narrated as deliberate under-performance: he stops taking initiative so that his employer will underestimate him.
- The murder is told in three flat sentences and treated by the district, and the police, as a highway robbery.
- The absence of written accounts, which he had once urged and then stopped urging, is what destroys the business he later buys cheap.
- Closes on the protagonist's ecstasy before the running saw rather than on any reckoning.

### A Friend of the Family
*By Jai Nimbkar*

A first-person story narrated over the course of one day by Liloo, a Bombay suburban wife who abandoned a Ph.D. in Psychology and fills her slack hours with extra coffee and unwanted phone calls. - Constructs a marriage in which the wife's demonstrativeness towards her husband is performed for the friend's benefit.
- Plants every clue to the real configuration in the narrator's own observations while withholding the inference from her.
- Turns on a wordless tableau at the front door rather than on any disclosure or confrontation.

### The Concept of the Secular State and India
*By S. P. Aiyer*

Aiyar reviews Ved Prakash Luthera's The Concept of the Secular State and India (OUP) under the headline 'The Meaning of Secularity', and reads it alongside Donald Eugene Smith's India as a Secular State (1963), insisting the two must be read together because they differ on the definition of the term, on their conclusions and in their documentation. Luthera's book was a 1959 Delhi doctoral dissertation published five years later; Aiyar remarks that time has not done too much damage to it, which is an index of the contribution it makes. Luthera's conclusion is that India cannot and should not be described as a Secular State, and that the Secular State ideal is neither desirable nor possible in Indian society. This rests on tracing the concept through European history to the separation of Church and State, first clearly demarcated in the First Amendment, and on the claim that such separation is only possible where there is an organised church — so that Hinduism, lacking a central organisation, cannot have it, and England, where the State regulates the Church, is not a Secular State either, but a third type, the jurisdictionalist State.

Aiyar grants the strength of the definitional position and the originality of the clarification, but presses the obvious objection: many terms in political science have come to mean something quite different from their origins, so why define this one so narrowly? He reports Smith's two grounds of disagreement from having read the thesis in manuscript — that separation of Church and State is too narrow a basis, and that Luthera takes a somewhat static view of Hinduism — and Smith's more comprehensive definition of a State which guarantees individual and corporate freedom of religion, is not constitutionally connected to a particular religion and neither promotes nor interferes with religion. The deeper difference, he argues, is of approach: Luthera's book is a contribution to constitutional law, Smith's an effort to explain Indian politics and society, taking in caste, communalism, minorities, education, reform and the missionaries, and tying the future of the secular state to the future of Indian democracy and constitutionalism — a larger aspect Luthera nowhere touches. Both have bibliographical limitations; Smith's is cast in the larger mould.

- Insists Luthera and Smith be read as a pair, since they differ on definition, conclusion and documentation.
- Sets out Luthera's thesis that India is not and should not be called a Secular State, and that the ideal is neither desirable nor possible here.
- Explains the argument's dependence on an organised church, which puts England in a third, jurisdictionalist category.
- Objects that political-science terms routinely drift from their historical meanings, so the narrow definition is a choice rather than a necessity.
- Reports Smith's manuscript objections — too narrow a criterion, too static a view of Hinduism — and his broader definition.
- Characterises the books as constitutional law versus political sociology, and notes Luthera never addresses the link to Indian democracy.

### Indian Nationalism and Hindu Social Reform
*By Suma Chitnis*

Chitnis reviews Charles H. Heimsath's Indian Nationalism and Hindu Social Reform (Princeton) under the headline 'Politics versus Reform'. She opens with her own argument rather than the book's: nineteenth-century social reform could draw on the Bhakti movement but is distinct from it, because the Bhaktas encouraged silent suffering rather than revolt, whereas the reform movement followed the pattern of agitation set by the Christian missionaries and grew out of Western education — which, as Chandavarkar noted, accomplished silently what no law could have accomplished by unsettling people's minds. The response took the form of agitation over sati and widow remarriage, of restatements of Hinduism by Vivekananda, Ramakrishna Paramhansa and Dayanand Saraswati, and of organised welfare work by the Brahmo and Arya Samajes. On the book itself she is warm. Where earlier authorities were themselves part of the current, Heimsath's detachment about the movement's brief successes and periods of apathy, and about how credit should be divided between individual reformers and the movement, is refreshingly objective. Her regret is that he could not discuss reform, and the lack of it, among Muslims and Christians. She ends by turning his epilogue's point about the Indian government's capacity to create economic change into a question of her own: whether it is not time to spell out the new values and behaviour patterns implied by the concept of the Dignity of Man we have accepted.

- Separates the reform movement from the Bhakti tradition on the ground that Bhakti counselled silent suffering rather than agitation.
- Credits Western education and missionary organisation, service and sacrifice with supplying both the stimulus and the model.
- Notes the movement's confinement to a tiny educated minority and its failure to reach the masses.
- Traces the schism between Ranade and Tilak, between social and political reform, as unresolved until Gandhi.
- Praises Heimsath's detachment against earlier authorities who were part of the current they described.
- Regrets the omission of Muslim and Christian reform and closes on the unfinished work of restating Indian social values.

### Essay 23
*By K. C. Lalwani*

A letter from K. C. Lalwani, opening 'Sir,', responding to Shashi Kant's 'Approach to History' in the Autumn 1964 Quest. Lalwani accepts Shashi Kant's complaint that there is a Hindu view, a Muslim view, a European view and a Marxist view of Indian history but none that is truly and dispassionately Indian, and that Indian history written since 1947 is still Europocentric — but argues that this is in the main the outcome of historical circumstances. The present structure not only of Indian but of world history is the handiwork of European scholars, and would have been basically different had it been independently attempted by the Semitics, the Dravidians and the Aryans; but no historical construction in the modern sense has come down from any of these earlier peoples, and any man is limited by the view of the dominant contemporary society. Only a superman with elements of mysticism could overcome the bondage of time and space, and such a man is hard to conceive actually undertaking the work, so history is bound to remain the handiwork of the most dominant culture-group of the moment.

The second half defends his own earlier thesis, 'A Model for Indian History' (Quest, Autumn 1963), against what he considers an uncharitable reading. His claim was that the Indus Valley civilisation was lost long before the dawn of Indian history at about the time of the Buddha, and that the traditional Aryans had by then outlived their genesis and growth phases, so that Indic society was already in the grip of stagnation at the point where Indian history begins. He insists that this does not mean nothing remarkable happened afterwards; on the contrary, the remarkable thing to him is the virtual stagnation of traditional Indic society, which continues in almost suspended animation without ever undergoing total extinction, while the actual governance passed in succession to the Nandas, Mauryas and Guptas from outside the traditional zone, then to the Kushanas and Huns, then to the Turko-Afghans, the Mongols and the Europeans. - Argues that no pre-modern people left a historical construction in the modern sense, so the framework was bound to be European.
- Holds that only a mystic superman could escape the dominant contemporary view, and that such a man would not write history.
