summary: |-
  Nimish Adhia reads Bollywood films as a barometer of India's ideological evolution. In the cinema of the 1950s and 1960s, he argues, self-sacrifice was a recurring and celebrated theme: in Mother India the heroine sacrifices endlessly, even killing her own son to protect her village's honour, and in Upkar (1967), released amid a food shortage, the film blames greedy farmers, traders and moneylenders and calls on them to work selflessly for the state rather than their own benefit.

  By the 1980s and 1990s, Adhia says, this valorisation of self-sacrifice disappears. Films instead depict individuals rebelling against the demands of family and community, often by insisting on marrying the person they love, as in Qayamat Se Qayamat Tak. Characters also engage in conspicuous consumption, with wealth openly flaunted; in the films of Karan Johar and others, flaunting money becomes almost a prerequisite for being a hero. Adhia presents these two eras as the polar ends of an ideological shift from socialism toward liberal, individualist values.
