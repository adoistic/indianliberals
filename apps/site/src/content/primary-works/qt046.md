# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 46 (July/September 1965; 'monsoon 1965' on the cover) is the Bombay quarterly of the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom under Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta, and its argumentative centre is a sustained, often abrasive inquiry into whether Indian society can carry a modern politics. A. B. Shah, the sponsoring committee's own Executive Secretary, opens with a paper written for a Seoul seminar arguing that 'modernity' and 'modernization' are not the same thing and that the dominant Hindu tradition is, in its Manu-codified form, 'so much deadwood today'. Nirad C. Chaudhuri, an advisory editor of the journal, follows with Part II of his anatomy of the 'opposed traits of the Hindu character', a piece of unrestrained cultural polemic that the issue's single letter, from K. C. Lalwani, then attacks at the back of the same number. Three further articles read the same pathology through institutions: Ranjit Chaudhuri on the displacement of intellectuals by a 'plebeian' Congress leadership, Hiren J. Pandya on the political executive's connivance in the public's contempt for the civil services, and Annada Sankar Ray (in translation from Bengali) on the language settlement, arguing that the Constitution has quietly produced 'a Hindi State'. Sisir Kumar Ghose reads Salinger as a failed Zen novelist, Dayal Saran Verma reconstructs Nietzsche's idea of progress as a moral aristocracy, and V. H. Deshpande, in the ARTS section, sets out to demolish the visiting Western musicologists' compliment that Hindustani music is 'spiritual'.

The CURRENT AFFAIRS section carries the issue's explicit liberal-Cold-War politics: M. R. Masani, general secretary of the Swatantra Party, delivers what is plainly a spoken address indicting Asian nationalism, non-alignment and autarkic planning, and Theodore Draper contributes nineteen printed pages — the longest item by far — reconstructing the April 1965 U.S. intervention in the Dominican Republic as a decision made against Juan Bosch rather than against Communism. Around these sit poems by Subhash Mukhopadhyay, Gauri Deshpande and Usha Rani (the last two appearing as page-fillers at pp.19 and 39), a self-translated Tamil story by Ka Naa Subramanyam, and seven book reviews under Nissim Ezekiel's desk covering Edith Sitwell's posthumous autobiography, two Nehru volumes, Gaudino's study of the Indian university, an Asian-Australian anthology, a Shakespeare number of Indian Literature, and a Harvard study of Henry James. Two unlisted apparatus blocks, 'OUR REVIEWERS' (p.113) and 'OUR CONTRIBUTORS' (p.116), complete the editorial matter; there is no shorter-reviews column in this number and no editorial plate anywhere in the issue.

## Essays

### Tradition and Modernity in India
*By A. B. Shah*

Shah's paper, submitted to a seminar on 'Problems of Modernization in Asia' at Korea University in June-July 1965, asks whether the Indian tradition is reconcilable with modernity, and answers by first separating two terms that Indian policy discourse conflates. 'Modernization' is a matter of civilization - literacy, urbanization, mobility, per capita income, an economy past the take-off stage. 'Modernity' is a type of culture, defined by rationality, plurality of opinion and of decision-making centres, secular ethics and respect for the private world of the individual. Industrialization, he argues, follows from modernity but does not produce it: Germany and Japan before the Second World War industrialized without weakening an authoritarian inherited culture, and Russia, Egypt, Ghana and Burma show modernization arriving with dictatorship.

Against that distinction he indicts the dominant Hindu tradition - world-negating, built on maya, karma and transmigration, with no concept of the rights of man and a tolerance 'rooted in indifference rather than in a recognition of the other man's right to be different'. He is careful to say which tradition he means: the framework codified by Manu, which he calls deadwood, not the protestant and life-affirming strands (Charvak, the Rig Veda) that he wants harnessed to make the transition less traumatic. His sharpest charge is against Indian intellectuals, who accept modernity only in their professional work while keeping 'two opposing sets of habit patterns in two water-tight compartments', and against the universities, national laboratories, Akademis and National Book Trust, whose failure he says is greater after Independence than under British rule and is not a question of money but of values, attitudes and the distribution of power.

- Distinguishes 'modernity' (a culture of rationality, plurality and secular ethics) from 'modernization' (literacy, urbanization, income), and argues the second does not generate the first.
- Dates Indian modernity to the appearance of the periodical press in Bengal and Western India 'nearly a hundred and fifty years ago', so the confrontation is not new and need not be as bitter as in the West.
- Locates the heart of modernity in the universities, and calls their post-Independence failure greater than their failure under British rule.
- Separates the Manu-codified Hindu tradition (which he says must go) from an 'area of indifference' (music, dance, painting, dress, handicrafts, festivals) that modernity can leave alone or even complement.
- Argues the West had advantages India lacks - four hundred years, colonial resources, and a Christian ethic available for harnessing - but also handicaps India is spared.

### Dichotomy in Hindu Life II
*By Nirad C. Chaudhuri*

Continuing a series begun in Quest 45, Chaudhuri takes two more 'dichotomies' of what he calls the Hindu character - courage against cowardice, stupidity against intelligence - and treats the recessive term in each pair. On physical courage he concedes a great deal: it exists to a high degree, but is emotional rather than reasoned, needs a high boiling point, bears no relation to bodily strength, and is very unevenly distributed by 'caste, breed, social status'. He reports that his own first military study, written in 1930-31 to rebut the British 'martial races' theory, converted him to it. Moral courage he says hardly exists at all: the Hindus have 'an abject fear of power', and the failure is worst precisely among those from whom leadership is expected - politicians, administrators, teachers, religious leaders and intellectuals - and has worsened rather than improved with independence, because under Swaraj the Swa, the Ego, has become infinitely more important.

The second half turns on intelligence, which he distinguishes from cleverness and from intellectual ability: the practical faculty of appraising a situation and meeting a crisis. Here he says the modern Hindus are more deficient than any human group in the world, and offers a catalogue of national misjudgments - the forty-year denial that a Hindu-Muslim question existed, the refusal of Irwin's Dominion Status offer and of the Cripps and Cabinet Mission proposals, the universal expectation of a German victory in both wars, the mishandling of the border question with China. What the intelligence does work hard at, he argues, is the maintenance of the myths that make those failures bearable; its function is to supply 'a cushion of insulation from all hard realities'. The closing image is of the Hindu intelligence as a retained advocate: the defence counsel for the Hindu way of life.

- Concedes high physical courage but calls it emotional, unevenly distributed by caste and group, and unrelated to bodily strength; reports his own conversion to the British 'martial races' theory while trying to refute it.
- Argues moral courage is effectively absent and is scarcest at the top of the social and official hierarchy, and that independence made this worse rather than better.
- Distinguishes 'courage of life' as endurance - fatalistic in private life, apocalyptic in public - and reads it as the reason Hindus neither fight resolutely nor compromise realistically.
- Separates cleverness from intelligence and offers a list of national misjudgments from 1929 to the NEFA reverses as evidence of a systemic failure of appraisal.
- Concludes that the Hindu intelligence is not a free agent but an instrument pre-set to defend a way of life, producing 'semi-consciousness and a good conscience'.
- The piece is a sweeping and deliberately offensive ethnic generalisation, written in the author's own voice rather than reported for criticism; it also prints a Hindi expletive as an elided triad.

### Indian Democracy and Crisis of Leadership
*By Ranjit Chaudhuri*

Under an epigraph from C. Wright Mills, Ranjit Chaudhuri asks whether democracy necessarily brings the dominance of mediocrity, and traces in India what he calls the displacement of educated and talented men from party leadership by a 'plebeian' leadership drawn from the lower rungs of society. He gives six causes. Independence created an enormous demand for administrative and technical personnel, which absorbed the educated middle class and left the field of politics vacant for those with no qualification for the services - producing politicians who, in Max Weber's terms, live 'off' politics rather than 'for' it. Universal adult franchise turned caste and community into the currency of local political competition, especially in southern India. Elite formation has gone wrong because competence and integrity, which Edward Shils makes the basis of political legitimacy, cannot be tested where corruption is the price of entry to the party hierarchy. Congress dominance has removed real competition. Illiteracy sustains parochial sentiment. And what Mannheim calls the 'proletarianization of the intelligentsia' has left the educated middle class economically dependent and politically subservient.

His conclusion is more careful than his diagnosis. The plebeian character of leadership is a crisis, but the intensity of the crisis depends on the dynamism of the system rather than on the character of the leaders. He points to the Congress having to recruit 'a man like Asoka Mehta' to supply intellectual content in the post-Nehru period, and to Nanda's anti-corruption venture being 'throttled to death by high level conspiracy inside the Congress', and argues that what is finally at stake is social ethics - a self-imposed, unwritten code sanctioned by human rationality rather than imposed by an articulate clique.

- Argues Indian democracy has produced a 'plebeian' leadership by absorbing the educated class into administration and leaving politics to those with no other vocation.
- Applies Weber's distinction between living 'for' and living 'off' politics and says Indian politicians predominantly live off it.
- Uses Shils on elite legitimacy to argue that corruption has become the entry price to party hierarchy, so talent cannot enter at all.
- Identifies one-party Congress dominance, quoting Rajni Kothari's 'The Congress is more than a party, it constitutes an entire party system', as the structural reason competition fails.
- Ends not on leadership but on social ethics, quoting M. N. Roy that good institutions cannot succeed where men have to suffer for being good.

### Administrative Reforms: An Attitude Analysis
*By Hiren J. Pandya*

Pandya writes into the moment of Home Minister Nanda's anti-corruption drive and the one-man Vigilance Commission set up on a modified version of the Santhanam Committee's recommendation, and his argument is that the drive is aimed at the wrong target with the wrong attitude. He first explains why the public despises the public services: democracy in an underdeveloped country raises expectations it cannot meet, promises are made 'with trumpets but without much seriousness at the time of the General Elections', and the citizen now meets a large 'Welfare' bureaucracy at far more points, with more levels of decision-making and more centres of discretionary power, which means both more delay and more scope for corruption. He judges the resulting public attitude 'not justifiable, but quite understandable'.

He then turns on the political executive. The leaders who had reviled the services before Independence left the machinery almost untouched and even entrenched its members' rights - a decision he attributes not to sympathy but to Sardar Patel's threat to resign and to the absence of any plan to reshape the administration. Since then they have at best connived at public antagonism and at worst accentuated it: he cites Jammu and Kashmir, where some hundred public servants were forced into premature retirement on suspicion alone, with 'no regular inquiry ... no charges framed nor cases filed'. His central objection to the Vigilance Commission is its jurisdiction: it covers only Government employees, leaving those in political authority outside, which tells the services that 'the target of this drive is not corruption or other weakness, but the services themselves'.

- Traces public contempt for the civil services to the gap between welfare-state expectations and capacity, and to the multiplication of discretionary contact points.
- Judges the public's attitude unjustifiable but understandable, and blames the political executive for not correcting it.
- Reads the post-Independence decision to leave the administrative machinery intact as a product of Sardar Patel's insistence, not of goodwill towards the services.
- Cites the Jammu and Kashmir premature retirements on suspicion alone as rash action in which 'all principles of justice and fairplay were thrown overboard'.
- Attacks the Vigilance Commission for confining its jurisdiction to Government employees and exempting political authority, and warns the drive has 'greater potentialities for harm than good'.

### Racing Backwards
*By Annada Sankar Ray*

Ray's essay, translated from Bengali by Lila Ray and modified by the author, reconstructs the language question as the story of a defeat - Gandhi's. He tracks Gandhi's shifting formula from the 1925 Sitapur Sammelan address through the 1935 definition of Hindustani as a language written in either Devanagari or Urdu script, the founding of the Hindustani Prachar Sabha, and the twin Devanagari and Urdu editions of Harijan Sevak, to the moment after Partition when Gandhi stopped both the Hindi and the Urdu editions rather than publish in one script only. The Hindi Sahitya Sammelan overruled him, Rajendra Prasad's casting vote in the Constituent Assembly settled Devanagari Hindi as the Union's official language, and a highly Sanskritized Hindi became, in Ray's account, the vehicle of revivalism and of the Arya Samaj and the R.S.S.

From there he argues the general case. Where one language among many is made official, its native speakers become 'members of the ruling class' and everyone else second-class citizens - the Belgian, Swiss and Alsace-Lorraine examples are his evidence, and he notes that German is spoken by over 70 per cent of the Swiss without any claim to superior right. He wants English naturalised as an Indian language, retained permanently for competitive examinations and defined by function rather than geography, with Hindi as first official language 'not the only one' and Bengali, Tamil and Telugu given equal status; there is, he insists, 'no such thing as a "regional language" because all our languages are national languages'. He takes the Tamil self-immolations seriously as a protest of principle, naming Sivalingam and comparing the movement to the Protestant Reformation, and he is unsparing about his own community: Bengalis' suicide 'will take place slowly, by degrees, not as dramatically'.

- Reads the language settlement as Gandhi's major defeat, tracking his Hindustani formula from 1925 to his suppression of both scripts of Harijan Sevak after Partition.
- Argues that a single official language makes its native speakers a ruling class and everyone else second-class citizens, and calls the Constitution's settlement 'a Hindi State'.
- Wants English naturalised as an Indian language and retained permanently for competitive examinations, with frontiers 'defined according to function, not geography'.
- Rejects the category of 'regional language' outright: all Indian languages are national languages.
- Takes the Tamil self-immolations as a protest of principle comparable to the Reformation, while predicting a slower Bengali decline.

### Sorrows of Salinger
*By Sisir Kumar Ghose*

Ghose begins from a question put to him in America - is Salinger well known in India? - and turns it into a sceptical assessment of the Salinger phenomenon and of what, if anything, Salinger has achieved. He is alive to the industry ('The "Salinger myth" is an active industry, at least on many campuses') and to the hostile case, quoting critics on the 'cunning and somewhat shoddy use of Zen' and on 'childhood lost' resolving into 'a half-mystical half-sentimental resignation'. His own reading places Salinger in the oldest tradition, that of the Quest, 'but in a disinherited age', and finds his characters - Holden Caulfield, Seymour Glass, Franny and Zooey - members of an 'Ancient and Honourable Brotherhood of Seekers and the Maladjusted' in which the dreamer survives but the dream dies.

He reads The Catcher in the Rye as 'the teenager epic' and Holden as divided between the heroic and the happy dream, the catcher in the rye and the deaf-mute in the cabin, with the division unhealed at the end. The Glass stories he treats as a family saga of attempted sainthood whose model, Seymour, has already killed himself before we meet him; Zooey preaches detachment while sitting in judgment, so the image of the Fat Lady 'fails to be a genuine symbol' and 'the parable is underweight, as Time said'. The essay's verdict is that the Zen experience the fiction speaks of has not yet become a felt quality in the action: the controlling idea does not yet control, and Salinger has still to write his Zen novel. Ghose measures the ideal against Indian terms - Brahmi-sthiti, Nishkama Karma, sarva-bhavena - and closes on an unattributed eight-line poem.

- Places Salinger in the tradition of the Quest 'but in a disinherited age', and reads his protagonists as seekers whose dreams die while they survive.
- Treats The Catcher in the Rye as a teenager epic built on an unhealed division between the catcher in the rye and the deaf-mute in the cabin.
- Finds Zooey's preaching of detachment contradicted by his judging, so the Fat Lady 'fails to be a genuine symbol'.
- Measures Salinger's ideal against Indian formulations of non-attached action and finds an imbalance between theory and action.
- Concludes that Salinger has yet to write his Zen novel - and that if he did, 'It would have to write itself.'

### Nietzsche's Idea of Social Progress
*By Dayal Saran Verma*

Verma sets out to recover Nietzsche the social philosopher from Nietzsche the poet-atheist, starting from 'God is dead; let us create new gods' and insisting that the injunction to remain true to the earth rules out any new god placed beyond man's creating will. He argues that Nietzsche's rejection of the nineteenth century's faith in inevitable progress was vindicated by the First World War, which exposed the absence of any correlation between man's growing control over nature and his moral development, and that what the age lacked was control by the moral will - the 'will' Nietzsche was searching for.

The substance of the essay is an insistence that the will to power be read psychologically rather than metaphysically: as an account of a fact about valuation rather than a metaphysics, which is what makes Nietzsche's irrationalism and existentialism intelligible. On that reading there can be no law of progress, because all knowledge is a 'world-arrangement' effectuated by the creative elements of human will, and progress consists not in the expansion of science and technology but in increasing the 'quantum' of will to power by rearing higher individuals. Verma is careful to interpret the higher man in terms of richness of personality - the capacity to harness antagonistic qualities - rather than self-assertion, and to refute the reading of Nietzsche as a supporter of state regimentation, quoting the Zarathustra passage on the state as 'the coldest of all cold monsters'. The conclusion is that Nietzsche conceives a progressive society as a moral aristocracy, and that the will to power is 'an urge for the higher life'. Verma notes in passing that many later theories of personality development were anticipated by Nietzsche.

- Reads Nietzsche's new gods as necessarily earthly, so that no transcendental or inevitable principle can be admitted.
- Insists the will to power is a psychological, not a metaphysical, concept - the move that makes Nietzsche's irrationalism and existentialism coherent.
- Denies any law of progress: knowledge is a 'world-arrangement' effectuated by will, so progress cannot be given an objective criterion.
- Interprets the higher man as richness of personality, the capacity to harness antagonistic forces, rather than self-assertion or self-aggrandisement.
- Refutes the reading of Nietzsche as an advocate of state regimentation, quoting Zarathustra on the state as the coldest of cold monsters and 'The better the state is organised, the duller will humanity be'.

### Nationalism and Regional Security in South & South-East Asia
*By M. R. Masani*

Masani opens from a remark by the Indian Foreign Minister, Swaran Singh, that Asian unity was being damaged by new ambitions and conflicts, and answers by touring the region: inter-Arab rivalries and the Yemen, the Arab states versus Israel, Pakhtoonistan, Kashmir, Chinese troops on the Himalayan heights (which he attributes squarely to 'the betrayal of Tibet by the Indian Government in 1950 and again in 1960'), the Naga ceasefire, Burma turning into 'a Cuba of the East', Laos two-thirds eaten up by 'neutralisation', Vietnam as a vacuum, Cambodia seeing threats only from Washington, and Indonesian aggression against Malaysia backed by both Peking and Moscow. His diagnosis is that the cause of this failure to combine against the threat from the north is nationalism 'of a rather superficial and foolish kind', arriving too late in history - he quotes E. M. Forster on India as 'Last comer to the drab 19th Century sisterhood'.

He then enumerates the forms this nationalism takes: neo-colonialism as 'a reflection of a subjective feeling of inferiority', non-alignment as 'a moral evasion', 'my country, right or wrong', and economic nationalism, which he says makes Soviet-type autarkic planning inevitable and starves agriculture in favour of steel. He quotes a Pakistani presidential adviser on the developing countries importing 'all the traditional diseases of Europe', and defends the proposition that genuine anti-imperialism must include opposition to the imperialism of one's own country. His prescriptions are a policy of interdependence, equity capital rather than government-to-government loans (because equity comes in at its own risk), aid tied to infrastructure rather than manufacture, and above all frank intellectual aid: he complains bitterly that visiting Western experts 'give a fine certificate to our governments', eroding the position of Liberals and democrats at home. He distinguishes legitimate Swedish or Swiss state neutrality, which he does not attack, from the intellectual neutralism he says Asian leaders have been preaching.

- Surveys the region from the Yemen to Singapore to show natural allies quarrelling instead of combining against the threat from the north.
- Names superficial nationalism as the cause, and argues the nation state arrived in Asia and Africa 'too late in history ... like a baby born after its time'.
- Calls non-alignment 'a moral evasion' and a theory of equidistance that inevitably takes the side of tyranny, while explicitly exempting Swedish and Swiss state neutrality.
- Attacks economic nationalism and autarkic planning for diverting capital from agriculture to steel, and welcomes the U.S. Congress's refusal of another steel plant.
- Argues for equity capital over government loans and for frank 'intellectual or ideological aid', complaining that visiting Western experts certify irresponsible governments and erode domestic liberals.

### The Roots of the Dominican Crisis
*By Theodore Draper*

Draper's nineteen-page reconstruction argues that the April 1965 crisis is not a separate event but a continuation of the 1963 coup, and that in both cases the Communist issue was a cover. He begins from the gap between American words and deeds - Johnson calling the pro-Bosch rising 'a popular democratic revolution committed to democracy and social justice' while U.S. forces patrolled Santo Domingo alongside the junta - and then supplies the history the official story omits. Bosch inherited the Trujillo armed forces intact and deliberately left them alone, knowing a purge meant civil war; he was in office seven months and knew of plots from his tenth day. On Communism, Draper shows that the charge preceded Bosch's presidency, that his party had disbanded its own youth section to stop MPD infiltration, that the Communists had attacked him for decades, and that the only name ever produced to support the 'infiltration' charge was Sacha Volman, a well-known anti-Communist whose New York institute was chaired by Norman Thomas.

He then tracks the manufacture of the 1965 case. On 29 April Johnson had reports of 'at least two and perhaps seven or eight' Communists among the rebels; by 30 April the CIA had supplied a figure of 55 or 58; after 5 May not a single one of the names was mentioned again in dispatches from Santo Domingo, and Newsweek reported that the embassy 'failed to convince the 156 foreign correspondents ... that "the 58" were a menace'. He notes that U.S. intelligence traced the beginning of Communist growth in the Dominican Republic to the period after Bosch's 1963 overthrow - 'a devastating commentary on the legend that Bosch was overthrown to prevent a Communist takeover'. His conclusion is that policy fashioned intelligence rather than the reverse, that the decision of 25-28 April was made against Bosch rather than against Communism, and that backing Wessin y Wessin was like the Allies backing Goering to save Germany from Communism.

- Argues the 1965 intervention continues the 1963 coup, with the same actors and the same pretext - 'Nothing has changed, not even the names.'
- Shows Bosch deliberately left the inherited Trujillo armed forces intact rather than risk civil war, and reads this as strength of character rather than weakness.
- Demonstrates that the Communist charge preceded Bosch's presidency and that the Communists had themselves vilified him for decades; the only name offered to support 'infiltration' was a known anti-Communist.

### Is Hindustani Music Spiritual?
*By V. H. Deshpande*

Deshpande's talk, given to the G. M. Vidyalaya's All Indian Music Teachers Conference at Jaipur in October 1964, takes up the compliment paid by Western musicologists at the February 1964 East and West Music Conference in New Delhi - that Indian classical music, unlike its Western counterpart, is essentially spiritual - and sets out to refuse it. He quotes Koellreutter, Junious, Meyer, Menuhin, Ashton, Stukenschmidt and Lutz, enters careful reservations that they may have meant something narrower by 'spiritual' than divine knowledge or self-realization, and then names what alarms him: none of the visitors except one spoke of the artistic values of the music at all, and Indians are already too fond of calling themselves spiritual in contrast to a materialistic West.

His argument turns on development. All music grew under religious protection, but the test of an art's maturity is how far it has cast off those crutches; if 'spiritual' means 'religious', the description is not a compliment but a confession of infancy. He traces the succession from dhrupad, dominated by devotional literary content, to the khyal evolved by Sadarang at Mohamedshah's court, 'which has obviously no religious or spiritual colouring'. He then divides the gharanas into an artistic group - Gwalior, Agra, Rampur, Jaipur, Patiala, Bhendibazar - united by structural beauty, melodic design and above all 'a sharp sense of "drama"', and a single possible spiritual case, the Kirana house, which he narrows further to its founder Abdul Karim Khansaheb alone, excluding even his disciples. He credits Abdul Karim's tonal trance while judging that his form 'suffered and became lop-sided', lacking 'the essential rigour of the rhythmic frame'. Against the visiting scholar who said dramatic tension is alien to Indian music he sets the concept of sama, the accented first beat at which each cycle's tension resolves. A Postscript suggests the Westerners may have generalised from the alaap-heavy instrumental performances of Ravi Shankar and Ali Akbar.

- Refuses the visiting musicologists' compliment that Hindustani music is spiritual, allowing exactly one exception.
- Argues that if 'spiritual' means 'religious', the description records an art still in 'a crawling infantile stage' rather than a virtue.
- Divides the gharanas into an artistic group defined by structural design and 'a sharp sense of drama' and a single spiritual case, narrowed from the Kirana house to Abdul Karim Khansaheb personally.
- Answers the claim that dramatic tension is alien to Indian music with the concept of sama, the point of resolution of each rhythmic cycle.
- Distinguishes meditativeness from spirituality: anything spiritual 'has to be employed spiritually', as with Haridas Swami, Meerabai or Thyagaraja.

*By Suma Chitnis*

Chitnis welcomes Robert L. - Follows Gaudino's method from the social setting - hierarchy, status, caste, scarce resources - to structures and then to persons.

- Finds hope in the UGC's willingness to reject 'higher education is a right for all' and in the absence of any attempt to control ideas.
