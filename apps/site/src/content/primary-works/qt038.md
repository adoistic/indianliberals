# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 38 (July/Sept. 1963; 'monsoon 1963' on the cover), edited by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta for the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, is the Bombay quarterly's post-1962 reckoning with communism. Its argumentative centre is the question of what the Chinese invasion has done to Indian non-alignment and to the Indian intellectual's habitual sympathy for the Left: R. Bhaskaran's lead article predicts a split of the 'so long happily united' intelligentsia into Right and Left and proposes a 'new Sanatanism' as the anti-communist's idiom, while K. A. Jelenski anatomises three layers of Polish revisionism, Leslie Bodi traces Lukacs's career as a case of philosophy captured by party dogma, and a five-hander symposium asks whether the Western and Soviet systems are converging. Around that spine the issue sets H. R. Pasricha's bleak, culturally deterministic verdict on Indian democracy, Mihir Sinha's revisionist history of the Brahmo Samaj, Punya Sloka Ray's audit of Indian linguistics, S. B. Sinha's survey of crime in the welfare state, William Phillips's New York letter on the middlebrow absorption of the avant-garde, a transcribed London discussion on African tribalism and Pan-Africanism chaired by Ernest Gellner, film and Hindustani-music criticism, six poems, Krishan Kumar's short story on widowhood and hypocrisy, ten signed book reviews, three unlisted shorter reviews and three correspondence letters, two of which are quarrels with Quest's own editors.

## Essays

### The Indian Intellectual & Communism
*By R. Bhaskaran*

Bhaskaran argues that the Indian intellectual is a recent, wholly Western import — a product of literacy and the printing press with no counterpart in ancient or mediaeval India — and that his instinctive 'progressivism' is an inherited fashion rather than a considered position. Because the revolutionary intelligentsia tradition comes from Tsarist Russia by way of Britain, and because the Indian variety combines a Western liberal concern for personal freedom with an old Indian aversion to violent action, the good Indian intellectual has become an ideal fellow-traveller 'up to a point', admiring Russian and Chinese achievement while declining communism in toto; Nehru is offered as the type of this eclectic 'majority' intellectual.

The Chinese invasion of 1962, Bhaskaran contends, has destroyed the third position. Neutralism's profits have run out, so the intelligentsia must now divide into Right and Left, which he welcomes as clarifying. He then sets out the handicaps of the avowed anti-communist — who will be called a capitalist stooge and a traitor, and who faces the entrenched beneficiaries of what Bhaskaran calls India's 'pseudo-socialism' — and his one advantage, the surviving moral vocabulary of Indian tradition. From that he proposes four principles: refusal of a perpetually rising standard of living as the goal of life, refusal of state monopoly over the definition of human values, assertion of every man's uniqueness as a natural right, and denial of the goodness of equality. The result he names 'a new Sanatanism'.

- Defines the intellectual negatively: we can be sure who is not one but cannot agree who is.
- Treats Indian intellectualism as a colonial derivative with no native tradition behind it, and predicts more than a generation before a non-Western Indian intelligentsia appears.
- Reads the pro-communist reflex as an intellectual fashion sustained by 15 years of government propaganda and a climate favouring the planned socialistic state.
- Argues that 1962 forced Indians into the contemporary world and destroyed the neutralist third position.
- Proposes four anti-communist principles, including an explicit 'denial of the possibility or the goodness of the principle of equality'.

### Three Aspects of Polish Revisionism
*By K. A. Jelenski*

Jelenski distinguishes three layers of revisionism at work in Poland. The first is the 'pragmatic revisionism of the rulers': Gomulka's 'Polish road to socialism', for which he was purged as a national deviationist in 1948 and which quietly disappeared as a slogan once Moscow conceded the realities behind it — private agriculture on 87 per cent of the cultivated area, a modus vivendi with the Church, and Khrushchev's oblique approval at the 22nd congress. The second is the 'heretic' or philosophic revisionism of the young Marxists of the weekly Po Prostu, whose editors began as fanatical Stalinists, coined the term 'New Class' before Djilas's book, rejected Marxist dogmas by testing them against historical experience, and were suppressed in 1957; its deepest expression is Leszek Kolakowski's insistence that historical necessity can never discharge a moral duty. The third is the 'orthodox revisionism' of Schaff, Lange and their generation, who modernise Marxist method while reserving to the Party the right to decide what counts as creative and what as liquidatory revision.

Jelenski's conclusion is that orthodox revisionism matters chiefly as a conduit — it launders the heretics' questions into forms Moscow can accept, and it is only through such men that Poland can influence Soviet thinking. But the genuinely new problems are always posed by the philosophic revisionists, and the same 22nd congress that opened the sciences has tightened the screws on literature: a novel withdrawn, the literary prizes abolished, Kolakowski's plays pulled after five days, and the liberal 'Crooked Circle' club closed after a debate in which Schaff argued that Poland enjoyed more intellectual freedom than the United States.

- Separates pragmatic (Gomulka), orthodox (Schaff, Lange) and philosophic (Kolakowski, Beylin, Strzelecki) revisionism.
- Reads Gomulka's 1949 confession about his attitude to the USSR as evidence that the CPSU, not Gomulka, has since moved.
- Portrays Po Prostu as internationally minded and eclectic — Mayakovsky, jazz, Steinbeck, existentialist psychoanalysis — and credits its writers with inventing 'New Class'.
- Identifies the essential difference between orthodox and philosophic revisionism as the question of who decides the limits: the Party, or not.
- Notes that scientific liberalisation after the 22nd congress coincided with renewed literary censorship, ending with the closure of the 'Crooked Circle'.

### The Problem of Value in Recent Political Theory
*By A. H. Somjee*

Somjee sets out to show that the long controversy over 'the relevance of science to values' in political theory has been barren, and to restate the problem. He traces the framework from Simmel and Rickert through Max Weber — who equated the value problem with means and ends and remained ambivalent, granting science a free hand over means and a hesitant one over ends — and then through those who worked inside Weber's frame: Charles Beard and George Sabine, who concluded that no social inquiry can be morally neutral, and David Easton, who took it as given that every moral frame of reference conditions empirical research. Against these he sets Dewey and Catlin, who tried to dissolve or redefine value, and Kaufmann and Brecht, who tried to give value-terms an objective base through agreed axiological rules or an intersubjective common denominator. All of them, he argues, remained prisoners of the positivism/anti-positivism quarrel.

His own proposal is to abandon the epistemological question for an operational one: under what conditions do values actually enter an inquiry? He introduces the term 'value-situation' and argues that such situations are not produced by the subject-matter, nor by the analyst's professed or unprofessed values, nor by the values of his social milieu, but by the interaction of three groups of factors — the dominating motive of the inquiry (of which he distinguishes six), the psychologico-intellectual quality of the assertion guiding it, and the testifiability of warranted assertions. A hunch has no value-source; a fully intellectualised hypothesis exposed to public testing has none either; it is the middle range, where assertions rest on a moral frame and cannot be publicly communicated or refuted, that generates value-situations. The consequence he draws is that value-free situations are possible even in the study of political phenomena.

- Argues the science-and-values controversy has obscured rather than clarified the problem of value in political theory.
- Reads Weber as the bridge and the source of the later rift, his treatment 'ambivalent' between scientific method and the will as the source of final axioms.
- Groups Dewey and Catlin as trying to do without value, Kaufmann and Brecht as trying to objectify it.
- Substitutes the operational concept of the 'value-situation' for the epistemological question.
- Concludes that value-free situations are possible in political study, since only some assertions carry value-quality.
- Closes with a 20-item reference list on printed p.30 citing Myrdal, Weber, Beard, Easton, Dewey, Catlin, Kaufmann, Brecht and Popper.

### Prospects of Democracy in India
*By H. R. Pasricha*

Pasricha argues that democracy, being Greco-Roman in concept and pragmatic in outlook, was transplanted into Asian and African soil without the stages that produced it in the West, and that in India the genius of the people refuses it. His explanation is frankly culturalist and, in places, openly contemptuous: Indian abstract thought has produced a personality 'steeped in self, vain and egotistical'; preoccupation with the eternal makes present suffering a purifier rather than a problem; feudal conceptions of serfdom survive, which he illustrates with anecdotes from his own medical practice; and he asserts that a hot climate has degraded labour and that Indian minds are handicapped in empirical science. He quotes Lord Birkenhead approvingly: 'You cannot distil Western wine into Eastern bottles'.

The constitutional half of the argument compares the Indian Republic with Weimar (printed throughout as 'Weimer'), which he calls the most liberal constitution of its kind and which died of proportional representation and presidential decree. India's equivalent 'built-in device of self-destruction' is adult franchise: conferred on an illiterate electorate and worked by a government holding vast economic power, it becomes a weapon for perpetuating one-party rule, so that India may already be under a dictatorship of the proletariat run by men determined to rise into the capitalist class. He closes on the political class — men without vocation or profession, hostile to the better-equipped, unable to resist 'filthy lucre' — and concludes that neither people nor leadership will exert itself for democracy, leaving its fate 'anybody's guess'. A closing note adds that uncontrolled population growth widens 'the sector of hungry unemployables who gravitate easily to communism'.

- Treats democracy as a Greco-Roman inheritance whose absence of pragmatic culture in India is decisive.
- Explains Indian backwardness in science and the low status of labour by inherited thought-patterns and climate — assertions made in the author's own voice.
- Draws a sustained parallel between the Weimar and Indian Republics, including the combination of cabinet government with a strong president.
- Identifies adult franchise as the Indian constitution's self-destructive device and suggests one-party dictatorship may already exist.
- Indicts the political leadership as unqualified, self-abasing before leaders of 'revolting personality', and corrupt.
- Appears in the issue alongside a contributors note identifying Pasricha as 'a leading member of the Swatantra Party' — relevant to the Aiyar correspondence in the same issue about writers with party preferences.

### George Lukacs
*By Leslie Bodi*

Bodi, lecturing to Australian philosophers, presents Lukacs as the case-history of a great modern thinker inside a closed ideological system. He tracks the early work — Die Seele und die Formen, the Hungarian history of modern drama, and The Theory of the Novel, read as a sophisticated 'Paradise Lost' in which the novel expresses man's transcendental homelessness — then the turn to Marxism, the Commissarship in the 1919 Hungarian Soviet Republic, and History and Class Consciousness, which fused Hegel's self-alienation of spirit with Marx's alienation of labour and became 'the profession of faith of an aesthetic Humanist turned Marxist'. That book also ended his standing as an official Party spokesman: 'Lukacsism' became a term of abuse, and the Blum Theses were denounced as rightist deviation.

The centre of the essay is a close reading of The Historical Novel, whose account of Scott's 'middle-of-the-road hero' Bodi calls brilliant while noting a second, speculative explanation of the same device offered in the same paragraph, and whose comparison of drama and epic he rates Lukacs's method at its best. The second half of the book, by contrast, shows Party dogma interfering: bourgeois 'decadence' after 1848, progressive form in Rolland and Mann. Bodi diagnoses the 'eery abstraction' Thomas Mann felt in Lukacs as the consequence of working with absolute categories that cannot be discussed — Lenin's theory of reflection, the ambivalent 'typical' — and credits him with the theory of the 'fundamental bias' of great art, by which the reactionary Scott may write better historical novels than a progressive contemporary. The narrative closes with the 1956 rehabilitation, the ministry under Imre Nagy, deportation to Rumania, and Lukacs at 77, having finished his Aesthetics and begun his Ethics.

- Presents Lukacs's whole career as a contradiction between the highest standards of European aesthetics and the closed system of Party doctrine.
- Notes that only two of his major works then existed in English, evidence of slow Anglo-Saxon reception.
- Reads The Historical Novel as strongest on genre theory and weakest where post-1848 'decadence' is being demonstrated.
- Explains the 'fundamental bias' theory via Engels on Balzac, and its corollary that Scott may outwrite progressive novelists.
- Retells the parable of the rabbit on the Himalayas and the elephant as Lukacs's answer to the dogma of automatic Soviet literary superiority.
- Closes with an anonymous New Statesman verdict on his survival; the essay carries a headnote ('Based on a lecture given in June 1962 to the Melbourne University Philosophical Society') and a bibliography.

### Brahmoism in Retrospect
*By Mihir Sinha*

Mihir Sinha offers a deliberately anti-hagiographic social history of the Brahmo Samaj. His opening move is to invert the usual account: Ram Mohan Roy did not found a movement so much as the movement gave him his historical importance, since significant social movements 'are never dependent on the fortuitous presence of a leader'. A footnote insists that Ram Mohan was a social thinker rather than a religious leader like Chaitanya, and warns the historian against hero-worship. The Atmiya Sabha and Brahmo Sabha are then read as the ideological arm of a new commercial aristocracy, created by trade with foreign interests and pushed by British policy and the Sale Law into land, competing for the leadership of Calcutta's rising middle class against Radhakanto Deb's Dharma Sabha on one side and Derozio's Academic Association radicals on the other.

The decline is traced through personalities and organisations: Debendranath Tagore, an introvert absorbed in spiritual speculation, abolished the Tatwabodhini Sabha in 1859 and built a superstructure of ritual, then joined Radhakanto Deb in the British Indian Association — for Sinha, the end of the original movement. Keshab Chandra Sen's career from firebrand missionary to the man-god cult and alignment with the Ramakrishna revival produced the split into Adi and Bharatbarshiya Samajes (1866) and then the Sadharan Brahmo Samaj (1878). Sinha's verdict is that the movement's real failure was not its inability to reform Hinduism but 'its subsequent success as a sect of reformed Hindus': having supplied the national movement with an educational and cultural basis, the Samaj has been relegated to one sect among many, its present members living off an older generation's pioneering.

- Reverses the conventional causal story: the movement made Ram Mohan Roy historically important, not the other way round.
- Reads Brahmoism as the ideological self-fashioning of a new trade-derived Calcutta aristocracy rather than as a religious awakening.
- Places the Brahmo Sabha between Dharma Sabha conservatism and Derozian radicalism as three answers to the same historical pressure.
- Identifies Debendranath Tagore's ritualism and his alliance with Radhakanto Deb as the point of no return.
- Treats the successive schisms of 1866 and 1878 as completing a full circle, with intellectual leadership lost each time.

### Linguistics in India
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

Ray calls his article 'a one-man report on the state of the scientific study of languages in India' and answers the sceptic's question — why study linguistics at all — with four arguments: literature departments are built for aesthetic appreciation, not for teaching practical command of a language to engineers, colonels and officials; small distortions of information now cost votes or military disasters, and modern campaigning requires real communication with local people; language learning is not a common-sense skill picked up by gesture; and modern linguistics has become a full discipline, as demanding as a physical science.

He then paints the situation harshly. Fewer than two dozen Indian scholars would be considered competent by modern standards, most of them without inner drive; the one-professor-per-department rule lets a single ageing eminence dam a field for a lifetime; the government anthropology department is interested in folk-dances and ruined temples and has about one competent linguist in its entire cadre, with nothing in its files more recent than Grierson's survey. He offers shock illustrations: officials who take a year to learn simple greetings, Russians in Calcutta speaking Bengali like natives, Chinese intelligence networks in border areas, an MA whose paper is returned from abroad as unintelligible. Equipment, he notes, is 'ridiculously inexpensive' — a tape recorder, tapes, and perhaps Rs. 500 a year of books. The Deccan College at Poona is the only training place worth naming, and it lacks staff in Tibeto-Burman languages, general syntax, suprasegmental phonology and monolingual field methodology while fighting students who claim degrees as a political right. He ends with six ranked priorities — structural descriptions of the leading spoken languages, contrastive presentations against English and Hindi, bilingual dictionaries for learners, a central pool of new technical terms, descriptions of strategically important tribal languages, and area manuals — while conceding some scope to pure science.

- Frames linguistics as a strategic and administrative necessity as much as a scholarly one, citing votes and military disaster.
- Puts the number of competent Indian linguists at under two dozen and blames the single-professor department structure for stagnation.
- Indicts the government anthropology department for antiquarian interests and for relying on Grierson's survey.
- Argues the material cost of competent linguistic work is trivial; the real costs are field trips and office-keeping.
- Names Deccan College, Poona as the only real training centre and itemises its gaps, including a war with students claiming degrees as a political right.
- Sets out six priorities headed by structural descriptions of prestige spoken dialects, with tribal languages of sensitive border areas included.

### Crime in a Welfare State
*By S. B. Sinha*

S. B. Sinha, a retired prisons superintendent and former Indian Medical Service officer, asks how far crime can be averted by education and improved living conditions in a welfare state. His premise is that nobody is born a criminal — 'the chromosomes do not transmit criminality' — so that the parents, the childhood environment and education form the triad that makes or mars character, and a welfare state owes the working individual a decent home and economic security as well as schooling. He classifies crimes against persons and property and describes the older pattern of 'emotional' crime arising from caste, village and zamindar-ryot feuds and from deviation from sexual morality, including a case reported by a Chief Judge of the Oudh High Court in which a farmer killed his nine-year-old daughter over an unmade breakfast.

The bulk of the article is an indictment of post-independence India. Crime has risen through Partition's uprooting, linguistic-reorganisation agitations, unemployment following the population explosion, migration to cities and partial prohibition; separatist demands for Vidarbha, Sikhistan, Nagaland and Dravidland are named as continuing sources of violence. The administration was weakened by the departure of senior British officers and by ministers who interfered, practised nepotism and 'shifarrish', and abandoned the Mahatma's advice to live in huts; Sinha judges 1957-62 worse than 1952-57 because of Congress group rivalries. Partial prohibition and anti-prostitution laws, he argues, have spread the evils they attacked; the beggars, bootleggers, prostitutes and wandering sadhus are the recruiting ground of professional crime; and roughly 10 per cent of the jail population is diseased, idiot, imbecile or mentally underdeveloped, whose incarceration he calls inhuman. The failure he names last is the absence of any comprehensive crime policy at all.

- Rejects innate criminality and locates crime in family, environment and education, making housing and economic security welfare-state duties.
- Distinguishes older 'emotional' crime from the new white-collar and gentleman crime produced by educated unemployment, with a catalogue of modi operandi.
- Gives the number of unemployed as nearly 10 million and links unemployment directly to crime.
- Blames partial prohibition and anti-prostitution measures for creating rackets and driving the evils underground.
- Argues that about 10 per cent of the prison population is mentally disabled or diseased and should be treated, not jailed.
- Attributes administrative decline both to the loss of experienced British officers and to ministerial interference and corruption.

### Letter from New York
*By William Phillips*

Phillips reports a becalmed New York. After the Cuban crisis and during the newspaper strike, little seems to be happening: there is a stalemate at home and abroad, extremes of action or thought are left to lunatics who are not even interesting, and the war scare has turned into its opposite, taken as final proof that war is out of the question. He reads Republican attacks on Kennedy for softness on Russia as a domestic necessity rather than a foreign policy, notes that toughness is always the posture of the party out of power, and observes that the lesson about Latin America — that only a democratic socialism which wipes out backwardness and corruption can stop the Russian brand of communism — is being learned purely abstractly, with no formula for building such movements against both conservative and communist opposition.

The cultural half of the letter is an argument about the apparent collapse of the line between serious and popular audiences. The New Yorker has run Hannah Arendt on Eichmann, Harold Rosenberg as art critic and James Baldwin on the Negro question; Nugget and Playboy have found a formula mixing sex and culture; Esquire prints Macdonald and Mailer; even the women's magazines and the Saturday Evening Post have gone in for intellectual fashions. Phillips counts himself among the traditionalists who view this with alarm, because its effect is to promote the chic and the slick, to turn ideas into sensations and writers into celebrities, with money rather than the arts driving the change; the Baldwin piece is his test case, its excitement 'took care of' readers' guilt. Recent fiction is mostly slick — Roth accomplished but feeding on himself, Updike's early books 'outstanding examples of undergraduate prose', Baldwin's Another Country crude in style and conventional in its sexual permutations — and Albee's Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf 'converts the living room into an unused bedroom'.

- Diagnoses a non-political mood in which brinkmanship has become a defensive weapon for keeping the stalemate.
- Reads party positions on Cuba and Russia as functions of being in or out of power rather than of foreign policy.
- Identifies the breakdown of serious/popular distinctions as more apparent than real, and driven by money.
- Uses the reception of Baldwin's New Yorker essay to argue that the excitement of reading substituted for responsibility.
- Judges current American fiction and theatre as technically proficient and safe, mixing sex, social consciousness and an experimental look.
- Printed with the line '(Exclusive to Quest)' at its close.

### Tribalism and Modern Political Developments in Africa
*By Masika, Asfaw Damte and Charles Patterson*

This is a transcribed discussion, printed under CURRENT POLITICS, between Masika (a Ugandan research student in London), Asfaw Damte (an Ethiopian economist at Cambridge) and Charles Patterson (an American research fellow from Berkeley), chaired by Ernest Gellner, who is named only in the bracketed headnote. Gellner opens by distinguishing three senses of 'tribe' — the small kin-based village unit that performs state functions in pre-industrial societies; the cultural-linguistic group, which may number millions; and the loose use of the word for any traditional African political organisation, including large states and empires. Damte accepts the second; Masika observes that the first is what anthropologists call a clan.

The argument then turns on Pan-Africanism. Patterson suggests it is a 'tribal mystique' writ large, an extension of common culture to a continental scale. Damte rejects any tribal or racial basis — all who live in Africa and consider themselves African, 'whether red or white', are raw material for a Pan-African state — and grounds the case in size: small, poor, backward states cannot survive beside the United States, the Soviet Union and the European Common Market. Gellner presses the practical difficulties: perpetuating small cultures obstructs a mobile modern economy, as India's linguistic diversity shows; who holds the membership card in a Pan-African union, the individual, the inherited colonial state or the tribe; and is tribal membership a legitimate ground for redrawing State Y's boundaries? Masika replies that Africans will not encourage what cannot survive modern conditions, illustrates the language problem with Swahili's acceptance in Kenya, Tanganyika and Zanzibar but not Uganda, and says the state is seen as the colonial power's immediate successor while Pan-Africanism 'will be our own creation'. Gellner's retort is that Pan-Africanism is popular precisely because it does not yet exist.

- Three distinct senses of 'tribe' are separated before any political argument is allowed to proceed.
- Damte argues the case for continental union from the economics of scale, not from race or culture.
- Gellner raises the membership question — individual, inherited state, or tribe — as the practical test of tribal political legitimacy.
- India's linguistic diversity is used as the comparative case for the costs of cultural pluralism in a modern economy.
- Masika reports that Swahili's spread is acceptable partly because 'the Swahili are a dying race', adoption of another living tribe's language implying inferiority.
- The Monrovia/Casablanca split is noted as a setback already suffered by Pan-Africanism.

### Atomic Operas
*By M. S. Dworkin*

Dworkin argues that the dramatisation of atomic holocaust has become an established genre with its connoisseurs, and that this is more dangerous than indifference: once dooms become a genre, prophecy is put up for sale, and the shock administered in a theatre carries its own catharsis, providing conventional substitutes for thought and action. His test cases are two films. The Day the Earth Caught Fire, written by Wolf Mankowitz with Val Guest, earns full marks for pro-survivalism, and its insistence on realism — shot in the Daily Express office with Arthur Christiansen playing himself — is meant to sharpen the fantasy of an earth knocked out of orbit by simultaneous Russian and American tests. But the newspaper milieu, Dworkin argues, lends a Sunday-supplement tinge to the science and associates two kinds of sensationalism through their shared familiarity; its imagery of a parched Thames and empty streets repeats an already established pictograph, after On the Beach and The World, the Flesh and the Devil.

Panic In Year Zero, directed by Ray Milland from a screenplay by Jay Simms and John Morton, is read as a colder and more corrosive fantasy: the vacationing family that sees the cloud over Los Angeles and retreats to a cave enacts the 'What would you do if...' daydream, with the mother standing four-square for culture against the suspicions, fortifications and violences the father demonstrates to be necessary, and with father and son finally blasting the punks who have raped 'the family virgin' — the hallowed movie conjunction of sex and violence with tasty rectitude. Dworkin's closing point is that it makes a difference whether audiences are being led to envision or to enjoy the worst they can imagine, and that so much terror sold to so little effect may be mirroring a perpetual destiny.

- Treats the atomic film as a genre whose consumers know what they are buying, advertising having called them carefully.
- Argues the more enjoyable the film, the less likely it is to disturb complacency.
- Reads the Daily Express setting of The Day the Earth Caught Fire as compromising its science with newspaper sensationalism.
- Finds Panic In Year Zero's family-survival scenario the more disturbing because it prescribes behaviour, including violence, as necessary.
- Ends by asking whether such films show more of our future than appears on the screen.

### Convergence of the West and the Soviet Union
*By Christopher Mayhew and others*

Mayhew's is the opening statement of the DISCUSSION symposium and the only affirmative one. He takes the principal differences between the systems to be the Soviet Union's lack of political, personal and cultural freedom, its lower living standards, greater social equality and higher level of planning, and examines each in turn. ### Convergence of the West and the Soviet Union
*By HENRY L. ROBERTS*

Roberts approaches the question historically and sceptically. He argues that pre-revolutionary contrasts between Russia and Western Europe have been drawn too sharply — the relationship is better described as gradations of a spectrum across the continent than as a polarity — while conceding that the subjective sense of antithesis mattered, since moderate differences of degree are easily taken for differences in kind. In the two decades before 1914 that picture was becoming irrelevant, especially culturally: Russia's Silver Age was allied to Western developments, but the similarity came from a common irruption into the disquieting landscape of the twentieth century, so that the convergence was 'not through resolution but through advance into the unknown'.

The war and revolution then restored the old antithesis in Communist and Imperialist dress, and Roberts identifies a specific impetus to divergence in the act of breaking away: like a child leaving its parents or a colony its mother country, the struggle for separate identity is an intimate internecine conflict in which features of the old order carry over and threaten 'backsliding' or Restoration. That fear dominated the first Soviet generation, but is losing force with the passage of time — with the paradoxical result that securely established distance may reduce divergence, as he felt in postwar Soviet scholars who were more remote yet less combative. Against this he sets the fact that the two societies are now constantly thrown together, producing a host of 'partial convergences', some inimical, some positive, which excite visiting scientists and artists. He doubts these can produce political convergence, and cites Khrushchev's rebuff to a French correspondent who suggested science might unify the world. Any general convergence, he speculates, would come as another common break-through into the uncharted, politics 'in a new key' with unsettling dissonances.

- Recasts the Russia-West antithesis as a spectrum, while insisting subjective perceptions of polarity had real effects.
- Reads the pre-1914 cultural convergence as a shared plunge into modernity rather than a meeting of two societies.
- Identifies disaffiliation — the struggle for separate identity — as an independent source of hostility, now fading with the generations.
- Distinguishes numerous partial convergences from political convergence and doubts the former produce the latter.
- Suggests too much human history has gone into the political divergences for explicit reconciliation.

### Convergence of the West and the Soviet Union
*By ZBIGNIEW BRZEZINSKI*

Brzezinski answers 'Not really.' He concedes universal traits inherent in the industrial order and in mass culture and leisure, so that in that sense convergence has happened, is happening and will happen, China included: every factory built in an isolated Chinese town reduces the cultural, economic and social distance between Chikurting and Chicago. ### Philosophy in Indian Universities
*By A. M. Ghose*

Ghose's letter responds to Punya Sloka Ray's article 'The Teaching of Philosophy in India' in Quest 34, which he calls admirably frank and perspicacious, and adds observations of his own. First, the traditions of higher learning are relatively new in India: by higher learning he does not mean the pandits and maulvis but a liberal outlook free of both subtilised mysticism and gross utilitarianism, caring more for intellectual adventure and unhurried research than scholastic hair-splitting — almost wholly a gift of European education, since for all the imposing speculative systems, knowledge here was rarely valued as power and was subservient to esoteric experience or immediate need. He then turns the criticism on the whole academic class. Academic philosophers are seldom interested in problems that agitate minds in other fields, but neither is anyone else: what colleagues in other disciplines are passionately interested in is 'career, recognition, committees and conferences'.
