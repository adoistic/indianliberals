# Freedom First

## Summary

Freedom First No. 565 (July 2014) is the monthly issue of the Bombay-based liberal magazine published in its 63rd year, appearing roughly six weeks after Narendra Modi's BJP won India's 2014 general election. The issue is dominated by a large "General Elections 2014 and After" section in which several contributors — Ashok Karnik, H. R. Bapu Satyanarayana, Firoze Hirjikaka, Nitin G. Raut, P. Koshy and M. D. Kini — assess why the Congress-led UPA collapsed, what Modi's cabinet formation and "Gujarat Model" portend for governance, and whether concentrating power in one leader is a cause for liberal concern even as the magazine's editorial formally welcomes his government while pledging to avoid personal attacks on the Prime Minister. A companion cover feature reprints a 1979 interview in which Minoo Masani recalls founding the Swatantra Party with C. Rajagopalachari and Professor Ranga and reflects, pessimistically, on whether such a party could be revived. Beyond the election, the issue carries pieces on economic risk (an El Niño-driven monsoon warning), foreign policy and security (a roadmap for Modi's foreign policy, an account of Taliban/ISI attacks on Indian assets in Afghanistan, and the Tibetan citizenship dilemma), social commentary (an essay on the causes of rape and a note on violence against women), a soldier's personal remembrance, a book review, and a substantial student-contributed "Educating Adults" feature marking the World War I centenary with pieces on its causes, weapons, trenches, women's wartime roles, and Japan's role in the war.

The volume's argumentative center is classical-liberal: minimum government, free enterprise, and scepticism of dynastic and statist politics, paired with real ambivalence about handing near-absolute power to any single leader, including one the magazine had itself supported. A "From Our Readers" letters section carrying strong pro- and anti-Modi reactions to the magazine's own prior coverage, and several short items (on bureaucratic "cooling-off" rules, parliamentary mourning-adjournments, and a failed astrological election prediction) round out the issue.

## Essays

### Can The Swatantra Party Be Resurrected? - Minoo Masani in Conversation with Frits Bolkestein
*By Minoo Masani in Conversation with Frits Bolkestein*

This is a reprint of a May 1979 interview of Minoo Masani by Frits Bolkestein (then the Netherlands' Minister for Foreign Trade and President of Liberal International), excerpted from a 1986 Festschrift. In the portion rendered, Masani recounts founding the Swatantra Party in 1959 with C. Rajagopalachari and Professor Ranga as a non-socialist Liberal-Conservative alternative, describes its rapid rise to become India's largest opposition party by the late 1960s through modern campaign techniques he introduced from British politics, and then narrates its collapse after the 1971 "Indira wave" (44 seats down to 8) and its eventual 1974 merger into the B.K.D. under Piloo Mody and Charan Singh, a decision Masani opposed.

Asked directly whether the Swatantra Party could be resurrected, Masani is doubtful, arguing that Indira Gandhi had "destroyed standards" of Indian political conduct and that a party of Swatantra's original moral calibre would now be much harder to build, though he maintains the party left a lasting mark by putting free enterprise "on the map of India."

- Swatantra Party founded 1959 by Rajaji, Masani and Professor Ranga as a Liberal-Conservative reaction to Congress/socialist dominance; Rajaji coined the name from 'Swa' (self) and 'tantra' (mechanism).
- The party combined three strands: Rajaji's conservative Hindu wing, Ranga's agrarian wing, and Masani's liberal bourgeoisie wing.
- Masani introduced British-style campaign methods (long-term constituency nursing, publicity, posters) that other Indian parties resisted; the party grew to 44 Lok Sabha seats by 1967, the largest opposition bloc.
- The party was devastated in the 1971 election (44 seats to 8) by the 'Indira wave' and was dissolved into the B.K.D. in 1974 under successors H. M. Patel and Piloo Mody, over Masani's objection.
- In 1979 Masani doubted the party could be revived, citing a general decline in Indian political standards since Indira Gandhi, though he credits Swatantra with permanently establishing free enterprise as a live idea in Indian politics.

### From Our Readers
The unsigned editorial ("The Importance of a Conservative-Liberal Alternative") explains why Freedom First supported Narendra Modi and the BJP during the 2014 campaign, arguing Modi spoke "the Liberal's language" — market economy, jobs over doles, and "minimum government, maximum governance." It reviews the Congress-I's four-decade record of never winning a majority of votes cast (peaking at 49.1% after Indira Gandhi's assassination, versus 19.3% and 44 seats in 2014) and argues India now needs a middle-of-the-road party to eventually replace it, recalling Freedom First's own hope for a revived Swatantra Party.

The editorial sets ground rules for the magazine's future coverage: it will not publish personal attacks on Modi or the BJP but will offer concrete policy critique (citing the Union Budget as an example), noting the magazine also criticized Modi after the 2002 Gujarat riots to rebut any charge of uncritical cheerleading.

- Freedom First backed Modi/BJP in the 2014 campaign because Modi's platform (market economy, jobs, women's security, 'Minimum Government, Maximum Governance') echoed the magazine's own liberal positions.
- Congress-I never won a majority of votes cast despite ruling most of the post-independence period; its 2014 result was 44 seats on 19.3% of the vote, versus a historic high of 49.1% after Indira Gandhi's assassination.
- The editorial hopes Modi's victory could be a 'tipping point' for a revived Liberal-Conservative Swatantra-style party to replace a declining Congress-I.
- States the magazine will avoid personal attacks or motive-attribution against Modi/BJP but will publish specific policy critique (e.g. on the Union Budget, law and order).
- Cites Freedom First's own critical 2002 coverage of Modi after the Gujarat riots (FF453, April 2002) as evidence its 2014 support was not uncritical.

### Editorial
V. Krishna Moorthy surveys the 2014 El Niño risk to India's southwest monsoon, explaining the Pacific ocean-atmosphere mechanism behind El Niño and citing past drought years (2002, 2004, 2009) it triggered. He reviews IMD and South Asian Climate Outlook Forum forecasts showing a 33% chance of deficient rainfall, and traces the likely knock-on effects on kharif crop output, food and non-food inflation (CPI already near 8-10%), GDP growth (risking a third straight sub-5% year), banking-sector NPAs, and India's rice-export competitiveness against Thailand.

He concludes that while poor monsoons have historically driven inflation spikes (food inflation hit 21% after the 2009 El Niño), El Niño is a necessary but not sufficient condition for drought, and calls for expanding north India's irrigation model nationally, along with cold storage and food-processing investment, reviving Dr. K. L. Rao's shelved river-linking proposal.

- El Niño (Pacific sea-surface warming) is linked to below-normal Indian monsoons and caused droughts in 2002, 2004 and 2009.
- IMD's June 2014 forecast put a 33% chance of deficient rainfall, with analysts fearing agricultural GDP growth could slow to 0.8% in FY15 from 4.7% in FY14.
- Only 45% of India's sown land is irrigated and the monsoon supplies over 70% of annual rainfall for 235 million farmers, so a weak monsoon threatens both inflation and overall GDP growth (already near 4.9-5.6%).
- A weak monsoon could also hit India's rice-export dominance (a record 10.5 million tonnes in FY14) versus resurgent competition from Thailand.
- Author recommends replicating north India's irrigation network nationally, building cold-storage/food-processing capacity, and reviving Dr. K. L. Rao's perennial-river-linking proposal, dropped for lack of funding consensus.

### EL NINO and India's Monsoon Concerns
*By V. Krishna Moorthy*

Ashok Karnik's recurring "Point Counter Point" column presents paired, opposing short takes on three post-election topics: Modi's burden of unprecedented expectations (weighing his SAARC-leader outreach and anti-corruption SIT against fears of an emboldened Hindutva 'mad fringe'); the Congress's failure to introspect on its scam record (CWG, 2G, Coalgate, Adarsh, the Robert Vadra land deals) versus its lawyer-driven, legalistic defensiveness; and the appointment of Ajit Doval as National Security Advisor, praised from both sides as likely to galvanize India's intelligence agencies into a more assertive posture.

A boxed sidebar, "Whither Astrology?", mocks a Pune astrologer whose detailed pre-poll predictions for the 2014 election were all proven wrong.

- Format deliberately pairs a critical and a sympathetic view on each of three topics to model even-handed analysis.
- On Modi: risk of an over-empowered 'mad fringe' committing Hindutva-linked violence, weighed against his surprise SAARC-leader invitation and a new anti-black-money SIT.
- On Congress: criticized for refusing to investigate its own scam allegations and instead relying on 'clever lawyers' as spokespeople, which voters saw as evasive.
- On the new NSA: both takes welcome Ajit Doval's operational record (the only civilian Kirti Chakra awardee) and expect him to push IB/R&AW into a more 'action oriented' mode.
- A sidebar highlights a Pune astrologer's completely failed 2014 election predictions as a jab at astrology's claims to scientific validity.

### Point Counter Point
*By Ashok Karnik*

H. R. Bapu Satyanarayana chronicles Narendra Modi's 26 May 2014 swearing-in as India's 15th Prime Minister before a record 4,000-strong gathering that included, for the first time, the heads of all SAARC nations (including Pakistan's Nawaz Sharif). He details Modi's compact 45-minister cabinet (25% women), the scrapping of UPA-era Groups of Ministers, a 10-point rule-cutting mandate with 100-day departmental timetables, and outreach to top bureaucrats to speed decision-making.

The piece also covers early symbolic and diplomatic moves — the Ganga cleanup entrusted to Uma Bharati, scientist-led delegations abroad, and cautiously optimistic notes on Modi's engagement with Nawaz Sharif and Jayalalithaa — closing with the President's 9 June address to Parliament and praise (even from critic Shashi Tharoor) for Modi's early conduct in office.

- Modi's swearing-in on 26 May 2014 was attended by SAARC heads of state including Pakistan PM Nawaz Sharif for the first time in such a ceremony.
- His 45-member cabinet, with 7 women ministers (25%), combined related portfolios to speed inter-ministerial decision-making and was described as the most compact to date.
- Modi scrapped the UPA's Groups/Empowered Groups of Ministers system, instructed departments to identify 10 obsolete rules each for removal, and set 100-day timetables.
- He engaged directly with 77 top bureaucrats to build a 'symbiotic relationship' between ministers and civil servants for faster implementation.
- Early diplomatic gestures — inviting SAARC leaders, courting Jayalalithaa, and entrusting the Ganga cleanup to Uma Bharati — are read as signs of both political skill and a cautiously constructive federal and foreign-policy approach.

### A Tectonic Shift in Political and Administrative Culture
*By H. R. Bapu Satyanaryana*

Firoze Hirjikaka reflects on his own mostly-wrong pre-election predictions to analyze why Modi's campaign of personal, near-plebiscitary appeal succeeded, tracing how both Rahul Gandhi and Arvind Kejriwal 'pressed the self-destruct button' — Rahul through poverty-glorifying rhetoric and abandoning reasoned discourse, Kejriwal through an all-consuming anti-Modi obsession that split the opposition vote and disillusioned his own base. He notes the stark disproportion between the BJP's 31% vote share and its resulting near-60% parliamentary majority under first-past-the-post.

The essay closes on an ambivalent note: admiration for Modi's oratorical and organizational skill and relief that hardliner Subramanian Swamy was excluded from the cabinet, mixed with unease at power concentrated in 'a single individual' who has 'brooked no opposition' in Gujarat, comparing him to Louis XIV's 'L'etat, c'est moi.'

- Author admits his April 2014 Freedom First predictions (decline of Rahul Gandhi's threat, Shiv Sena's decline) were largely wrong, attributing this to underestimating the scale of the 'Modi wave.'
- Argues the BJP's 31% vote share (versus Congress's 19%) translated into roughly seven times the seats and a near-60% parliamentary majority, questioning the fairness of first-past-the-post representation.
- Both Rahul Gandhi and Arvind Kejriwal are blamed for self-destructive campaigns — Rahul for alienating the poor by dwelling on handouts, Kejriwal for an anti-Modi obsession that split anti-BJP votes.
- Notes UPA-2 had belatedly cleared 152 stalled infrastructure projects worth Rs. 5.30 lakh crore just before leaving office, credit for which Modi will likely claim.
- Expresses apprehension about concentrating 'near absolute power' in Modi, citing his sidelining of senior BJP leaders Advani and Joshi, while noting relief that Subramanian Swamy was kept out of the cabinet.

### All Hail The King!
*By Firoze Hirjikaka*

Nitin G. Raut argues that Modi's personal leadership, branded around 'Vikas' (development) and slogans like 'Sab ka saath, sab ka vikas,' single-handedly overcame the entrenched Nehru-Gandhi dynasty, likening it to Obama's 2008 'Yes We Can' campaign. He contends Congress-aligned 'secularists' discredited the term itself through appeasement politics (citing a UP minister's remark linking soldiers' religion to the Kargil War) and that this, combined with youth demand for jobs over doles, produced Modi's decisive win.

The essay contrasts Modi's self-made, oratorically confident leadership style with Rahul Gandhi's scripted, dynasty-shielded persona, and frames the BJP's outright majority — the first for a non-Congress party — as demolishing the post-1987 assumption that coalition government was India's permanent future, while criticizing Congress's post-defeat elevation of Mallikarjun Kharge as proof the party still prizes dynasty over merit.

- Frames 2014 as Modi single-handedly challenging the Nehru-Gandhi dynasty's grip on Indian politics, comparing his campaign to Obama's 2008 'Yes We Can.'
- Argues 'secularist' rhetoric was discredited by selective, vote-bank-driven appeasement politics, citing a UP minister's remark on soldiers' religion during the Kargil War debate.
- Contrasts Modi's humble-origin, oratorically confident leadership with Rahul Gandhi's dynastic, ghost-written persona.
- BJP's outright majority (crossing 272 seats alone) is described as the first non-Congress party majority government and a repudiation of the post-1987 'permanent coalition era' thesis.
- Criticizes Congress's appointment of Mallikarjun Kharge as Lok Sabha leader as evidence the party still prioritizes the Gandhi family's wishes over merit.

### The Importance of Leadership
*By Nitin G. Raut*

P. Koshy diagnoses Congress's 2014 defeat through ground-level anecdotes — voters frustrated by Aadhaar bureaucracy, MGNREGA's labour-market distortions, the controversial Gadgil Committee (Western Ghats) report, and the divisive bifurcation of Andhra Pradesh — arguing UPA policy was enacted 'without proper study, consultation and consensus.' He contends the belated Food Security offer, on top of already-poor PDS grain quality, only deepened public disillusionment.

His central claim is that Congress fundamentally misread its own target voters: India's poor prefer dignified, independent livelihoods (symbolized by the tea-vendor entrepreneur Modi presented himself as) over being cast as recipients of welfare schemes named after the Nehru-Gandhi family, and Congress's continuing reliance on dynasty (illustrated by Rahul Gandhi's refusal to lead the parliamentary party) shows it has not learned the lesson.

- Congress campaign managers, drawn from elite institutes (IIT/IIM), remained overconfident despite mounting anti-incumbency signs, including defections among usually loyal Congress voters in Kerala.
- Cites voter anecdotes on Aadhaar-linkage hassles, the disruptive labour-market effects of MGNREGA, and controversy over the Gadgil Committee's Western Ghats ecology report.
- The pre-election Food Security scheme is seen as backfiring given already poor-quality subsidized PDS grain.
- Congress's Christian-dress-code and reservation-in-private-sector remarks are cited as examples of the party's own 'venomous communalism' even as it accused the BJP of communalism.
- Central argument: India's poor prefer dignified independence over benefits under schemes named after the Nehru-Gandhi family, a psychological shift Congress failed to grasp.

### What Happened to the Congress Party in 2014
*By P. Koshy*

M. D. Kini defends the 'Gujarat Model of Development' against critics who cite its average human-development-index ranking, cataloguing concrete administrative and infrastructure achievements under Modi: digitized e-governance and e-panchayats that curbed corruption, stable bureaucratic tenures via 'Chintan Shivir' brainstorming sessions, and reform of the Gujarat State Electricity Board (GSEB) under Manjula Subramaniam into a profit-making entity with 24-hour domestic power.

He details Gujarat's agricultural transformation (100,000+ check dams, micro-irrigation area up fiftyfold, Bt cotton success, soil-health cards), infrastructure gains (port capacity more than doubled 2001-11, 74,000 km of roads), and cites migrant-worker testimonials from UP and Bihar as informal validation, concluding the 2014 election verdict is a mandate to replicate the model across all Indian states.

- E-governance (digitized procurement, village internet connections, e-panchayats) reduced corruption and 'speed-money' in government transactions.
- GSEB was transformed from a loss-making PSU into a profit-making entity by separating agricultural, industrial and domestic power feeders under Chairperson Manjula Subramaniam.
- Agricultural growth relied on massive check-dam and micro-irrigation expansion (from 10,000 to 500,000 hectares in ten years) with subsidized farmer incentives, plus Bt cotton and soil-health cards.
- Port capacity more than doubled (135 to 284 million tonnes, 2001-11) via public-private partnerships; road network reached 74,000 km connecting nearly all villages.
- Migrant-worker testimonials from UP and Bihar are cited as grassroots validation of higher wages, reliable power, and better roads in Gujarat versus their home states.

### What is 'The Gujarat Model' of Development
*By M. D. Kini*

B. Ramesh Babu lays out a foreign-policy roadmap for the new Modi government, arguing that restoring domestic economic growth is foreign policy's 'most fundamental, enduring, and overarching' purpose, while warning against the government becoming captured by 'corporate capitalism' favoring the Adanis and Ambanis. He calls for a stronger US-India-Japan strategic partnership to counter an 'aggressively expansionist' China, arguing India should build a broader Asia-Pacific coalition and treat the Modi visa-denial and Devyani episodes as manageable irritants rather than derailing the India-US relationship.

On Pakistan, he urges careful re-examination of India's failed counter-terrorism approach, including cautious consideration of a 'hot pursuit' doctrine as a weapon of last resort, alongside continued expansion of trade and people-to-people ties. He also highlights India's underappreciated role as a foreign-aid donor (~$2.5 billion over three decades to ~125 countries) and recommends a more coherent, MEA-led aid doctrine, plus expanded ties with Latin America.

- Argues restoring high, sustained economic growth is foreign policy's central purpose, while cautioning against the government being captured by 'corporate capitalism' benefiting the Adani and Ambani groups.
- Recommends a stronger US-India-Japan strategic triangle to deter Chinese expansionism, since 'the only thing China fears is our proximity to the U.S.'
- On Pakistan, urges re-examining a possible 'hot pursuit' doctrine against cross-border terrorists as a weapon of last resort, while cautioning this must not escalate into wider conflict given both countries' nuclear arsenals.
- Highlights India as a significant, underpublicized foreign-aid donor (~$2.5 billion over three decades to ~125 countries) hampered by lack of MEA coordination across ministries.
- Recommends greater economic and political engagement with Latin America (Cuba, Brazil, Mexico, Venezuela, Argentina) as an underexploited opportunity.

### Foreign Policy Agenda for the Modi Government
*By B. Ramesh Babu*

Tibetan writer and activist Tenzing Tsundue writes a first-person account of the February 2014 Election Commission ruling allowing Tibetans born in India between 1950 and 1987 to claim Indian citizenship and vote, describing the anguished community debate this triggered between pragmatists and identity-preserving idealists like himself. He recounts his own history of detention and prosecution as a 'foreigner' under the Foreigners Registration Act despite being born in India, and details how a landmark 2010 court case (Namgyal Dolkar's passport suit) established that such Tibetans are legally natural-born Indian citizens.

He describes the low turnout (217 of 14,000 Dharamshala Tibetans voted on 7 May 2014) driven by fear of losing Registration Cards, and closes by arguing that Tibetan refugees — including 7,000 soldiers in India's Special Frontier Force guarding the Himalayan border — are a strategic asset for India's China policy that most Indians, and Indian textbooks, do not recognize.

- The Election Commission's 7 February 2014 announcement allowed Tibetans in India, under Citizenship Act 1955 Section 3(1)(a), to register as citizens and vote for the first time.
- Namgyal Dolkar's five-year legal battle (won 22 December 2010) established the precedent that Tibetans born in India between 26 January 1950 and 1 July 1987 are natural-born Indian citizens.
- Only 217 of 14,000 Dharamshala Tibetans voted on 7 May 2014, most fearing loss of their Registration Cards (RCs), which establish Tibetan refugee identity.
- Author was himself jailed 11 days and prosecuted under the Foreigners Registration Act 1946 despite being born in India, illustrating the community's precarious legal status.
- Argues Tibetan refugees, the Dalai Lama, the Karmapa, the government-in-exile, and 7,000 Special Frontier Force soldiers are an unrecognized strategic asset for India's border security and China policy.

### To Be or Not To Be a Citizen: The Tibetan Dilemma
*By Tenzing Tsundue*

Brig. Suresh C. Sharma (retd.) surveys Taliban and ISI-linked attacks on Indian diplomatic and civilian assets in Afghanistan since 2005, arguing Pakistan views close Indo-Afghan relations as a strategic threat and has used the Taliban to damage them. He details a string of attacks — a 2007 grenade attack on the Jalalabad consulate, a 2008 ITBP soldier killing, and the deadliest attack, the 7 July 2008 suicide bombing at the Kabul embassy that killed 58 people including diplomat V. Venkateswara Rao and Defence Attaché Brig. Mehta, attributed by Afghan intelligence and RAW to the Haqqani Group with ISI backing — through the most recent 23 May 2014 assault on the Herat consulate.

He outlines India's roughly $2 billion in Afghan infrastructure and humanitarian commitments (the Pul-i-Khumri power house, parliament building, Delaram-Zaranj road, Hajigak iron-ore mining) and argues that with US troop drawdown to about 10,000 by 2014, India must expand military/economic aid and soft-power support despite lacking the land access for direct military options.

- Pakistan sees close India-Afghanistan ties as a strategic disadvantage and has used its influence over the Taliban to disrupt Kabul-Delhi relations.
- The deadliest attack was the 7 July 2008 Kabul embassy suicide bombing, killing 58 including Indian diplomat V. Venkateswara Rao and Defence Attaché Brig. Mehta; the Afghanistan Intelligence Agency and RAW attributed it to the Haqqani Group with Pakistani ISI support.
- India has committed roughly $2 billion to Afghan infrastructure and humanitarian projects, including the Pul-i-Khumri power house, parliament building, Delaram-Zaranj road, and Hajigak iron-ore mining.
- A 23 May 2014 attack on the Herat consulate was repelled by ITBP and Afghan National Army forces with what the author calls 'excellent coordination.'
- With US forces drawing down to about 10,000 troops, India faces a real security threat from Taliban spillover but has limited direct military options due to lack of land access, so must rely on aid, training, and soft power.

### Attack on Indian Assets in Afghanistan
*By Suresh C. Sharma*

Sharu S. Rangnekar surveys the causes and history of rape — distinguishing under-recognized 'war rapes' (Nanking, WWII 'comfort women,' the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War, the Bosnian War), commercial rape linked to prostitution, marital rape, and largely unreported incest and 'date rape' — and argues public reaction to the Delhi and Mumbai cases produced more stringent laws driven by 'emotional outbursts rather than logical analysis.'

He contends the short-term remedy is sex education (credited with a 60% US reduction in rapes) and self-defence training, while the long-term structural fix is a partial return to matrilineal social systems (citing Kerala's Nairs and Meghalaya's Khasis), which he argues correlate with lower rape incidence than the male-dominated patrilineal systems that emerged as human society organized into families and tribes.

- Distinguishes categories often excluded from rape statistics: war rape (Nanking Massacre ~80,000 victims, WWII 'comfort women,' ~200,000 women raped during the 1971 Bangladesh Liberation War, ~20,000 Bosnian Muslim women during the Bosnian War, recognized as a crime against humanity only from 1998).
- Cites roughly 60% of rapes worldwide as unreported, and up to 95% of US campus rapes as unreported, with incest historically hidden within extended families.
- Argues marital rape, once widely condoned, is now criminalized by international convention though still tolerated in practice in many countries.
- Short-term recommended fixes are sex education (credited with a 60% US rape reduction) and self-defence training for women.
- Long-term structural argument: societies with matrilineal family systems (Kerala's Nairs, Meghalaya's Khasis) show lower rape incidence than patrilineal, male-dominated cultures, so partial restoration of matrilineal structures is proposed as the durable remedy.

### Rape: Yesterday, Today and Tomorrow
*By Sharu S. Rangnekar*

Air Vice Marshal (Retd.) Cecil Parker offers a light, affectionate personal memoir of his 1951-cadet-course mate 'Sugu,' written for the 61st reunion anniversary of their Pilots Course (15 of the original 30 members surviving).
