# QUEST

## Summary

This issue of Quest (Vol. II No. 2, October-November 1956), edited by Nissim Ezekiel for the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, opens with an unsigned editorial arguing that a liberal democracy is the only political order that can support the arts without dictating to them, explicitly contrasting this with a Soviet-style Ministry of Culture. The seven articles that follow range widely: A. B. Shah examines the dangers and limits of Gandhian satyagraha as a tool for a self-righteous majority or a fractious minority in independent India (using the Bombay-Maharashtra linguistic dispute as his test case); Sunil Mukherji and Walter Z. Laqueur (the latter reprinted from The New Leader as a 'prophetic' pre-nationalisation piece) analyse the Suez Canal crisis and the character of Nasserism; Satindra Singh surveys the politicised historiography of post-Independence India; Philip Spratt offers a Jungian/mythological reading of the Subhas Chandra Bose 'return of the hero' legend; A. Ranganathan traces Thoreau's influence on Gandhi; and Natwar Singh contributes an admiring literary tribute to R. K. Narayan. A short story by Gangadhar Gadgil (translated from the Marathi by Padma S. Rao), three poems, eight book reviews and two correspondence letters (on the state of academic philosophy and on anti-Americanism) round out the issue, along with a 'Contributors to this issue' page and a Congress for Cultural Freedom short-story-competition announcement.

Beyond the printed table of contents, the issue also carries four unnumbered art/photography plates tipped in mid-issue (between printed pages 22 and 23): a Raza painting, a photograph by Dr. S. B. Cooper, and two pages of street photographs by Jean Herman -- none of which is listed in the contents page. Read across the whole issue, a recurrent argumentative thread is a liberal, anti-totalitarian scepticism of both Communist and majoritarian-nationalist solutions -- whether in Indian domestic politics (satyagraha, historiography, linguistic-state reorganisation), in the Suez crisis, or in Cold War alignment -- paired with a strong interest in literary and artistic culture for its own sake.

## Essays

### Satyagraha and Democracy
*By A. B. Shah*

A. B. Shah examines satyagraha as an instrument of political change in independent India, arguing that its usual justification -- that it awakens the moral conscience of an opponent -- assumes an idealised Gandhian actor rarely found in practice. He traces the theory of satyagraha (its etymology, its reliance on 'soulforce' and quality over quantity of participants, Gandhiji's willingness to work with 'ordinary mortals') before turning to its dangers: a mass civil-disobedience movement tends to slip into violence (citing the 1955-56 Goa demonstrations and the reaction to the States Reorganisation Commission report in Bombay), and it relies on emotion rather than reasoned agreement, which is corrosive of parliamentary norms. Using the Congress leadership's decision to make Bombay a centrally administered area against the wishes of Maharashtra as his central illustration, he argues satyagraha is justified only as a last resort, when parliamentary methods are genuinely closed, and that even Gandhiji's own movement did no more than 'rouse and weld together' Indians against a common foreign enemy -- a very different, and much easier, task than a minority group or regional unit would face today.

- Democracy relies on persuasion by rational argument, but a determined majority can still curtail minority rights while formally observing the Constitution (the Madras caste-exclusion example, the Jeep-scandal exchange).
- The Bombay-Maharashtra dispute is used as the central case: linguistic and cultural claims were conceded 'in theory but denied in practice' when Bombay was made a centrally administered area.
- Satyagraha is defined via its Gandhian theory: insistence on truth, willing suffering, 'soulforce' over numbers, non-violence in word, action and (ideally) thought.
- In practice, mass satyagraha risks violence by irresponsible elements even under careful leadership, as recent Bombay experience showed.
- Satyagraha appeals to emotion rather than reason and is only legitimate as a last resort after parliamentary and rational avenues are exhausted.
- Even Gandhiji's satyagraha achieved only a 'popular awakening,' not independence itself, which came from other historical forces; a minority group today has far less scope for a comparably unifying appeal.
- Conclusion: satyagraha, like virtue, must be treated as its own reward rather than a reliable political weapon.

### Areas of Conscience
*By Harry Roskolenko*

A short lyric on guilt, cruelty to a drowned cat, and the equalising, self-transforming passage of time and pain from childhood to adulthood.

- Uses the memory of drowning a cat as a recurring image of youthful sin and later remorse.
- Builds a refrain structure ('and so...', 'in all my ways') across three stanzas moving from guilt to fear to self-knowledge.

### Portrait in Purple
*By Harry Roskolenko*

A satirical character sketch in verse of careerist, morally compromised men -- their 'amoral kingdoms,' hidden treasons and calculated ambitions -- rendered in a mock-mathematical vocabulary of 'arithmetic,' 'measure' and 'exactness.'

- Applies a conceit of arithmetic/mathematics to satirise the calculated, transactional morality of ambitious men.
- Each stanza intensifies the indictment, moving from social observation to explicit moral suicide and lechery.

### The Suez Canal Crisis
*By Sunil Mukherji*

Sunil Mukherji reads the Suez Canal nationalisation as one symptom of a broader, difficult re-adjustment between newly sovereign Asian and African states and their former colonial rulers, complicated at every turn by the Cold War contest between the Soviet and Anglo-American blocs. He argues India's own experience -- friendly relations with the West curdling somewhat as the USSR courted New Delhi and the US, in response, armed Pakistan -- typifies the dynamic. His central prescription is that national sovereignty itself, both Western and 'newly independent,' is an 'anachronism' in a shrunken, technologically interdependent world, and that the Suez Canal's internationalisation could be the opening move toward abrogating sovereignty altogether. He closes by criticising Indian statesmen for indulging Soviet 'crocodile tears' about colonial victims while ignoring how the Cold War itself, not Western malice alone, blocks aid and development -- pointedly asking whether India might instead 'blaze a new trail' by championing cooperative internationalism over national glory.

- Frames Suez, the Algerian war and Goa as three symptoms of the same post-colonial 'birth pangs.'
- Argues the Cold War, not colonial legacy alone, is what makes decolonisation-era readjustment so combustible.
- India's drift toward the Soviet bloc pushed the West toward Pakistan, souring Indo-Western relations.
- Calls national sovereignty (Western and Asian/African alike) an anachronism the Suez crisis could help dismantle via internationalisation of the Canal.
- Criticises Indian leaders for uncritically accepting Soviet professions of anti-colonial solidarity while ignoring the World Bank/Aswan Dam funding dispute that actually triggered Nasser's move.
- Ends with a rhetorical challenge: can India ground its foreign policy in cooperative human welfare rather than national power politics?

### Dilemma of the West in Egypt
*By Walter Z. Laqueur*

Reprinted from The New Leader (23 July 1956) with an editor's note flagging it as remarkably prophetic (it predates the Canal's nationalisation), Laqueur's essay dissects Western 'conservative' and 'liberal' schools of thought on how to handle Nasser. He rejects the conservative call to prop up Iran, Turkey and Iraq against 'Nasserism' as resting on unstable partners, and is harsher still on the liberal position, which he accuses of refusing to distinguish constructive nationalisms (Burma, Ceylon) from an expansionist, Napoleon III/Mussolini-style Nasserism bent on regional 'leadership' rather than domestic development. He insists Western observers must take Egyptian and other Arab press and radio at face value rather than dismissing their anti-Western rhetoric as posturing, warning that a handful of hidden 'wire-pullers' could turn Nasser's 'left-wing fascist' regime into a Communist one with little effort, and that only a realistic reading of what is actually being said in Cairo can produce a workable Western Middle East policy.

- Surveys the immediate regional backdrop: British withdrawal from Suez, Nasser's 99.9% election, Sharett's resignation, Soviet diplomatic overtures.
- Rejects the 'conservative' Western strategy of backing Iran/Turkey/Iraq as resting on weak, unstable regimes.
- Rejects the 'liberal' Western strategy of accommodating Nasserism, arguing it wrongly equates it with constructive nationalisms elsewhere in Asia.
- Warns that Egyptian state media should be read literally, not dismissed as bluster, citing an editorial boasting 'every Egyptian is proud to be a Goebbels.'
- Argues the current Cairo regime is already functionally the 'first stage' of a people's democracy, needing only a change of 10-20 personnel to become fully Communist.
- Calls for Western policy grounded in what is actually printed/broadcast in Egypt rather than filtered official translations.

### Rewriting of Indian History
*By Satindra Singh*

Satindra Singh surveys the post-Independence enthusiasm for rewriting Indian history and finds it wanting on both sides of the old divide: foreign historians vilified India to justify colonial rule, while Indian historians (nationalist and Marxist alike, from Nehru's Discovery of India to Dange's and Namboodripad's materialist histories) over-glorified or force-fitted the past to serve present political ends. He lists a set of unanswered puzzles -- why India's civilisation persisted where Egypt's, Syria's or Babylon's did not, why it never industrialised despite an advanced commercial-urban civilisation, why conquerors other than the British were absorbed -- and argues these can only be answered through patient, non-ideological empirical research (agrarian economy, land revenue records, trade and archaeology) rather than further politically motivated synthesis.

- New official and independent bodies (a government committee, the Bharatiya Itihasa Samiti, Communist pamphleteers) are all producing new 'true' histories of India, welcome in principle but suspect in execution.
- Foreign historians used historiography to justify colonial rule; Indian historians overcorrected into self-glorification.
- Politician-historians (Nehru's Discovery of India, Dange's and Namboodripad's Marxist histories) distort the past to fit present political programmes.
- Lists open, unanswered historical puzzles about the persistence, technological stagnation and repeated conquest of Indian civilisation.
- Proposes empirical remedies: agrarian-economy and land-revenue research, trade and archaeological data, comparative work with Western scholarship on other civilisations.

### The Lonely Hour
*By Ranjani Rao*

A brief lyric on grief, twilight and unending loneliness, tomorrow perpetually deferred.

- Uses storm and twilight imagery ('dark hooves of bodiless air', 'razor-sleek sparrow-way') to figure grief as an elemental, ongoing event.
- Closes on an image of loneliness that 'may never end' with tomorrow 'always far away.'

### The Rebel
*By Philip Spratt*

Philip Spratt offers a mythological-psychological reading of the popular refusal to accept Subhas Chandra Bose's 1945 death, arguing that the coloured prints of a youthful, defiant 'Netaji' returning against a rising sun re-enact an ancient Indian myth-pattern of the rebellious slain-and-resurrected god (Indra/Vritra, the Oedipal Prajapati story in the Brahmanas, Krishna, Rama), a pattern Spratt (drawing on A. M. Hocart and Ananda Coomaraswamy) argues had been largely suppressed or moralised out of later Hindu mythology in favour of images of filial, ascetic piety. He reviews Bose's biography -- his youth relative to Gandhi and Nehru, his austere and 'brahmachari' reputation, his repeated imprisonments and hunger strikes, his rebellion against 'the Father of the Nation,' his flight underground to Germany and Japan -- as fitting this template unusually well, and argues Bose's actual political and intellectual contribution was slight (independence was already implicit in the 1935 constitution and the Cripps offer) even as the emotional force of the myth was real. He closes with the generalisation that 'the mere rebel is only too likely to turn authoritarian,' finding Bose politically sterile but psychologically at the centre of 'much of the world's romance.'

- Popular belief that Netaji survived and would return functions as a modern instance of the ancient rebellious-slain-god myth pattern.
- Traces the myth through the Rigveda (Indra/Vritra), the Brahmanas' Oedipal Prajapati story (via Coomaraswamy), and later, more moralised puranic and epic versions (Krishna, Rama, the Vali-Sugriva episode).
- Argues later Hindu tradition largely purged the image of the rebel, replacing it with elderly, ascetic national heroes -- Bose is presented as the exception.
- Reviews Bose's biography (youth, austerity, repeated jailing and hunger strikes, revolt against Gandhi, flight to Germany and Japan, the I.N.A.) as fitting the mythic template closely.
- Argues Bose's substantive political contribution was minimal since independence was already implicit in the 1935 Act and the 1942 Cripps offer.
- Concludes that 'the mere rebel' without a constructive programme tends toward authoritarianism, and that the Bose legend has since faded into 'the national unconscious.'

### Thoreau's Impact on Gandhi
*By A Ranganathan*

A. Ranganathan traces the intellectual debt Gandhi owed to Thoreau, quoting President Sukarno's claim that this 'remote and lofty mind' of nineteenth-century Concord helped shape the twentieth-century Afro-Asian awakening. He follows Thoreau's own debt to Indian classics (the Gita, the image of Walden mingling with 'the sacred water of the Ganges'), Gandhi's 1907 encounter with Thoreau's Civil Disobedience in South Africa, and the parallel between Thoreau's tax resistance against slavery and Gandhi's campaigns against 'apartheid' and later against British rule (Champaran, then national satyagraha). Ranganathan also places Tolstoy and Ruskin in this genealogy of influence, and traces a further diffusion outward from Gandhi to Nkrumah-era Gold Coast and Nigeria, Bourguiba's Tunisia, and the Montgomery bus boycott, framing the whole as a 'cross-fertilization of ideas' underwriting a new East-West synthesis.

- Opens with Sukarno's claim that Thoreau and Gandhi jointly helped form the contemporary Afro-Asian political awakening.
- Traces Thoreau's own debt to Indian philosophy and Gandhi's 1907 encounter with Civil Disobedience in South Africa.
- Draws a direct parallel between Thoreau's resistance to the poll tax (over slavery) and Gandhi's campaigns in South Africa and India.
- Situates Tolstoy and Ruskin alongside Thoreau as formative influences feeding into Gandhi's synthesis.
- Traces the onward diffusion of Gandhian method to Nkrumah's Gold Coast, Nigeria, Bourguiba's Tunisia, and the Montgomery bus boycott.
- Frames the whole chain (Locke to the Glorious Revolution, Voltaire/Rousseau to the French Revolution, Marx/Lenin to the Russian Revolution, Thoreau/Gandhi to the Indian Revolution) as a general thesis that 'revolutions are made in the minds of men.'

### Tribute to R. K. Narayan
*By Natwar Singh*

Natwar Singh's tribute praises R. K. Narayan as unique among major Indian English writers for never having left India, crediting this for the 'chastity and purity' of his fiction. He surveys Narayan's Malgudi novels from Swami and Friends (1935, praised in Graham Greene's introduction to The Bachelor of Arts) through The English Teacher, The Dark Room, Mr. Sampath, The Financial Expert and the then-recent Waiting for the Mahatma, describing Narayan's characters as gently comic, free of violence or moralising 'isms,' and his prose as simple, sincere and quietly sad. He singles out Bharati in Waiting for the Mahatma as Narayan's finest female character to date, and closes by wondering whether Narayan, having apparently exhausted Malgudi's material, will now venture into fiction with a wider emotional and geographic range.

- Frames Narayan as unique among Indian English writers for never leaving India, crediting this for his fiction's authenticity.
- Quotes Graham Greene's introduction to The Bachelor of Arts praising Narayan for first bringing Indian life alive to English readers.
- Surveys Narayan's novels chronologically from Swami and Friends (1935) to Waiting for the Mahatma (the previous year), with a mixed verdict (praising The Bachelor of Arts and The English Teacher, more critical of The Financial Expert).
- Highlights Narayan's freedom from 'isms', violence and moralising, and his consistently comic-but-melancholy tone.
- Praises Bharati (Waiting for the Mahatma) as Narayan's best-drawn female character, contrasting her with his earlier 'flat and passive' women.
- Closes on whether Narayan's apparent move beyond Malgudi in his latest novel signals a lasting change in range.

### The Cage
*By Gangadhar Gadgil (translated from the Marathi by Padma S. Rao)*

A Marathi short story (translated by Padma S. Rao) about Ravindra Phatak, an impoverished, hot-tempered college lecturer crushed by his family's needs -- an ailing, asthmatic mother, a wife, two young daughters, a sister still at school -- in a cramped two-room Bombay home. When a wealthy student's father tries to buy a passing grade for his son, first via an ingratiating car ride and a planted cigarette case, and later via the Principal's thinly veiled hint that a promotion depends on the boy's marks, Phatak reacts with a proud, wounded refusal each time (hurling the cigarette case back, later throwing a bribery parcel at the same student), even though he immediately regrets the lost opportunity and cannot bring himself to voice his contempt for the Principal aloud. He remains a 'sheep belonging to the middle class,' powerless to escape his cage of poverty and obligation even as he preserves, in isolated bursts, his integrity.

- Establishes Phatak's poverty and domestic burdens (asthmatic mother, wife, children, a school-going sister) in a cramped two-room flat.
- A wealthy student's father attempts to bribe Phatak with a car ride, flattery and a planted cigarette case; Phatak angrily throws the case back and gets out of the car.
- The College Principal separately hints that a promotion depends on passing the same rich man's son; Phatak, ashamed, capitulates verbally but cannot bring himself to act corruptly or to protest.
- The story ends with the student, having 'bluffed' his way through the exam anyway, delivering a gift parcel that Phatak again refuses, throwing it back at him.
- Phatak's recurring self-description as 'a slave to my job, a sheep belonging to the middle class' frames the story's title image of a man caged by economic necessity.

### The Colour Curtain by Richard Wright
*By Prabhakar Padhye*

Prabhakar Padhye reviews Richard Wright's The Colour Curtain, an account of the 1955 Bandung Conference, arguing that despite Wright's reportorial gifts the book is disfigured by an 'obsession' with race that leads him (echoing a charge made against Wright by the Indonesian writer Mochtar Lubis) to read colour and anti-Western feeling into every attitude he encounters. Padhye argues Wright downplays Nehru's own impatience with the racial and religious rhetoric at Bandung and glosses over the delegates' silence on Chinese aggression against Burma and on Soviet 'colonialism' in Eastern Europe, concluding the book is 'eminently readable though not equally rewarding.'

- Wright's earlier work (Black Boy, his chapter in The God That Failed) is praised, setting up disappointment with The Colour Curtain.
- Cites the Indonesian writer Mochtar Lubis's charge, made in Encounter, that Wright viewed Bandung through 'coloured glasses.'
- Argues Wright underplays Nehru's discomfort with racial/religious rhetoric at the conference and its ambivalence toward Communist colonialism.
- Notes Bandung produced little real economic or cultural cooperation and no protest against China's Burma border violation.
- Final verdict: illuminating in parts (interviews, vignettes) but not an organically successful book.

### Integration of States by V. P. Menon
*By P. Kodanda Rao*

P. Kodanda Rao reviews V. P. Menon's account of the integration of the Princely States, crediting Sardar Patel's trust in his lieutenant Menon and their combined 'ruthless daring and incredible speed' for achieving what he calls a near-impossible feat within two months of Independence. He recounts how the departing British granted the Princes technically full sovereignty rather than transferring paramountcy to Delhi, creating over 600 nominally independent states that Patel and Menon then absorbed via a policy of limited, then complete, accession. He singles out Kashmir as the one case where Patel and Menon were not given a free hand -- the issue was moved to the Ministry of External Affairs under Nehru once the United Nations was invoked -- and laments that Kashmir remains unresolved as a result.

- Credits Sardar Patel's leadership style (delegating full trust to Menon) and Menon's own energy for the integration's success.
- Explains the British 'gift' of full, separate sovereignty to every Princely State on lapse of paramountcy as needlessly chaotic.
- Details the policy of limited-then-complete accession that absorbed most states, including holdouts like Travancore and Hyderabad.
- Presents Kashmir as the singular failure: the Maharaja delayed, Mountbatten's advice is disputed, and the issue was later moved out of Patel/Menon's Ministry of States to Nehru's External Affairs Ministry once the UN was invoked.
- States as fact that Menon's book was 'screened by the present Government of India before publication.'

### The Time of the Assassins by Henry Miller
*By Lola Chatterji*

Lola Chatterji dismisses Henry Miller's The Time of the Assassins, a study of Arthur Rimbaud published for his centenary, as a self-indulgent projection of Miller's own biography onto Rimbaud's, arguing Miller confuses appreciation of a poet's work with excusing or romanticising his personal 'faults', and that the book's 'sonorous' prose obscures rather than illuminates its subject. She insists a poet must be judged by 'his handiwork' alone and that Rimbaud's poetry, not his vagabondage, is what has earned him lasting influence.

- Miller's claimed qualification for the biography is a supposed personal resemblance to Rimbaud, which Chatterji finds overworked and unconvincing.
- Situates Rimbaud within the broader 1880s-90s European movement of nihilism/aestheticism (Verlaine, Baudelaire, the Yellow Book group) rather than as an isolated 'lonely rebel.'
- Criticises Miller for treating the poet as an 'unacknowledged ruler of the world' exempt from ordinary standards of interpretation.
- Argues an artist's biography is at most background, not an excuse for or explanation that supersedes judging the actual work.
- Concludes the book's 163 pages are not worth Miller's 'sonorous' but confused prose.

### The Republic of Indonesia by Dorothy Woodman
*By S. Natarajan*

S. Natarajan praises Dorothy Woodman's The Republic of Indonesia for its historical account of sustained Indonesian resistance to Dutch rule and the accelerated nationalism produced under Japanese occupation, and draws repeated parallels with India -- language policy, the unitary versus federal question, and education, where Woodman shows the Republic building six universities from a base of fewer than 1,000 university-educated Indonesians. Natarajan's main criticism is that Woodman fails to distinguish community-development models properly (comparing India unfavourably on this point) and gives too little space to Indonesia's adoption of a secular national ideal.

- Woodman frames Indonesian nationalism as continuous resistance to Dutch rule, intensified rather than broken by Japanese occupation.
- Draws Indo-Indonesian parallels: language policy (Bahasa Indonesia versus English), and the ease of unitary administration compared to India's federal structure.
- Highlights Woodman's account of the post-Independence expansion of higher education from a tiny colonial-era base.
- Criticises Woodman for not properly distinguishing community-development approaches and for underplaying Indonesia's secular national ideal.
- Notes Woodman's attention to the prominent role of Indonesian women in the new republic.

### What the United States Can do About India by E. Seligman
*By A. H. Somjee*

A. H. Somjee reviews Eustace Seligman's What the United States Can Do About India, describing it as a well-intentioned but conceptually dated attempt to convince American readers that India, though non-aligned and often critical of Western policy, is fundamentally on the side of democracy against Communism and therefore deserves warmer US support. Somjee's central objection is that Seligman tries to place India on an obsolete binary ideological map (democratic versus communist, with a hasty 'Indian Socialism' chapter bolted on) when India's actual programme is best understood as pragmatic experimentalism rather than doctrine, and that simply telling Americans India is 'with democracy' risks a further disillusionment rather than genuine understanding.

- Seligman, a lawyer and former colleague of John Foster Dulles, spent six weeks interviewing Indian public figures for the book.
- His thesis: India, despite non-alignment and criticism of Western military alliances, is fundamentally committed to democracy over communism.
- Somjee argues Seligman never asks whether Indians see America itself as genuinely democratic, an omission shared by many Western commentators.
- Criticises the 'Indian Socialism' chapter as a hasty attempt to relocate India on an already obsolete East-West ideological map.
- Concludes India's economic and political programme is experimental and independent rather than classifiable on Seligman's binary map, and that oversimplifying this to US audiences risks backfiring.

### The Legacy of the Lokamanya by Theodore L. Shay
*By S. R. Tikekar*

S. R. Tikekar welcomes Theodore L. Shay's The Legacy of the Lokamanya as a fresh, sympathetic outsider's reading of Bal Gangadhar Tilak's political philosophy, arguing Shay's very distance from India (and reliance on translated Marathi sources) gave him clarity that closer, partisan Indian commentators lacked.
