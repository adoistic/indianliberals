# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 20 (Jan-Mar 1959) is the first issue examined in this run to appear fully under the quarterly masthead of editors Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta, with an advisory board of Nirad C. Chaudhuri, Sudhin Datta and Laxmanshastri Joshi. Its centre of gravity is the crisis of Cold War liberalism: Michael Polanyi's opening essay diagnoses a 'modern dynamism' running from the French Revolution through Stalinism and reads the 1956 Hungarian and Polish revisionists as a reactionary return to 1848 liberal idealism, while Sibnarayan Ray's essay on the banned Dr. Zhivago and Herbert Luthy's long review-article dismantling Fritz Sternberg's Marxist world history extend the same argument about the exhaustion of revolutionary dogma. A second cluster of pieces -- P. Spratt's psychoanalytic essay on Indian 'uniqueness', Humayun Kabir's essay on federalism, and an anonymous ('A. D.') diagnosis of pro-Soviet 'neutralism' among Indian intellectuals -- turns the same Cold War lens on India itself. A third cluster is linguistic and literary: three separate essays on Urdu, Hindi and the sociology of language, P. C. Chatterji on the ontology of the 'art object', poems (including Bengali, English and four classical Korean poems in translation), Benjamin Polk on architecture and town planning, and a full page of book reviews (Zhivago, Camus, Toulmin, M. R. Jaykar's memoirs, and a Marathi short-story collection) plus a public exchange between Shankar Trivedi and C. Rajagopalachari on unilateral disarmament, and a correspondence column debating the decline of the Indian intellectual.

## Essays

### Tyranny and Freedom
*By Michael Polanyi*

Polanyi argues that the single 'master idea' driving twentieth-century upheaval is the doctrine of unlimited social self-determination, born in the French Revolution, which split into two streams: a revolutionary dynamism that treats law and morality as mere instruments of a single omnipotent centre of power, and a reformist/parliamentary dynamism that extends the rule of law by persuasion. He reads Stalinism as the fullest expression of the first stream, and interprets Pasternak's 'thaw', Tito's 1948 break, and the 1956 Hungarian and Polish revisionists as evidence that revolutionary dynamism in the Soviet bloc has passed its peak and is receding -- a reactionary (in his own, approving, sense) return to the liberal ideals of 1848. He closes by warning against defining democracy procedurally (rule of law plus universal suffrage) without an explicit, admittedly fiduciary, commitment to specific liberal ideals, since procedure alone could as easily uphold a Spanish Inquisition or a Stalinist orthodoxy.

- Locates the source of all modern political dynamism in the French Revolution's principle of unlimited social self-determination.
- Distinguishes revolutionary dynamism (which destroys legality and denies the reality of moral motives) from reformist/parliamentary dynamism (which extends legality by persuasion).
- Treats Stalinist tyranny as revolutionary dynamism's most complete form, absorbing all ideals of justice into 'the internal fuel of its political machinery'.
- Reads the 1956 Hungarian and Polish revisionists, whom he calls 'reactionaries', as a return to the liberal-idealist principles of 1848 against Leninist orthodoxy.
- Argues that parliamentary procedure alone cannot define or guarantee a free society; a free society requires an explicit, faith-like commitment to specific ideals of truth and human responsibility.
- Surveys newly independent states (India, Israel, Egypt, Tunisia, Lebanon) briefly, suggesting each is negotiating the same dynamism/liberty tension in its own way.

### The Uniqueness of India
*By P. Spratt*

Spratt asks why India and Europe, despite similar ethnic origins, diverged so sharply in temperament -- India more 'religious, philosophical... introverted, restrained, pessimistic... tolerant, docile' -- and rejects Marxist unilinear explanations (Dange, Kosambi) as unable to account for difference rather than similarity between civilizations. Drawing on V. Gordon Childe, Hamdi Bey and Maine, he argues India's key institution is caste, itself sustained by a persistently 'mother-centred' (as opposed to Europe's 'father-centred') psychic type in the Freudian-Oedipal sense he borrows from Hamdi Bey, reinforced by yogic techniques of sublimating aggression inward rather than outward. He traces Europe's shift toward the 'father-centred' type to the Roman reception of Stoic Natural Law and later to centuries of Christian sexual discipline, and closes by noting -- without endorsing either side -- that Gandhi, 'the greatest leader of India out of tribalism into nationhood', himself admired the Roman Church's clerical celibacy.

- Frames the problem as explaining civilizational difference, not likeness, against Marxist 'unilinear' historiography.
- Credits Hamdi Bey's psychoanalytic theory: Indian civilisation remains 'mother-centred' (weak Oedipal rebellion, tolerant, low psychic tension) while Europe became 'father-centred' (punitive conscience, aggressive, expansionist).
- Argues caste, not race or economic class, is the key institution explaining Indian conservatism, following A. M. Hocart against both Marxist class-theory and British race-theory readings of varna.
- Presents yoga as a technique that sublimates aggression inward (via chakra ascent to identification with the mother-goddess) rather than repressing it outward as Christianity does, reinforcing gerontocracy and mother-centred conservatism.
- Traces Europe's father-centred turn to the Roman adoption of Stoic 'Law of Nature' and later to the Church's long campaign against tribal sexual mores.
- Explicitly declines to prescribe: 'I am not advocating Gandhian puritanism or the European example... My concern has been solely with the past and the facts.'

### India and Federalism
*By Humayun Kabir*

Kabir argues that federalism is not a modern expedient but a structural universal of human society, present from the family-as-primitive-state through the village panchayat, and that India in particular elevated it into 'a philosophy of life' -- unity-in-diversity -- rather than treating it, as most nations do, as a grudging necessity. He surveys the family as the original federated unit, the slow transfer of power from family to state, and the long survival of village self-government in India despite successive empires, before turning to the Government of India Act 1935 and the post-Independence debate between centralisers (citing scale economies, defence, and the risk of linguistic sub-nationalism) and decentralisers (citing India's sheer diversity and administrative delay). His conclusion is that India's own tradition of 'unity in diversity' -- Hindu society's historical ability to absorb heterogeneity without homogenising it -- is itself the resource that can reconcile the rival claims of central planning and provincial autonomy.

- Claims federalism is inherent in the very concept of the State, visible even in social-contract and organic theories of sovereignty.
- Traces the shift of authority from family to State via agriculture, warfare, and growing commercial complexity.
- Argues India's federal character survived unusually long because village panchayats retained real local sovereignty regardless of which empire or dynasty ruled at the centre.
- Reviews the failed 1935 federal scheme (diarchical, weighted toward vested interests) and explains why Congress and the Muslim League were, in retrospect, wrong to have opposed it in favour of provincial autonomy alone.
- Sets out both sides of the post-1947 centralisation/decentralisation debate (scale and defence economies vs. risk of administrative delay and linguistic sub-nationalism).
- Concludes that Hindu society's historic capacity for 'unity in diversity' -- not a Hegelian synthesis that erases its parts -- is the distinctively Indian resource for reconciling the debate.

### What is the Art Object
*By P. C. Chatterji*

Chatterji asks what, exactly, 'the art object' is -- not simply the printed score, canvas, or page, since a work like Beethoven's Violin Concerto or Keats's 'Ode to a Nightingale' must be more than its physical trace. He surveys and rejects two rival theories: the 'solipsistic' view that each reader's re-creation is a different, equally valid poem, and the Croce-Collingwood 'expressionist' view that the art object exists only in the artist's own private act of creation and cannot survive it. Working mainly with poetry (via I. A. Richards's four-part account of meaning -- sense, feeling, tone, intention) and close readings of Gerard Manley Hopkins and Robert Browning, he argues criticism can, contrary to the solipsists, discriminate correct from incorrect readings, and that the art object is best understood as an objective 'pattern' or 'configuration' latent in the physical stimuli -- discoverable, like a figure hidden in a colour-blindness test card, by a sufficiently sensitive and patient viewer.

- Opens with Beethoven's Violin Concerto to show the art object cannot simply be identified with its physical score or performance.
- Rejects the 'solipsistic' view that there is no single poem, only as many poems as there are readings.
- Rejects the Croce-Collingwood view that the art object exists only inside the artist's private, unrepeatable act of creation.
- Uses I. A. Richards's four elements of meaning (sense, feeling, tone, intention) and close readings of Hopkins's 'Peace' and Browning to show criticism can adjudicate between competing interpretations.
- Proposes his own resolution: the art object is an objective pattern latent in the physical stimuli, found rather than invented by a sufficiently sensitive and patient critic.
- Uses the analogy of an Ishihara-style colour-vision test card: only the reader with 'correct vision' and patience will see the true pattern rather than none or a false one.

### Modern Urdu Literature
*By Gurbachan Singh Talib*

Talib surveys Urdu literature a decade after Partition, arguing the language was placed 'in something like a quandary': stripped of its dominant social position in the areas of India where it had evolved for centuries, while its main centre of continued growth -- with state patronage and Iqbal's towering legacy -- shifted to Pakistan's Punjab. He traces Urdu's earlier history as the possession of a small, socially insulated eighteenth-century elite (producing exquisite but reality-divorced verse) and its transformation after the 1857 Revolt forced that elite into new professions and closer contact with ordinary life, yielding new realism in prose and verse. He concludes that post-Independence Indian Urdu writing, though not producing dramatically new departures in only a decade, is discarding merely 'ephemeral or capricious' experiments (free verse, stream-of-consciousness, faddish emphasis on sex) in favour of more mature, socially engaged realism -- unhappily but not disastrously diminished, not 'a dried up stream'.

- Argues Partition placed Urdu 'in something like a quandary', losing its former social and educational dominance in India.
- Notes Urdu's centre of literary growth shifted to Pakistani Punjab, heir to Iqbal's tradition and backed by state patronage.
- Recounts Urdu's origin among a small, socially cloistered 18th-century North Indian Muslim elite producing finished but reality-divorced verse.
- Credits the 1857 Revolt's aftermath with forcing this class into new professions and closer contact with ordinary life, bringing new realism into Urdu prose and verse.
- Reports that post-Independence Indian Urdu writing is shedding faddish techniques (free verse, stream-of-consciousness, sex-emphasis) for more mature social realism.
- Insists India's contribution 'is in no way inferior to that of Pakistan' despite the shift of the language's institutional centre.

### Hindi
*By M. S. Kalyanasundaram*

Kalyanasundaram, a South Indian Hindi teacher, argues for pragmatic reform of Hindi as the proposed common official language of India, setting aside the political controversy over Southern resistance to Hindi's imposition. He itemises what he considers genuine structural defects for a learner: unreliable and inconsistent gender assignment for inanimate/abstract nouns, an ambiguous direct/indirect-narration construction around 'ki', and the grammatically 'irrational' ergative postposition 'ne' in the past transitive, which he wants dropped in favour of a simpler subject-verb agreement pattern. He also proposes simplifying Hindi numerals (which invert and irregularly mutate at the tens-ones junction) and criticises Sanskrit neologisms coined by language planners (e.g., 'yantra' for machine) as needlessly displacing already-familiar loanwords like 'machine' that ordinary people already use. He closes with a slogan distinguishing Hindi-as-mother-tongue from Hindi-as-national-language: 'Make it rational; it becomes National.'

- Deliberately brackets the North-South political controversy over Hindi imposition to focus on the language's internal learnability.
- Identifies unpredictable grammatical gender for inanimate/abstract nouns as the first major structural defect for learners.
- Flags the ambiguous 'ki'-clause construction (direct vs indirect speech) as a second defect, expecting English-style indirect narration to eventually prevail.
- Argues the ergative-like postposition 'ne' in transitive past tense is 'not rational' and should be dropped, citing errors even among native Hindi-speaking clerks.
- Criticises invented Sanskritic neologisms (e.g. 'yantra', 'vimaan') as displacing loanwords (machine, aeroplane) ordinary people already use, serving neither professional nor mass audiences.
- Proposes simplifying irregular Hindi numeral formation (e.g. the 'one-short-of' pattern for 19, 29, 39) toward a more uniform decimal pattern.
- Closes with the reformist slogan 'Make it rational; it becomes National.'

### Language and Audience
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

Punya Sloka Ray argues that meaning, style and even the acceptability of a loanword are all determined not by an individual speaker but by the audience being addressed -- 'It is not Humpty Dumpty who chooses but his hearers who choose which are acceptable forms and which are not.' He illustrates this at every level, from pronunciation ('haspataal' vs 'hospital') to register (talking to a spouse, students, or magistrates), and diagnoses writing failures as lapses in maintaining a stable, appropriate mental image of one's audience. He then turns this audience-centred theory against India's technical-terminology planners, arguing their invented Sanskrit-derived coinages address no real audience -- neither professionals (who need internationally shared vocabulary) nor ordinary people (who already use loanwords like 'position' or 'police') -- and diagnoses the whole federal-language debate as treating language as 'a symbol of social identity... rather than... communication.' He ends on a personal admission: as a modern Indian he is a 'guest-member' of English when addressing professional peers, with no fully satisfying solution to the resulting loss of spontaneity.

- States the essay's central thesis: audience, not speaker intention, ultimately fixes linguistic meaning and acceptable form.
- Illustrates with pronunciation, vocabulary and stylistic register across multiple real-life audiences (spouse, students, colleagues).
- Diagnoses failed communication -- his own example of a philosopher colleague -- as a rigid failure to picture one's actual audience while writing.
- Applies the theory critically to India's manufactured technical vocabulary, arguing invented Sanskrit terms serve no real audience, professional or popular.
- Frames the federal-language controversy as a category error: treating language primarily as an emblem of identity and domination rather than as a tool of communication and persuasion.
- Admits his own predicament as an Indian scholar: fluent only as a 'guest-member' of English for professional work, with no clean solution offered.

### Boris Pasternak
*By Sibnarayan Ray*

Sibnarayan Ray frames Boris Pasternak's persecution as the culmination of a structural conflict, going back to Plato's Republic, between the totalitarian order's need to reduce citizens to 'functional stereotypes' and the artist's irreducible individuality. He sketches Soviet literary history from the brief post-Revolutionary freedom, through Stalinist 'Party-mindedness' and Zhdanovism, the 1953-56 'thaw' (Ehrenburg, Pomerantsev, the Second Writers' Congress), and Khrushchev's re-imposed controls from 1957, culminating in the campaign against Dr. Zhivago and Pasternak's expulsion from the Writers' Union. He then argues Dr. Zhivago is the only truly great work of Soviet prose fiction, not because it argues against communism polemically but because its humanism -- its insistence on 'inward reticence', on the irreducible particular over the collective, on contemplation over pitiless certainty -- is inherently unassimilable to any closed ideological system, which is precisely why the regime found it intolerable.

- Opens with Plato's Republic as the paradigm case of the totalitarian state's fear of the poet's uncontrollable individuality.
- Traces Soviet literary repression from Lenin's 1905 dictum that literature must be 'a small cog and screw' through Stalinist Party-mindedness to the 1953-56 thaw and its 1957 reversal under Khrushchev.
- Cites named 1954-56 Soviet dissenting voices (Olga Bergholtz, Veniamin Kaverin, Mikhail Sholokhov, Ehrenburg, Pomerantsev) as evidence of a genuine, then crushed, movement for artistic freedom.
- Argues Dr. Zhivago's greatness lies in its combination of lyric and epic scope comparable to Stendhal, Tolstoy, Dostoevsky and Proust.
- Insists the novel is dangerous to the regime not chiefly through explicit political criticism but through its humanist spirit -- valuing the 'inward reticence' of the individual over reified collectivity.
- Closes with Andre Chanson's personal reminiscence of Pasternak's character and the phrase 'Life is more generous, all the same, and above all lighter.'

### Exchange
*By Amiya Chakravarti*

A short lyric of loss and consolation translated from Bengali: the speaker catalogues what has been received 'instead of her' -- reflected light on a pond, a bough of blossoms, a cloud of violets filling 'all your heart' -- concluding that 'the empty heart holds all one looks upon', and closing on an ambiguous question about whether the leaving (of the beloved, or of grief itself) was ever complete.

- A short elegiac lyric structured as a catalogue of natural images received 'instead of' the lost beloved.
- Turns on the paradox that an empty heart can hold everything it looks upon.
- Ends on an unresolved question about whether the act of leaving/grieving was ever finished.

### Eagle Into Swan
*By Evelyn Wood*

A sonnet imagining an ageing eagle transformed into a swan: its powers of flight failing, it finds its only remaining triumph is 'to be at rest, in thought's relief', while still regretting the 'trackless air' where its flight once 'can leave no trace'.

- Sonnet using the eagle/swan transformation as a figure for ageing and diminished power.
- Locates the aged bird's one remaining triumph in rest and contemplation rather than continued flight.
- Closes on regret for the freedom of trackless, traceless flight now lost.

### Early Korean Poems
*By Chong Mong-ju, etc*

Four short classical Korean sijo (occasional verse), spanning the 14th to 19th centuries and translated by Peter Hyun: Chong Mong-ju's oath of unchanging loyalty even through death and dissolution; Whang Chin-yi's image of cutting a long winter night in two to save half for a lover; Yi Won-ik's meditation on ties that cannot truly bind if the loved one chooses to leave; and An Min-yong's assurance that no wintry despoiling of the plum-blossom can prevent spring's eventual return. A brief editorial note explains the sijo form's origins in the 12th century and its combination of Provençal-style spirit with epigrammatic wit.

- Presents four sijo (traditional Korean occasional verse) spanning roughly the 14th to 19th centuries, translated by Peter Hyun.
- Chong Mong-ju (1337-1392) vows unchanging loyalty even through repeated death.
- Whang Chin-yi (15th c.) imagines cutting a winter night in two to save time with a lover.
- Yi Won-ik (15th c.) questions whether any tie can hold if the beloved chooses to leave.
- An Min-yong (c.1850) closes on the assurance that winter's despoiling cannot stop spring's return.
- An editorial note situates the sijo form historically, comparing it to old Provençal verse for its wit and technical discipline.

### Winter
*By Amaresh Datta*

A bleak free-verse poem built from a chain of harsh images of a wintry, violent world -- a girl's 'truncated body... cold now by a railway track', hopes that 'bark like way-side dogs round the dustbin' -- returning three times to the refrain 'I seek the truth' as the only motive that survives despair, hunger, and 'mighty wars' that 'convulse the sky'.

- Builds a sustained winter/violence image-chain (frost, a corpse by a railway track, barking hopes, erupting rock) to figure a bleak contemporary world.
- Repeats the refrain 'I seek the truth' as the poem's only affirmative, survivable motive.
- Closes amid 'mighty wars' convulsing the sky, without resolving the despair the poem has built.

### On a Dead Freedom-Fighter
*By F. R. Stanley*

An elegy for an anonymous street fighter shot dead mid-afternoon, his death framed with explicit Christ imagery ('Christ-like his body lies, / broken between thieves') and read as both instantly forgettable to a 'generation in whom the old hunger... has died' and permanently unforgivable, destined to end as 'the martyr's fable' -- one more cross among ten thousand crosses.

- Elegises an unnamed freedom-fighter killed by a bullet in the street, observed by passers-by moved to pity, terror, or shame.
- Uses explicit Christ/crucifixion imagery ('broken between thieves') to sacralise the death.
- Argues the fighter's own generation, having lost 'the old hunger, the old desire', will reduce his death to a forgettable historical footnote even while it remains unforgivable.
- Ends flatly on 'He will be dead', refusing any redemptive uplift.

### Indian Intellectuals and Communism
*By A. D.*

Writing under the initials 'A. D.', the author diagnoses the 'average Indian intellectual' as a 'neutralist with a bias' toward communism, explained by the USSR's rapid industrialisation, dictatorship's appeal amid social disintegration, and above all by asymmetric historical memory: direct experience of Western imperial 'crimes' versus no comparable direct experience of Soviet ones, so that 'the Western powers are old convicts... while the Communist governments are considered innocent until crime is proved.' The essay catalogues absurd equivalences intellectuals draw between Western and Soviet restrictions on dissent, then explains why, despite this bias, most Indian intellectuals stop short of communism itself: residual admiration for British justice and parliamentary rule, the deep hold of religion (which tolerates but cannot wholly assimilate to atheistic Marxism), and above all nationalism, since 'the communist has sold his soul to Moscow' remains a widely (if not always fairly) held belief, reinforced by the Communist Party of India's habit of echoing Kremlin lines with a lag.

- Names the average Indian intellectual's stance as 'neutralist -- with a bias' toward the Soviet Union.
- Explains the bias via direct, remembered experience of Western imperialism versus no comparable experience of Soviet imperialism.
- Cites absurd equivalences drawn between Soviet and Western restrictions on free speech as a symptom of this bias.
- Identifies three countervailing forces preventing full identification with communism: residual respect for British rule of law, the resilience of religious tradition, and Indian nationalist suspicion of Moscow's control over the CPI.
- Cites Nehru's frequent complaint that the CPI 'has lost the habit of independent thinking', always trailing Kremlin policy shifts.
- Closes hoping India can find its own synthesis of reason and religion (citing Raja Rammohan Roy as a 19th-century precedent) rather than let Cold War polarisation or 'untempered militant materialism' decide the outcome.

### Town Planning and Architecture
*By Benjamin Polk*

Polk argues that town planning is not 'office work' or 'drawing-board thinking' but an extension of architecture rooted in direct, embodied attention to the 'spirit of the place' -- climate, vegetation, topography, and the small human-scale neighbourhood group. He draws on Gaston Bardet's 'social topography' survey method and Patrick Geddes's 'conservative surgery' (readjustment rather than wholesale rebuilding, notably applied by Geddes to Indian cities) to argue for humane, walkable, mixed-use neighbourhoods over zoned, car-centred, drawing-board master plans. Citing Eric Fromm on alienation and the Tao Te Ching on non-domineering action, he closes with a near-mystical account of architecture as discovery rather than conquest -- a 'converse with the tutelary genius of the Place' -- and warns, citing the ecological collapse of Roman-irrigated North Africa after the Vandal conquest, that mishandled man-made landscape can permanently destroy a civilization's foundations.

- Argues true town planning has always grown from embodied, on-the-ground attention to site and 'spirit of the place', not from abstract drawing-board schemes.
- Cites Gaston Bardet's 'social topography' survey technique and Patrick Geddes's 'conservative surgery' (readjustment, not rebuilding), noting Geddes applied it to Indian cities.
- Criticises rigid zoning that separates industry from residence and cars-first planning that disintegrates the walkable medieval town pattern.
- Draws on Eric Fromm's Man For Himself to diagnose modern alienation as loss of a sense of individual significance under mass production and mass entertainment.
- Invokes the Tao Te Ching's non-domineering 'Way of Heaven' as a model for architecture that discovers rather than conquers its site.
- Warns, citing the desertification of Roman North Africa after the Vandal conquest, that man-made landscape mismanagement can permanently destroy a civilization's material base.

### Fearlessness and Civilization
*By Shankar Trivedi*

Trivedi respectfully but pointedly challenges C. Rajagopalachari's earlier Quest essay on 'Fearlessness and Civilization', arguing Rajaji conflates two different sources of a disarmed, fearless citizenry: the rare individual sage's total detachment from worldly goods, versus (the actual, general case) confidence inspired by an effective state monopoly on protecting property.
