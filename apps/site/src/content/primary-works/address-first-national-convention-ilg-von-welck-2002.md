# Address at the First National Convention of the Indian Liberal Group

*By Hubertus von Welck (Friedrich-Naumann-Stiftung) · Hyderabad, 6 December 2002*

## Summary

Address at the First National Convention of the Indian Liberal Group is a speech delivered on 6 December 2002 in Hyderabad by Hubertus von Welck, Regional Director of the Friedrich-Naumann-Stiftung in New Delhi, who welcomes the ILG's first national gathering as an exciting phase for Indian liberalism and conveys the good wishes of the Foundation's Chairman, Count Lambsdorff. Framing his remarks around some basic liberal principles linked to good governance, von Welck sets out the classical-liberal case that the freedom of the individual, protected by human rights, the rule of law and democracy, is the essential precondition for any country's social and economic development.

The heart of the address is a nuanced, liberal defence of democracy. Von Welck argues that liberals support democracy "as long as democracy promotes freedom and does not engender tyranny," but that it is by no means the sole solution to public problems: majorities can err, minorities and fundamental rights must be protected even against the majority, and power must be limited through checks and balances, the rule of law and what he calls "small government." He stresses the principle of subsidiarity — that decisions be taken at the lowest possible level — warns against both over-reliance on democracy and the danger of an "illiberal democracy," and urges liberals to "focus on freedom first," a phrase he ties to the title of the ILG's own magazine, Freedom First. He then reads out the Liberal International resolution on good governance adopted at its 51st Congress in Budapest in March 2002, which calls for returning power to individuals, separation of powers, decentralisation, transparency, free trade, a social market economy and the fight against poverty and corruption.

Turning directly to the ILG, von Welck presents good governance as inexpensive, transparent, accountable and decentralised — favouring privatisation and citizens' initiative — and praises concrete chapter work such as the Bangalore chapter's public-transport privatisation proposal and electoral-reform efforts in Gujarat. He then presses the group with a series of strategic questions: whether it means to be a pressure group or a political party, how it will build networks, activate its chapters, motivate members and set a three-to-five-year plan. Commending Mr. Raju for reviving the liberal movement in India and situating the ILG within a global family of 84 liberal parties across 67 countries, he closes with a plea to treat India's pluralism as enriching rather than threatening, expressing hope that the Indian Liberal Group can grow into a true Indian Liberal Movement with the Friedrich Naumann Foundation's support. (The author notes his debt to Dr. Stefan Melnik's paper "Democracy and freedom: general observations from a liberal point of view.")
