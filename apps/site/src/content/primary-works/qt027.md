# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 27 (Oct./Dec. 1960), the longest issue of the run so far at 130 pages, opens with Denis de Rougemont's retrospective address on the tenth anniversary of the Congress for Cultural Freedom, then turns to A. H. Somjee's diagnosis of why no Indian party can honestly claim the name 'socialist'. A. S. Ayyub's memorial tribute to the poet and Quest advisory editor Sudhindranath Datta (who had died only weeks earlier, on 25 June 1960) sits alongside Sterling P. Lamprecht's outsider's critique of dogmatic traditionalism in Indian university philosophy departments, C. L. Nahal's defence of D. H. Lawrence against the Lady Chatterley's Lover obscenity charge (with a dissenting extract from Katherine Anne Porter), and Dan Jacobson's dispatch on the psychology and near-term prospects of apartheid South Africa. Shorter pieces cover English schooling, the Tamil poet Kamban, five poems, a review-essay on modern Indian painters (Amrita Sher-Gil, an unnamed Sikh-nobleman portrait, and M. F. Husain), P. Lal's translation of the Savitri legend, two continuing epistolary debates (on the objectivity of value, and on direct versus representative democracy), B. G. Verghese's report from the Congress's Berlin conference, a books column, nine book reviews, and a heated correspondence exchange over whether Indian creative writers should write in English. The issue is bookended by a full contributors' page and closes on the perennial editorial argument, begun in Quest 26, that fiction and poetry by Indians in English remain the exception rather than the rule — a position the Writers Workshop group (P. Lal, Jai Ratan, Pradip Sen, Anita Desai, Deb Kumar Das, Sasthibrata Chakravarti) contests at length.

## Essays

### The Impact of Progress on Freedom
*By Denis de Rougemont*

Denis de Rougemont looks back on the first ten years of the Congress for Cultural Freedom, unpacking its name — Congress, Freedom, Culture — to argue that the Congress is not a political front but a standing effort by intellectuals to defend the preconditions of freedom. He locates danger to freedom not only in totalitarian systems but in poverty, in the West's own materialism, and above all in the meaninglessness that both dictatorship and mass affluence can produce; culture, he argues, is what restores meaning by combining tradition with criticism and invention. He closes by insisting freedom is not itself the ultimate value but the means by which individuals can risk, choose and find meaning, and that a society's health is measured by the chances it gives each person to do so, not by statistics.

- Traces the Congress for Cultural Freedom's ten-year evolution from an anti-dictatorship 'striking force' into a broader inquiry into the effects of technical and cultural progress on freedom.
- Defines culture as passing on tradition AND permitting 'criticism and disruption'; the two must stay balanced or each becomes dangerous.
- Names three complementary sources of renewal on the world stage: Western technology/intellect, African 'living soul' (song, dance, rhythm), and the Indian 'spirit'.
- Argues the loss of meaning in life, not political repression alone, is what most corrodes the will to be free.
- Concludes freedom is not an absolute end in itself but the means to the 'perpetual enhancement of the individual's possibility of taking personal risks'.

### Problems of an Indian Socialist Party
*By A. H. Somjee*

A. H. Somjee argues that Indian politics is saturated with parties claiming 'socialism' — Congress, the Communist Party, the Praja Socialist Party — yet none earns the label on European terms. He diagnoses four specifically Indian problems: too many rival 'socialisms', an 'incipient collectivism' oriented toward raising national income rather than redistributing it, excessive dependence on individual personalities (especially Nehru) rather than settled party principle, and naked electoral opportunism that treats a socialist label as a vote-getting device rather than a programme. His most pointed claim is that Congress 'socialism' is really just Nehru's personal conviction projected onto the party machine, and that the PSP is paralysed by personality clashes rather than doctrine.

- Distinguishes four Indian problems facing self-styled socialist parties: 'too many socialisms', incipient collectivism, personalism, and electoral opportunism.
- Contrasts European socialism's emphasis on redistributing existing wealth with the Indian emphasis on raising national income and capital investment first.
- Judges Congress's 'socialist pattern of society' to be Nehru's personal project rather than a genuinely internalised party doctrine — 'Nehru-deep'.
- Describes the Praja Socialist Party as riven by ex-Congressmen, ex-Communists and Gandhians with no agreed theory, dominated by personalities rather than principle.
- Argues no Indian party contesting elections has ever done so purely on socialist grounds, instead courting 'maharajas, jagirdars, religious leaders... local lawyers, contractors, bootleggers, dadas' for votes.

### Sudhindranath Datta
*By Abu Sayeed Ayyub*

Abu Sayeed Ayyub's memorial essay on the poet Sudhindranath Datta (30 October 1901 – 25 June 1960), written within weeks of his death, argues that Datta's poetry drew its power from unresolved contradictions: a man of 'princely' good fortune whose verse sang of life's emptiness; a professed hard-line behaviourist materialist whose poems are wracked by an idealist's grief at a Godless, meaningless universe; a poet of classical prosodic discipline who wrote in the syntax of abstract discursive prose. Ayyub compares Datta's contradiction to Arthur Koestler's (whom he says he once challenged directly in Calcutta) and to Bishnu Dey's uneasy Marxism, concluding that Datta was 'balanced and whole though not unified' — an anekantavadi who accepted that no single worldview exhausts truth. The essay includes several stanzas of Datta's own poetry in translation, including from '1945' and 'Cyclone'.

- Frames Datta's biography (privileged upbringing, wide travel, recent return from a two-year US/European trip, sudden death at 59) against the bleak, 'despair'-illumined tone of his poetry.
- Identifies Datta's central contradiction as philosophical materialism at war with an emotional, near-idealist horror at a meaningless universe.
- Notes a formal contradiction too: classically disciplined Bengali prosody carrying the syntax of abstract, logical prose.
- Compares Datta's contradictions to Arthur Koestler's simultaneous belief in 'cosmic emotion' and description of the universe as 'lousy'.
- Credits Datta's 1931-40 quarterly Parichay with pioneering 'new poetry' and 'new criticism' in Bengali despite having 'few readers'.
- Quotes Datta's own translated verse (from the poems '1945' and 'Cyclone') to illustrate the despair he calls the poetry's true 'Zeit motif'.

### Philosophy in the Indian Universities
*By Sterling P. Lamprecht*

The American philosopher Sterling P. Lamprecht reports on visits to some twenty Indian university philosophy departments in 1958-59, concluding that Indian academic philosophy is 'frequently hampered by an uncritical allegiance' to what is treated as a single, unified 'Indian tradition' — usually equated, incorrectly in his view, with metaphysical idealism borrowed more from Anglo-Hegelian sources than native Sanskrit texts. He recounts a senior professor who insisted his own tradition was wholly true and all others largely false, and a seminar where a professor's 'intemperate harangue' silenced a student for ninety minutes. Lamprecht argues India's real opportunity — a synthesis of Eastern and Western thought free of the nationalistic bias that limits Bergson, Russell or Dewey — is being squandered by dogmatism on one side and student docility on the other, and closes urging 'humanism' (the 'middle way') over otherworldly asceticism as India's most needed philosophical message.

- Based on a 1958-59 tour of roughly twenty Indian universities' philosophy departments.
- Argues there is 'no such thing as the Indian tradition on any subject at all' — the Vedas, Upanishads, Pali texts and Sutras contain idealism, realism, materialism, theism and atheism alike.
- Contends that academic Indian idealism is imported Anglo-Hegelian technique (Bradley, Bosanquet) more than native Sanskrit philosophy.
- Reports dogmatic classroom dynamics: rote dictation, memorised lecture notes reproduced verbatim in exams, and one seminar where a professor's harangue prevented a student from ever making his point.
- Argues India's mixed intellectual inheritance gives it a unique chance to build a philosophy 'more widely significant... than philosophy has ever been before', but that promise so far exceeds achievement.
- Closes with a postscript letter to Ayyub noting his observations were confined mainly to Hindu professors, with only brief contact with Muslim teachers at Aligarh and Hyderabad.

### Lady Chatterley's Lover and Lawrence
*By C. L. Nahal*

C. L. Nahal defends D. H. Lawrence against the reputation for obscenity that followed Lady Chatterley's Lover, arguing Lawrence's real subject across his whole body of work is not 'sex' but 'self': a near-Vedantic conviction that ultimate reality can never be grasped by intellect alone and must be approached through reverence for the created, embodied world, of which sex is the most intense expression. Nahal concedes real weaknesses — a contrived plot premise (Clifford's disability is an accident, not organic impotence, undermining Connie's moral case for leaving him), didactic 'prejudged' characterisation, and language that 'overstepped its mark' — but insists the novel is a serious, moral (if inferior) work within Lawrence's larger, 'religiously' phallic vision, and that Lawrence's own life was strikingly puritanical despite his reputation. A boxed extract from Katherine Anne Porter's Encounter essay 'A Wreath for the Gamekeeper' is printed immediately after, giving the opposite, hostile view of the novel and the 'fraudulent crusade' mounted in its defence.

- Argues Lawrence's true theme across his career is 'self', not 'sex' — reality is approached through the finite, embodied world since 'the infinite can never be known as infinite'.
- Identifies a structural flaw: Clifford Chatterley's impotence stems from a war wound, not disease, which weakens the moral logic of Connie leaving him.
- Cites Lawrence's own private conduct (refusing to spend a night with Frieda before marriage) as evidence he was, personally, puritanical rather than licentious.
- Grants that Lawrence 'overstepped his mark' in his use of explicit sexual vocabulary, arguing the plea of 'artistic necessity' does not fully justify it.
- Concludes the novel is inferior to The Rainbow and Women in Love but still 'a serious and moral novel' when judged by the artist's motive rather than by taboo words alone.
- The editors append, without endorsement, a hostile extract from Katherine Anne Porter calling the book 'a very laboriously bad book' and mocking the 'publicity device' around its defence.

### Back to South Africa
*By Dan Jacobson*

Novelist Dan Jacobson, reporting on a visit home amid South Africa's 1960 political crisis (Sharpeville and its aftermath), argues that the country's overriding fact is not any single dramatic reckoning but vast empty space, poverty (white and black alike), and an Afrikaner nationalism forged from repeated historical defeat and English contempt. He analyses apartheid as a 'Big Lie' — the plan for 'Bantu National Homes' most South Africans will never see, and Verwoerd's literal vision of a city sliced like a cake by race — and predicts neither a single apocalyptic uprising nor indefinite stalemate, but 'a long series of crises' as growing Afrikaner prosperity makes leaders more willing to negotiate even as followers grow less able to bear rising world condemnation. He closes arguing that the racial 'purity' of white South Africans is itself already a fiction, and that the country's fate will be decided as much by ordinary human failings — 'indifference... lethargy... greed' — working against the government as by any grand ideological force.

- Describes South African apartheid policy as a fantastical 'Big Lie': the promised 'Bantu National Homes' are the existing, overcrowded Reserves, and Verwoerd's model city is literally 'round, and cut into segments radiating from a centre' by race.
- Contrasts the position of South African whites with French colons in Algeria: white South Africans have no metropolitan power to fall back on and 'will have to do their own fighting... with their own lives'.
- Predicts neither swift revolution nor indefinite stability but a prolonged, unpredictable series of crises, intensified rather than eased by growing Afrikaner prosperity.
- Argues world condemnation weakens Afrikaner Nationalist morale among followers even if it hardens some leaders' paranoia.
- Notes that 'the whites of South Africa are no longer white' in blood, undercutting the very racial 'purity' the Nationalist movement claims to defend.
- Explicitly reports, rather than endorses, the language and logic of segregationist and racist rhetoric of the period in order to dissect it; flagged here as historically dated, offensive terminology used critically, not approvingly, by the (white, liberal, anti-apartheid) author.

### Some Impressions of Schools in England
*By Lola Chatterjee*

Lola Chatterjee, an Indian teacher educated partly in England, tours several Harrow schools — a state Primary and Junior School, a Secondary Modern, and (briefly and less successfully) the famous Harrow public school itself — and reports admiringly on post-war British state education's move away from rote academic instruction toward 'activity-wise' learning, free school lunches, and vocational streaming, while also registering the strain on teachers of 'latch-key children' from busy or broken homes. She contrasts this favourably, and pointedly, with India's 'woefully inadequate' primary provision, closing with a call for free compulsory schooling, food, music and enough paid teachers.

- Visits a state Primary/Junior School (co-educational, LCC-run, post-war housing estate catchment) and finds an emphasis on activity-based learning over formal instruction.
- Introduces the concept of the 'latch-key child' — a child from a busy or broken home who lets themselves into an empty house after school — as a strain teachers must additionally manage.
- Describes the 1944 Education Act's tripartite Grammar/Technical/Secondary Modern system and visits a thriving Secondary Modern school with a student-built swimming pool and strong vocational options.
- Finds the famous Harrow public school itself disappointing to visit — more a 'historical monument' shown to tourists than a living school, with staff and pupils largely inaccessible.
- Closes by explicitly comparing England's provision unfavourably against India's 'woefully inadequate' primary education and calling for free schooling, food and adequately paid teachers.

### Kamban's Forte
*By Hephzibah Jesudasan*

Hephzibah Jesudasan argues that the Tamil poet Kamban's 'especial forte' is neither the beauty of his language (which she grants is often uneven) nor his descriptive imagery, but a Shakespeare-like gift for dramatic characterisation and psychological insight, which turns his Ramayana into something closer to a supreme drama than a conventional epic. She singles out his humanising treatment of Ravana — a villain 'only in that he is not able to be a saint' — and his restrained, indirect rendering of Rama's grief, arguing Kamban wrings sympathy even for the story's 'worst' characters in a way few poets, including Valmiki, Milton or Tennyson, ever manage, while acknowledging Kamban's genius overcame considerable orthodox religious opposition in his own time.

- Argues Kamban's central genius is dramatic/psychological, not merely stylistic — 'human interest really is Kamban's especial forte'.
- Notes Kamban faced orthodox Vaishnavite opposition in his own era for a relatively free-thinking Ramayana (e.g. Rama calling the hunter Guha his brother, and denying caste distinctions matter more than character).
- Contrasts Kamban's treatment of Ravana — sympathetic, humorous, tragic in defeat — with how thinly villains like Iago or Milton's Satan earn the reader's compassion.
- Praises Kamban's indirection in depicting emotion, as when Rama merely 'bites his lips' rather than lamenting outright for Sita.
- Ranks Kamban far above Valluvan and Ilango among Tamil poets, and above Valmiki as a dramatist of human passion.

### Water as Metaphor
*By A. K. Ramanujan*

A. K. Ramanujan's 'Water as Metaphor' uses tidal and oceanic imagery — currents that shape the boatman, wash away 'carious coasts', and dissolve 'father seeds' and 'mother-roots' — as a figure for forces that both sustain a 'landsman' self and threaten to dissolve its inherited identity into 'alien seas'.

- Builds an extended metaphor of ocean currents that both nourish (fishing, whaling) and erode (coastlines, ancestral roots).
- Turns from external, working-world imagery (boatmen, fishwives, whale-men) to an internal 'grief's own map of veins and rents'.
- Ends on an image of dissolution — 'drinking without gills the lees, the greensick light of alien seas'.

### Truants
*By A. K. Ramanujan*

A. K. Ramanujan's 'Truants' is a short, wry poem about the futility of disciplining children ('dulness dress them... a taste of cat-o'-nine fears') into conformity, since 'thoughts will be children' and will find ways to play and transgress regardless.

- Catalogues conventional disciplinary measures (washing, dressing plainly, corporal punishment) aimed at suppressing childish unruliness.
- Argues such measures fail because thought itself remains childlike and truant.
- Closes on an image of concealed rebellion: children who 'play at sin' 'under the skin'.

### Hope
*By Bal Patil*

Bal Patil's short poem 'Hope' compares hope to a loyal dog waiting patiently in a corner, ready to respond to 'a slight pat' and 'lick the bones' of one's desire.

- A brief, single-image poem comparing hope to a domestic animal's patient loyalty.
- Ends on the ambivalent image of hope 'licking the bones' of desire, suggesting both comfort and scraps.

### And these my words
*By Feroze Moos*

Feroze Moos's 'And these my words' is a quiet, imagistic poem about persistence and quiet renewal — words 'undisturbed and quiet' amid 'congealing dust' and falling autumn leaves — ending on an image of unseen, unheard change ('little understood leaves falling unstruck in a wind not seen before').

- Uses seasonal, natural imagery (autumn, leaves, wind) to figure quiet, unnoticed processes of change.
- Balances stillness ('still alive... involuntarily') against continuous, imperceptible transformation.

### In Remembrance
*By Haig Khatchadourian*

Haig Khatchadourian's 'In Remembrance' — described in the contributors' page as his first poem published in a professional venue — is a formal love-elegy about disciplining oneself to forget a lost intimacy 'now and again, almost casually', while hoping for a reunion 'on the other side' where the suppressed past can be 'taken out' again.

- A first-person address to a lost love, structured around the discipline of forced forgetting.
- Uses the image of packed-away porcelain/china to figure a treasured but deliberately unused memory.
- Ends on a hoped-for future reunion where the 'common past' can be reclaimed.

### Three Indian Painters
*By Jyotirmoy Datta*

Under the printed title 'Two Indianists and A Painter', Jyotirmoy Datta reviews R. de L. Furtado's album Three Painters, using Amrita Sher-Gil as his central case study of what he calls modern Indian art's central flaw: painters who set out consciously to be 'fundamentally Indian' rather than simply painting sincerely, producing art that is 'picturesque rubbish' created partly for the admiration of foreigners. He contrasts Sher-Gil's abrupt, deliberate 'conversion to Indianism' (decided in a Paris studio, he speculates, out of failure to make her mark as a European modernist) with Georges Rouault's slow, organic stylistic evolution, and with M. F. Husain, whom he singles out as the one painter of the three who borrows from Western modernism (Matisse's blank faces, rendered instead as a 'black mark') creatively rather than merely imitatively, without anxiously performing Indianness.

- Frames Amrita Sher-Gil as a romantic, mythologised figure whose real paintings are harder to see clearly than the legend around her.
- Argues Sher-Gil's 'conversion to Indianism', decided while still in Paris, was less an organic discovery than a deliberate career strategy after failing to find a distinctive style there.
- Diagnoses a broader problem in modern Indian painting: an anxious, conscious pursuit of a 'patently Indian manner' (unlike modern Bengali poetry, protected from this anxiety by its own language) which produces triviality.
- Contrasts Sher-Gil's and George Keyt's imitative borrowings from Cezanne/Gauguin and Picasso with M. F. Husain's more original transformation of a Matisse motif (the blank face, replaced by 'a black mark').
- Quotes and mildly mocks Furtado's introduction, which claims Indian art shows a 'ceaseless endeavour' of 'spirited new conceptions' comparable to industrial output figures.

### The Story of Savitri
*By P. Lal (translated by)*

P. Lal's translation from the Mahabharata retells the story of Savitri, who marries the doomed Satyavan knowing (via the sage Narada's prophecy) that he will die within a year, and who, when Yama the god of death comes personally to claim her husband's soul, follows him on foot, wins his admiration through a series of wise sayings about virtue and companionship, and extracts from him — as successive 'boons' granted for anything except Satyavan's life — the restoration of her blind father-in-law's sight and kingdom, a hundred sons for her own father, and finally, by asking for a hundred sons for herself and Satyavan (a boon impossible to fulfil without his life), tricks Yama into returning her husband to her. The frame narrative has the sage Markandeya tell this story to Yudhishthira as reassurance that Draupadi, too, will bring the Pandavas good fortune despite her sufferings.

- Savitri knowingly marries Satyavan despite Narada's prophecy that he will die within a year.
- When Yama comes in person to take Satyavan, Savitri follows on foot rather than turn back, winning Yama's respect through successive wise sayings.
- She wins boons restoring her father-in-law's sight and kingdom and granting her own father a hundred sons, before winning Satyavan's life itself by a clever final request that only he could fulfil.
- The story is framed as Markandeya's consolation to Yudhishthira that Draupadi, like Savitri, will bring good fortune out of suffering.
- The translation preserves formulaic epic dialogue and repeated ritual phrasing characteristic of the Sanskrit original.

### Social Norms in Value-judgements
*By Manashi Das Gupta and A. S. Ayyub*

Continuing a debate begun in Quest 25, Manashi Das Gupta presses Abu Sayeed Ayyub on his claim that 'higher' values (truth, beauty) can be simultaneously personal and objective without resting on social consensus or external criteria, asking what moral obligation attaches to objective values and how his position differs from defining value as mere persistent liking. Ayyub replies by distinguishing four senses of 'good' (bare desire; lifetime satisfaction/prudence; the value of a personality, tied to duty; and the 'eternal values' of truth, beauty and moral perfection, tied to personal morality), arguing that only the last kind generates a non-universal, self-binding 'ought', and that consensus or external aesthetic criteria (he cites conflicting judgements of Whitehead, Edith Sitwell and Nandalal Bose) cannot settle higher-value disputes — only 'the depth, durability and felt rightness of one's own experience' can, with correction possible only through further value-intuition, not external adjudication.

- Das Gupta questions whether 'objective' values can exist without either social confirmation or an inherent moral obligation attached to them.
- Ayyub distinguishes four types of 'good' — desired object, lifetime-satisfaction ('prudence'), personality-value (tied to duty), and 'eternal values' of truth/beauty/moral perfection (tied to 'personal morality').
- Ayyub argues appreciating an objective aesthetic value (e.g. Othello's excellence) carries no universal moral obligation in the way a genuine moral duty does.
- He rejects both social-consensus and 'external criteria' theories of aesthetic value, citing disagreement over Whitehead, Edith Sitwell and Nandalal Bose as evidence no reliable consensus exists.
