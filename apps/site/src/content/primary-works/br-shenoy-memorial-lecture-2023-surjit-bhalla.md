summary: |-
  In the 2023 B.R. Shenoy Memorial Lecture, economist Surjit Bhalla defines a true liberal in the 19th-century, Hayekian sense as one who speaks truth to power and, in Keynes's spirit, changes his mind when the facts change. He frames Shenoy, an admirer of Hayek, as a lonely, courageous dissenter whose free-market, export-led, flexible-exchange-rate advice to Sri Lanka in 1966 was rejected there but resembled what Singapore later implemented. Bhalla's central theme is the difficulty of identifying truth amid social-media noise, spin and 'narratives', urging listeners to demand evidence and apply a 'smell test' to data.

  He illustrates 'illiberal' institutional behaviour with his own experiences: at the World Bank around 1980, his finding that calorie-based poverty lines would classify most Americans as poor was rejected for publication; at the IMF in 2020, his paper 'COVID versus Lockdowns, COVID Wins', showing lockdowns were ineffective and that Sweden's excess deaths matched Denmark's, was refused a seminar and later vindicated by a 2022 Lancet study. Turning to India, he uses data to contest several popular claims: that Bangladesh is richer than India (arguing Bangladesh's nominal GDP was upgraded an implausible 79%), that Indian GDP growth is really 3.5% rather than 6.5% (noting the same electricity-based method flags Germany as the biggest overstater), that investment is too low (real investment-to-GDP is near record highs), that India needs 100-200 million jobs (UN data implies only about 5 million net jobs are needed), and above all that female labour force participation is 8-10% (which he calls junk, citing PLFS data near 46%). He closes on gender wage gaps and poverty measurement, defending the PLFS/consumption data and criticising multidimensional poverty indices as adding negative value.
