# Quest

## Summary

Quest Vol. II No. 1 (August-September 1956) opens with an unsigned editorial attacking India's muted official reaction to Khrushchev's denunciation of Stalin, and closes with correspondence defending and attacking Western reputations abroad — bracketing an issue whose centre of gravity is Cold War alignment, the health of Nehru-era Congress leadership, and the unfinished business of India's own liberal and reformist traditions. K. K. Sinha's 'State of the Nation' and A. Subbiah's 'A Question of Language' both diagnose a Congress leadership drifting without Gandhi's foresight, sliding toward the Soviet bloc over Kashmir, and improvising a 'socialistic pattern' without a coherent economic philosophy; Rita Rudra's 'The Nagas in Revolt' turns the same self-critical lens on majority-Indian prejudice toward the Naga hills. G. P. Pradhan and A. K. Bhagwat's long essay on Lokamanya Tilak, and R. P. Masani's review of a Bhoodan bibliography centred on Vinoba Bhave and Jayaprakash Narayan, extend this into intellectual history. Alongside the politics, Quest lives up to its 'arts and ideas' masthead with Sunil Janah's photographic testament on India's tribal and rural poor, an abridged Paris Review-style interview with William Faulkner, a critical study of C. Day Lewis, five poems (including work by the 18-year-old Dom Moraes and by Arun Kolatkar), three theatre/cinema/arts columns, six book reviews, and two correspondence letters (James T. Farrell on anti-Americanism; Marie Seton defending her film criticism). Two substantial items carry no contents-page entry at all: an unsigned notice on international efforts to free Communist-bloc political prisoners, and 'The Poznan Riots: Two Statements,' reproducing an international writers' solidarity declaration (Camus, Jaspers, Koestler, Silone, Spender among the signatories) alongside a parallel statement the sponsoring Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom was then circulating for Indian signatures. The issue's single uncredited-on-the-contents-page art insert is a photographic portrait, 'Chhara youth,' by Chaturbhai P. Patel — a different hand from Janah's — captioned only with an Agfa film credit.

## Essays

### A Photographer's Testament
*By Sunil Janah*

Sunil Janah's photo-essay and accompanying prose 'testament' argues that direct visual evidence — his 'forty thousand photographs' — reveals an Indian rural and tribal population that is unselfconscious about the body, hospitable, and quietly joyful despite poverty, in sharp contrast to the puritanical 'Indian Mentality' that urban reformers, officials and legislators project onto them. He traces the veiling of women and moral squeamishness to upper-caste and colonial-era overlays rather than to any native trait, and criticises welfare officials who ban tribal dances and clothe villagers into drab 'respectability.' The essay is illustrated by his own photographs of Bhil, Muria, Santhal and Saora subjects (rendered on the preceding, unnumbered pages) and closes on the idea that photography also records 'the passing away of ways of life in a country so ready for changes.'

- Frames his 'forty thousand photographs' as visual evidence more trustworthy than words.
- Argues Indian prudishness about the body is a myth invented by 'scribes and politicians,' not a trait of ordinary people.
- Locates the veiling of women historically among upper castes of the Gangetic/Indus valleys under Islamic influence, not as a pan-Indian norm.
- Recounts easy hospitality, communal drinking and unselfconscious nudity among Bhils, Murias, Santhals, Kashmiri peasants and Assamese weavers.
- Criticises officials and welfare workers who ban tribal dances and impose 'respectable' dress, making communities 'as dreary' as the reformers themselves.
- Notes that his photographs also document a fast-vanishing way of life.

### Faulkner Interviewed
*By Jean Stein*

An abridged transcript, syndicated via the Congress for Cultural Freedom, of a Paris-Review-style interview with William Faulkner conducted in New York in early 1956. Faulkner argues the artist's individuality is unimportant next to the work itself ('there is nothing new to be said'), that a first-rate writer needs no economic security ('all he needs is a pencil, and some paper'), and that writing for the movies is an unavoidable compromise that cannot damage a genuinely good writer. He discusses his reading (the Old Testament, Dickens, Conrad, Cervantes, Melville, Shakespeare), names Dilsey from The Sound and the Fury among his favourite characters, and explains how, beginning with Sartoris, he discovered his 'own little postage stamp of native soil' and built the Yoknapatawpha saga by 'sublimating the actual into apocryphal.' He closes by describing the artist's aim as arresting motion — life — so that it moves again for a stranger long after the artist's death.

- The artist is 'of no importance'; only what he creates matters, since all writers work with the same material.
- A first-rate writer needs nothing but 'a pencil, and some paper' — economic patronage corrupts, not liberates, the good writer.
- Moving-picture work is always compromise, but cannot injure a genuinely first-rate writer's own fiction.
- The Sound and the Fury caused him 'the most grief and anguish' of anything he wrote, rewritten five times.
- Beginning with Sartoris he found his fictional 'gold mine,' moving characters through a self-created, non-linear sense of time ('there is no such thing as was — only is').
- The artist's task is to 'arrest motion' so that, like a name scrawled in graffiti, the work outlives the man.

### A Question of Language
*By A. Subbiah*

A. Subbiah argues against the constitutional plan to replace English with Hindi as India's official language, contending that India's multilingual political unity is a fragile British-era legacy that must now be sustained through democratic accommodation of linguistic nationality rather than suppressed. He favours reorganising states on linguistic lines, adopting the mother tongue as the medium of instruction with English as a compulsory second language, and retaining English — not Hindi — as the Union's official language, on the model of linguistic parity in Switzerland, Belgium, Canada and (after initial resistance) Pakistan. He warns that installing Hindi would concentrate administrative power in Hindi-speaking hands and stoke a already-voiced fear of 'Hindi Imperialism.'

- Quotes the anthropologist Kroeber: language is 'the most important' single factor producing the 'consciousness of kind' behind nationhood.
- Argues India's political union is a British-era convenience that democratic accommodation, not compulsion, must now sustain.
- Cites Switzerland's German-speaking 72% majority conceding linguistic parity to its 6% Italian-speaking minority as the model to emulate.
- Notes Ceylon's retreat from linguistic parity is already producing minority secessionist demands, while Pakistan granted Bengali parity after electoral defeat.
- Warns that Hindi as sole official language would concentrate administrative jobs and power in Hindi-speaking hands.
- Reports a Madras conference including C. Rajagopalachari unanimously resolved that English, not Hindi, should remain the Union's official language.

### State of the Nation
*By K. K. Sinha*

K. K. Sinha argues that Nehru-era Congress leadership, unlike Gandhi's, lacks the foresight to guide rather than merely react to events: India is sliding from declared non-alignment toward the Soviet bloc over the Kashmir dispute with Pakistan, and its economy is 'steadily being transformed into a socialistic pattern' by improvisation rather than any considered philosophy, abandoning the earlier ideal of a 'Co-operative Commonwealth.' He warns that continued drift could produce either a 'Hindu revivalist' dictatorship or Communist exploitation of the vacuum, and lays much of the blame on an intelligentsia and press too eager to flatter Nehru rather than think independently. He closes by quoting Minister T. T. Krishnamachari's own admission to the Lok Sabha that India has 'not yet evolved an economic philosophy of our own.'

- Contrasts Gandhi's foresight-led Congress with Nehru's reactive 'mere drifting,' driven by 'world forces' and 'subconscious urges.'
- Argues the invitation to Soviet leaders and their backing of India on Kashmir marks a real, if unacknowledged, shift away from neutrality.
- Diagnoses the turn to socialism and public-sector expansion as improvised policy-making ('We will see as the problem arises'), not a reasoned choice between the 'Co-operative Commonwealth' and Western-style socialism.
- Fears a future 'strong-willed Hindu revivalist' dictator, or Communist provincial secession, if 'nothing new emerges' in Indian political thought.
- Assigns the intelligentsia and the daily press joint responsibility with Nehru for the absence of independent, critical public debate.
- Closes with Krishnamachari's own concession that India's economic thinking is 'ambivalent' and lacks synthesis.

### The Nagas in Revolt
*By Rita Rudra*

Rita Rudra, who visited the Naga Hills in 1953, argues that Naga hostility toward India stems from real Indian prejudice and neglect — not merely, as commonly assumed, foreign instigation. She contrasts Naga community-oriented, gender-freer tribal life (the 'morung' dormitory system, free choice in marriage) with caste-conscious Hindu India, and Naga hospitality with the contempt Nagas experience as 'outcastes' on the Assam plains. She notes that Naga admiration for India curdled once Naga territory was 'grafted on to the Indian Union' without consent after Independence, and that the worst wartime reputation among occupying troops was left not by the Japanese but by 'our own men.' She calls for self-critical re-education on the Indian side rather than scapegoating 'foreign agents,' as the surest path to winning Naga loyalty.

- Opens by quoting the Nagas' own verdict: 'Indians treat us like dogs.'
- Argues political union is not psychological integration; many Nagas were 'barely aware of India even as a geographical entity.'
- Describes wartime Naga loyalty to the British curdling into estrangement after unconsulted incorporation into the Indian Union.
- Details the Naga 'morung' dormitory system and freedom in marriage choice, which visiting Indians often misread as impropriety.
- Attributes the worst wartime misconduct toward Naga women to 'our own men,' not the occupying Japanese army.
- Concludes Indian caste-based contempt and neglect, not external agents, best explain the revolt, and urges winning Naga 'goodwill' through genuine acceptance.

### C. Day Lewis
*By Sadhan Kumar Ghosh*

Sadhan Kumar Ghosh traces C. Day Lewis's poetic development from the near-Communist 'New Signatures' group of the 1930s (alongside Auden, Spender and MacNeice), through wartime reflective verse, to a mature, Yeats- and Hardy-influenced pastoral mode centred on 'the abiding things of life.' He argues that even Day Lewis's most political book, The Magnetic Mountain, represents a personal, quasi-mystical utopia rather than a genuine surrender to Communism, and that Day Lewis's lasting achievement lies in his eventual return to English tradition, childhood and nature rather than in the political 'programmes' of his Thirties poetry.

- Places Day Lewis among the Auden-generation 'New Signatures' poets, shaped by 1930s economic collapse and the fear of fascism.
- Cites W. B. Yeats's 1936 observation that these poets called themselves Communists though their actual verse showed 'no trace of the recognised Communist Philosophy.'
- Traces an influence trail from Whitman and Auden to, from Overtures to Death (1938) onward, Yeats, Hardy and Emily Brontë.
- Reads The Magnetic Mountain as closer to a personal 'Shangri-la' than a Communist manifesto, despite surface Marxist imagery.
- Notes the Spanish Civil War poem Nabara had the same emotional impact on the New Signatures poets as the First World War had on an earlier generation.
- Concludes Day Lewis will be remembered as 'the poet of youth' and of 'returning illusions,' not for political message.

### Lokamanya Tilak
*By A. K. Bhagwat & G. P. Pradhan*

Writing on the centenary of Tilak's birth, G. P. Pradhan and A. K. Bhagwat situate him within the Indian Renaissance begun by Ram Mohan Roy and the subsequent nationalist-revivalist current of Ramakrishna and Vivekananda. They examine Tilak's metaphysics (siding with a mystic 'Metaphysical' stage against Comte's Positivism, drawing on the Gita's idea of 'Lokasangraha'), his defence of caste and the four Varnas as social cement comparable to European trade guilds, and his often-fraught rivalry with the reformer Agarkar, who condemned caste inequality on liberal, rights-based grounds where Tilak defended it pragmatically. The essay closes on Tilak's late, unresolved ambivalence about untouchability: a ringing declaration that he would not recognise a God who tolerated it, followed by his declining, at the last moment, to sign a reform memorandum he had all but agreed to.

- Quotes Aurobindo's contemporary judgement that Tilak's 'principles of work' had already become 'the commonplaces and truisms of our political thoughts.'
- Contrasts Ram Mohan Roy's universalist Vedanta-based Renaissance with the later nationalist, revivalist Vedanta of Vivekananda that shaped Tilak, Lajpat Rai and Bipin Chandra Pal.
- Explains Tilak's ethics: rejecting Comte's Positivism, defending disinterested 'Lokasangraha' (social good) drawn from the Bhagavadgita.
- Recounts Tilak's justification of caste as pragmatic social integration, against Agarkar's liberal, individual-rights-based condemnation of caste inequality.
- Notes Tilak's use of religious revivalism (Ganpati and Shivaji festivals) for mass political mobilisation, judged 'a good servant but a bad master' once the masses were politically awakened.
- Closes on Tilak declaring 'If a God were to tolerate untouchability, I would not recognize him as a God at all,' yet declining at the last moment to sign Vithal Ramji Shinde's anti-untouchability memorandum.

### Lover's Night Thought
*By R. Parthasarathy*

A short lyric comparing a suffering lover, prostrate on the bed, to Christ on the cross, but distinguishing self-inflicted romantic anguish ('he suffered for others, but you for yourself') from redemptive suffering, and closing on the thought that divine mercy exceeds love's cruelty.

- Frames romantic suffering as self-centred against the model of Christ's suffering for others.
- Turns on a single theological contrast between the Lord's mercy and Love's lack of it.

### Waves
*By Nirendranath Chakravarti*

A Bengali lyric, translated by Srinarayan Ray, picturing a barren, miserly place that neither light, waves nor love ever reach, its fields choked with weeds where corn should grow, closing on the refrain that 'this is no place for me.'

- Builds a repeated refrain of absence — no light, no waves, no gold — to figure spiritual aridity.
- Uses a choked cornfield as an image of thwarted abundance.

### Step of Unwisdom
*By Monika Varma*

A lyric meditation on turning forty, imagined as standing on 'the fortieth step of unwisdom' while 'surrogate years' crowd around the speaker amid pastoral imagery of birds, trees and light.

- Turning forty is figured as an 'isle of unwisdom.'
- Natural imagery (birdcall, dogs, horses, drifting leaves) marks the passage of years.

### Dual
*By Arun Kolatkar*

A short poem picturing a man and woman as caged animals watched by 'amused witnesses' (a leopard, camel, deer), their intimacy stripped and bruised under artificial light, ending on the possibility that they might harden into cactus-like defensiveness against the world.

- Uses menagerie imagery (leopard, camel, deer) for onlookers to a couple's exposed intimacy.
- Closes on a cactus metaphor for emotional self-protection.

### Song
*By Dom Moraes*

A fable-like lyric in which a wizard's flute stills dancers, archers and animals in a valley beneath a 'gross sun,' before the scene turns wintry and a fall of snow brings peace and silences the flute.

- Enchantment imagery: a flute's music holding a valley's creatures motionless.
- The poem moves from a squatting summer sun to a hushed winter close.

### Tagore in the Bengali Cinema
*By Ashis Burman*

Ashis Burman surveys Bengali and Hindi film adaptations of Rabindranath Tagore's work since 1932 (Natir Puja, Chira Kumar Sabha, Noukadubi, Chokher Bali, Zalzala and a recent Devaki Bose remake of Chira Kumar Sabha), arguing that nearly all fail because Indian directors merely transplant stage conventions onto film — long dialogue, song, static staging — rather than developing a distinct visual 'filmform' able to carry Tagore's subtlety.

- Praises the amateur-cast 1932 Natir Puja for purity of intent despite technical amateurishness.
- Calls Paul Zils's Hindi Zalzala 'the most vulgarized and atrocious' Tagore film adaptation.
- Diagnoses Indian cinema's core problem as an absence of 'filmform' — reliance on dialogue and song over visual storytelling.
- Finds even the technically improved recent Devaki Bose remake of Chira Kumar Sabha still fails to achieve genuine cinematic quality.

### Letter from Paris
*By Rene Tavernier*

Rene Tavernier's Paris dispatch finds the current boulevard theatre season (Anouilh, Ayme, Roussin) competent but uninspired, praises the Corneille tercentenary revivals and an open-air Cromwell, and singles out Beckett's Waiting for Godot for its 'unquestionable verbal power.' He contrasts the Soviet ballet's technical brilliance but 'reactionary' choreography with Jean Babilee's avant-garde troupe, and highlights three original French documentaries of the year — The World of Silence, The Picasso Mystery and especially Resnais's Night and Fog on the concentration camps, unjustly barred from Cannes.

- Judges the boulevard season 'cleverly contrived, but no better... than those of the late Henri Bernstein.'
- Reads Corneille, via the tercentenary revivals, as an unexpected forerunner of existentialism.
- Calls Soviet ballet's choreography 'the most reactionary in the world' despite its dancers' technical virtuosity.
- Names Resnais's Night and Fog the most striking of the year's documentaries, barred from Cannes for 'diplomatic reasons.'

### Strindberg's The Father
*By Nissim Ezekiel*

Nissim Ezekiel reviews Theatre Unit's Bombay production of Strindberg's The Father, directed by Ebrahim Alkazi: the direction is dramatically strong but the abstract-modernist sets fail; Nergis Cowasji's Laura is singled out as her career-best performance for physical stillness and command, and Bomi Kapadia's Captain for his rare ability to weep onstage without losing audience sympathy. Ezekiel situates the play's paternity-doubt plot within Strindberg's larger, carefully constructed study of a marital power struggle the wife wins, warning against literalist misreadings of the play as mere melodrama.

- Alkazi's sets are 'amateurish cut-outs' the audience learns to ignore; his sparing use of music is praised.
- The play's real subject, per Ezekiel, is a marital power struggle the wife wins by knowing 'the precise weaknesses of her enemy.'
- Nergis Cowasji's Laura is called better than her earlier work in Arms and the Man or Oedipus.
- Bomi Kapadia is praised for making the Captain's onstage weeping and eventual breakdown believable without exaggeration.
- Warns against reducing Strindberg's play, as one local critic did, to mere melodrama about a lamp-throwing husband.

### The Torment of Secrecy by E. Shils
*By K. K. S.*

K.K.S. praises sociologist Edward Shils's study of McCarthyism for avoiding both simple condemnation and simple defence, tracing hyper-patriotism, xenophobia, isolationism, fundamentalism and populism in American political culture, and for proposing a 'pluralistic' model of overlapping, balanced group loyalties (family, profession, party, church, nation) as an antidote to political extremism of right and left alike.

- Notes Shils spent a year in India studying Indian intellectual attitudes, lending him comparative standing.
- Identifies America's constituency- rather than party-oriented politics, plus a populist press culture, as drivers of Congressional 'investigation' excess.
- Presents Shils's definition of pluralism as balanced, simultaneous loyalty to many groups, with none allowed to dominate.

### The Letters of D. H. Lawrence
*By R. Choksi*

R. Choksi reviews the Aldous Huxley-edited Letters of D. H. Lawrence (1910-1930), finding Lawrence a compelling, difficult, self-consumed personality whose letters express a temperament 'in revolt' against fixed society, money, homes and even love — more valuable as testimony to 'an unusual being' than as polished letter-writing, since they lack the 'gift of communication' the reviewer expects of the genre's best examples.

- Quotes Lawrence's credo that blood and flesh are wiser than intellect.
- Finds sympathy fluctuating: moved by the WWI-era letters, but finding Lawrence 'futile and peevish' in his bitter, self-concerned moods.
- Judges the collection valuable mainly for illuminating Lawrence's novels and poems, not as letter-writing in its own right.

### English Thought 1860-1900 by L. E. Elliot-Binns
*By G. C. Chatterji*

G. C. Chatterji gives a mixed review of L. E. Elliot-Binns's study of Victorian theology, welcoming its thesis that theology is shaped by, and shapes, the wider currents of science, historical criticism and politics, but criticising its opening treatment of theological 'development' as scrappy, and regretting that the book stops at 1900, before Christianity's severest twentieth-century trials. He closes by contrasting Christian theodicy — God incarnate sharing human suffering — with the Hindu idea that suffering and evil are mere 'Lila,' finding Elliot-Binns's optimism about religious revival unconvincing.

- Approves the book's central claim that theology and the general current of thought mutually influence each other.
- Faults the treatment of theological 'development' as dodging the real question of whether religious truth is fixed or evolving.
- Lists Froude, Matthew Arnold, Leslie Stephen, John Morley and Henry Sidgwick as Victorian doubters who nonetheless kept outward conformity.
- Regrets the 1900 cutoff, given two World Wars and the Cold War as arguably graver tests of Christian faith.
- Contrasts Christian and Hindu ('Lila') responses to the problem of suffering and evil.

### Phoenix Fled by Attia Hosain
*By Foy Nissen*

Foy Nissen reviews Attia Hosain's short-story collection Phoenix Fled, set among North Indian life, framing the challenge for Indian novelists writing in English as one of 'translation from one discrete tradition into the idiom of another.' Using a comparative-quotation method (a stilted passage from Kamala Markandaya's Nectar in a Sieve against a vivid, vernacular-translated village woman's speech recorded by Verrier Elwin, and an E. M. Forster comic dialogue), he argues good 'Indian English' comes from close attention to idiom rather than literary cliche, and praises Hosain's stories — especially 'The First Party,' 'After the Storm' and the long 'Street of the Moon' — for psychological authenticity and disciplined symbolic control.

- Frames Indian fiction in English as a problem of 'translation' between discrete cultural and linguistic traditions.
- Contrasts a cliche-ridden passage from Markandaya's Nectar in a Sieve with an idiomatically vivid village woman's speech (via Verrier Elwin) to diagnose good and bad 'Indian English.'
- Singles out 'After the Storm,' a Partition story about a character named Bibi, for its controlled use of symbol.
- Concludes Hosain's writing sits 'on the English side of literary tradition as instruments to the validity of her Indian subject-matter.'

### Vinoba and Bhoodan by J. S. Sharma
*By R. P. Masani*

R. P. Masani warmly reviews Jagdish Saran Sharma's descriptive bibliography of the Bhoodan land-gift movement, praising its organisation into biographical studies and 562 annotated agrarian, cultural, economic, political and social entries. He frames Vinoba Bhave as a fourth twentieth-century 'revolutionary' alongside Lenin, Sun Yat-sen and Gandhi, distinguished by spiritualising rather than merely politicising his cause, notes Jayaprakash Narayan's prominence in the literature as Vinoba's chief practical lieutenant, and records the movement's stated goal of fifty million donated acres by 1957.

- Describes Bhoodan/Sampattidan as extending Gandhi's trusteeship ideal to the voluntary dedication of one's talents and spiritual resources, not only land.
- Notes seven eminent Britons publicly appealed for funds to support the movement.
- Reports the bibliography finds few critics doubt Bhoodan's philosophy or feasibility, though fragmentation of land is raised as an objection.
- Frames Jayaprakash Narayan as Vinoba's leading practical collaborator in the movement's literature.

### Poetry London edited by Tambimuttu
*By N. E.*

N.E. welcomes the launch of Poetry London-New York, Tambimuttu's revived transatlantic poetry magazine, noting its first issue's roster (Auden, Barker, Campbell, Cummings, Empson, Graves, Spender) and three translated Indian poems (Amrita Pritam from Punjabi; Buddhadeva Bose and Jibanananda Das from Bengali), and endorsing the editor's stated policy of maintaining a 'balanced view' against literary factionalism.

- Cites Tambimuttu's own account of the old Poetry London surviving rival factions: 'Objective Reporting,' 'The New Apocalypse,' 'The New Romanticism.'
