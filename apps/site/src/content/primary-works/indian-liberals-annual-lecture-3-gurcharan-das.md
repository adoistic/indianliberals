summary: |-
  Gurcharan Das delivers an autobiographical lecture, casting himself as a storyteller (sutradhar) to trace 'the making of a middle-class Indian liberal.' He first defines his terms: a classical liberal wants maximum equal liberty, prefers market outcomes to state control, supports public goods like education and health and a safety net, and places liberty above equality without abandoning either. He argues liberalism is not merely Western, contending the Indian temper has always been liberal, grounded in the Rig Veda's Nasadiya verse, the neti-neti scepticism of the Upanishads, and the Arthashastra's limits on the king.

  Das then recounts a sequence of 'liberal awakenings': witnessing a policeman's murder at Partition in 1947; hearing Nehru speak at Bhakra Dam; encountering American segregation and, through it, Indian caste; a Vicks VapoRub run-in with the Licence Raj that turned him from socialism toward the Swatantra Party; the 1991 reforms under Narasimha Rao and Manmohan Singh, achieved by 'reform by stealth'; and the 2002 Gujarat riots. He criticises English-speaking left liberals for a snobbish, alienating secularism that condemns communal violence but cannot stop it, and Hindu nationalists for trying to build a narrow, European-style identity nation-state, 'a sort of Hindu Pakistan.' He recounts reluctantly endorsing Modi in 2014 for economic reasons and then being disappointed by demonetisation and polarisation. His dilemma: having lost friends on the right over Gujarat and demonetisation and on the left over his 2014 vote, he is 'a man in the middle' with no one to vote for. He closes with the Shiva Nataraja story from the Madras Museum, urging liberals to respect the sacred world of ordinary religious Indians.
