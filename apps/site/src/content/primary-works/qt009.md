# QUEST

## Summary

Quest Vol. II No. 3 (December 1956-January 1957), edited by Nissim Ezekiel for the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, appeared in the middle of the Suez and Hungarian crises, and a large share of its front matter and articles are shaped by that moment: an unlisted dossier on the newly formed Indian Committee for Solidarity with Hungary (Jayaprakash Narayan's press statement, the Committee's own announcement, and messages from the Congress for Cultural Freedom and from Hungarian writers themselves) sits alongside S. Natarajan's report on the Asian Socialist Conference and K. K. Sinha's historical reassessment of Indian Leftism. Other articles range across Devendra Prasad Singh's survey of Chinese pressure on India's neighbours, S. R. Tikekar's indictment of All India Radio, T. Andhyarujina's philosophical defence of a narrowly-bounded Satyagraha under democracy, an unsigned Geneva-sourced argument for cultural exchange with the USSR, and A. Ranganathan's warning against the Second Plan's collectivist drift. A substantial back section carries theatre and travel writing (Rene Tavernier on the Baalbek Festival, Aamir Ali on Cairo, Uma Anand's critical report on Bombay's International Theatre Conference, Asha Bhende on open-air theatres), a Calcutta gossip-and-culture column, four correspondence letters (including James T. Farrell's reply on anti-Americanism), and four book reviews (Amlan Datta on Bertram Wolfe's Six Keys to the Soviet System, Kobita Sarkar on Charles Seltman's Women in Antiquity, Laeeq Futehally on Osamu Dazai's The Setting Sun, and P. C. Chatterji on W. Elton's Aesthetics and Language). Two unlisted visual plates -- stylised paintings by the young Bombay artist Rasik Raval, and photographs of the Baalbek Festival's Julius Caesar and The Infernal Machine -- are tipped into the pagination without a contents entry.

## Essays

### Indian Leftism
*By K. K. SINHA*

K. K. Sinha traces Indian 'Leftism' from its early-twentieth-century roots in Bengali terrorism and constitutional agitation, through Gandhi's mass-participation methods, the Congress Socialist Party, wartime splits between Radicals, Communists and the Forward Bloc, to the post-1947 fragmentation of Socialists, Communists, PSP and Radicals. Sinha argues that by 1956 the Congress government itself has absorbed Leftism's programme -- nationalisation of insurance, state trading, the Second Five Year Plan's socialistic pattern -- so that the historic distinction between 'Leftism' and the ruling party has collapsed to 'minor details'. He concludes that inherited habits of anti-government agitation now survive only as a reflexive, potentially anti-democratic cult of disobedience, divorced from any real programmatic content, and calls on Leftists to shed this obsolete posture before it curdles into a threat to democracy itself.

- Reviews the historical lineage of Indian Leftism: Bengal terrorism and Swadeshi agitation, Gandhian mass politics, the Congress Socialist Party, and wartime splits among Radicals, Communists and the Forward Bloc.
- Argues that post-independence Congress policy (Second Five Year Plan, nationalisation of insurance, state trading) has itself become more 'socialistic' than most self-described Leftists anticipated, eroding the Left/Right distinction.
- Identifies Leftism's residual creed as anti-imperialism, socialism and pro-Soviet sympathy, and notes growing unease among some Leftists about Soviet as well as Western imperialism.
- Warns that Leftism's real danger going forward is not ideological but methodological: a habitual culture of hartals, agitation and 'revolution'-talk that could be exploited by anti-democratic forces.
- Concludes that historical Indian Leftism has lost its raison d'etre and must consciously discard its old reflexes to make way for a 'new and more relevant contest of ideas'.

### The Second Asian Socialist Conference
*By S. NATARAJAN*

S. Natarajan reports impressionistically on the 2nd Congress of the Asian Socialist Conference, held in Bombay in the first week of November 1956, against the backdrop of the Suez and Hungarian crises. He finds the Conference organisationally weak and prone to 'seeing the wood for the trees', passing resolutions on Poland-Hungary, Egypt and West Asia without confronting the deeper crisis of U.N. authority. He singles out Moshe Sharett's surprise denunciation of French and British action in Egypt, and contrasts the varying discipline of national delegations, praising Burma's U Ba Swe. The most substantial passage is Natarajan's account of Jayaprakash Narayan's seminar address calling for a new orientation for Socialist parties, and JP's subsequent leadership of the Bombay-based Indian Committee for Solidarity with Hungary, which Natarajan credits with pushing Nehru toward a firmer public stand on Hungary.

- The 2nd Asian Socialist Conference met in Bombay in November 1956 during the simultaneous Suez and Hungarian crises.
- Natarajan criticises the Conference's organisation and its tendency to pass safe resolutions rather than confront the erosion of U.N. authority by the Great Powers.
- Highlights Israeli delegate Moshe Sharett's unexpected criticism of French and British intervention in Egypt.
- Profiles Jayaprakash Narayan's seminar address urging a 'new orientation' for Socialist parties beyond conventional profit-motive critique.
- Credits JP Narayan's Bombay-based Committee for Solidarity with Hungary with shifting Nehru's public position toward unambiguous condemnation of the Soviet action.

### Cultural Relations with Russia
An unsigned piece (credited only to 'EDITOR', reproducing a statement from the European Cultural Centre, Geneva) argues that despite Molotov's blanket 1955 Geneva rejection of Western cultural-exchange proposals, the free world should keep pressing for cultural contact with the USSR, both because Soviet intellectuals genuinely desire it and because a free society loses nothing by offering completely open exchange. A long essayistic middle section (unattributed, evidently translated from French, given references to 'Racine' and continental idiom) argues that Russia's Soviet interlude is a temporary aberration from a longer European identity stretching back to 988 AD, and that Russia can only fulfil its potential by 'becoming European again'. The piece then sets out concrete, modest proposals -- writers' round tables, scientific teams, touring exhibitions -- deliberately scaled down from the 17 Western proposals Moscow had rejected, and closes with a page-by-page table of the 1955 Western proposals and Soviet replies.

- Rebuts the common view that Molotov's 1955 'Niets' closed the door on East-West cultural exchange, noting several proposals were accepted in principle.
- Contends that Soviet intellectuals (citing Sholokhov and Ehrenburg) genuinely want contact with the West, distinct from the state's propaganda purposes.
- Argues at length that Russia's totalitarian present is a temporary deviation from a centuries-old European identity, and that only a 'European' Russia has a real future.
- Proposes modest, concrete cultural exchanges -- a writers' Round Table, joint scientific seminars, touring exhibitions -- scaled back from the rejected 1955 Western package.
- Closes with a tabulated point-by-point record of the 17 Western cultural-exchange proposals of October 1955 and the Soviet replies of November 1955.

### Failure of Broadcasting in India
*By S. R. TIKEKAR*

S. R. Tikekar delivers a sustained indictment of All India Radio, arguing that after more than 25 years of departmental management the state has proved 'a most unsuitable agency' for broadcasting. He documents low set ownership, a top-heavy central administration, absence of any AIR administration report since 1939, untrained staff, chronic transfers, and a mishandled film-music ban that only increased film-songs' popularity via rival broadcasters. Tikekar criticises AIR's disregard for authors' and performers' copyright compared with the BBC, and its use of Ministry discretionary-grant appointments to keep artistes compliant. He also flags an engineering-staff shortage (84 vacant posts per the 1954 Estimates Committee report) and argues transmitters should be relocated away from vulnerable coastal cities. His remedy is decentralisation -- more regional autonomy for stations -- and a long-overdue independent enquiry commission, of the kind granted to film and the press but never to broadcasting.

- Argues state management has left AIR static and unimaginative despite 26 stations and Rs. 40 million annual budget, with only about a million domestic licences in use.
- Criticises the Ministry's refusal to hold any enquiry into broadcasting, unlike the film industry and the press, and notes no AIR administration report has been published since 1939.
- Describes AIR's mishandled 'ban' on film-music as counter-productive, driving listeners to rival outside broadcasters.
- Faults AIR for exploiting rather than fairly compensating authors, composers and performers, unlike the BBC's fee structure.
- Documents a serious shortage of trained engineering staff (84 vacant posts per the 1954 Lok Sabha Estimates Committee report) and calls for decentralised, regionally autonomous station management plus an independent enquiry commission.

### China and Panchshila
*By DEVENDRA PRASAD SINGH*

Devendra Prasad Singh argues that China pays only lip service to Panchshila's principle of non-interference, citing a survey of Chinese subversion and border pressure across Burma, Malaya, Singapore, Laos, Cambodia, Nepal and India. He details the disputed Sino-Burmese Iselin Line, Chinese troop incursions into Burma's Wa and Kachin states, a surging ethnic-Chinese population and Communist-linked business takeover in Burma, Peking-directed student unrest in Singapore's Chinese schools, Communist gains in Laos and Cambodia, and Chinese aid to Nepal read locally as opening the door to political penetration. He also notes a disputed McMahon Line incident in India and a Chinese five-coloured map claiming Burmese and Indian border territory. Singh concludes that the poverty and fragile democratic institutions of post-colonial Asia make it fertile ground for China's 'over-painted' economic propaganda, and calls for continued Western support (minus lingering imperial postures) alongside Asian democracies' own economic progress to blunt Chinese expansion.

- Argues China observes Panchshila only where it costs nothing, while steadily undermining the sovereignty of Burma, Malaya, Singapore, Laos, Cambodia, Nepal and India.
- Details Chinese Communist troop incursions into Burma's Wa and Kachin states along the disputed Iselin Line, and a fivefold rise in Burma's Chinese population since 1948.
- Describes Peking-directed unrest among Chinese Secondary School students in Singapore and creeping Communist influence in Laos and Cambodia.
- Warns that Chinese aid and a new treaty with Nepal risk opening the country to political penetration, alongside unresolved incidents on the Nepal-Tibet and India-China (McMahon Line) borders.
- Concludes Asian poverty and weak democratic traditions make the region susceptible to China's propagandised economic image, and calls for Western support without residual imperial postures.

### Satyagraha and Democracy
*By T. ANDHYARUJINA*

T. Andhyarujina examines whether Satyagraha remains compatible with a free, democratic India, tracing its theory of conditional obedience through Socrates, Thoreau, J. S. Mill, Blackstone, Duguit and Ernest Barker before returning to Gandhi's own formulation of Satyagraha as truth-force and self-suffering rather than coercion. He argues Satyagraha's post-independence 'metamorphosis into a cult of disobedience and resistance' now threatens the rule of law that India inherited from Britain, since in a functioning democracy the ballot -- not civil disobedience -- is the legitimate remedy for grievance. Quoting the Congress President's own reappraisal restricting Satyagraha to matters touching the state's very foundations, Andhyarujina concludes that in a democracy Satyagraha can only be a theory of the outer limits of political obligation, 'the medicine and not the daily food of the government', reserved for rare, extreme cases rather than routine political tactics.

- Surveys Western theories of justified disobedience (Socrates, Thoreau, J. S. Mill, Blackstone, Duguit, Ernest Barker) as context for evaluating Satyagraha's place in a democracy.
- Distinguishes Satyagraha from these theories by its exclusive emphasis on non-violence, truth and self-suffering rather than inflicting suffering on an opponent.
- Argues Satyagraha's post-independence overuse as a 'cult of disobedience' undermines the rule of law inherited from British administration.
- Cites the Congress President's own restriction of Satyagraha to matters touching the 'very foundations of the State or its Constitution', with the ballot box as the normal democratic remedy.
- Concludes that in a democracy Satyagraha is properly a theory of the limits of political obligation, to be used only in rare, extreme circumstances.

### Presence
*By A. WILBER STEVENS*

A short, imagistic free-verse poem by the American poet A. Wilber Stevens (editor of the literary periodical Interim, and a Fulbright professor in Burma who attended the Bombay International Theatre Conference). 'Presence' strings together disjointed, dream-like images of faces, snow, fire and dread to evoke an anxious, quasi-religious meditation on identity and mortality, closing on the line 'God's ice is my only road.'

- A single-page free-verse poem, not an essay or article.
- Uses recurring, disjointed imagery of 'the face', fire, snow and dread across sixteen lines.
- Closes on a stark religious image ('God's ice is my only road') after invoking Christ and 'nameless saints'.
- Contributed by A. Wilber Stevens, an American Fulbright professor and magazine editor visiting India for the International Theatre Conference.

### Open Air Theatres
*By ASHA BHENDE*

Asha Bhende assesses Bombay's experience with open-air theatres for Marathi drama, focusing on the Mumbai Marathi Sahitya Sangh's ground, the only one then in regular use. She finds open-air staging harmful to singers' voices, unable to sustain the intimacy modern plays require, and beset by avoidable defects: no dimmer, stray street light, noisy surroundings, garish proscenium lettering and advertisement boards, and poor backstage facilities. Given limited funds, she argues Bombay would be better served by a few small, well-equipped closed theatres than by more open-air venues, which have 'proved their complete unsuitability for their primary purpose: the development of drama.'

- The Mumbai Marathi Sahitya Sangh's ground was, at the time of writing, Bombay's only regularly used open-air theatre for Marathi plays.
- Open-air staging is said to damage singers' voices and to dissipate the concentrated atmosphere modern (non-historical) plays require.
- Lists avoidable technical defects: absence of a lighting dimmer, uncontrollable ambient light, noisy surroundings, and intrusive signage around the proscenium.
- Recommends a few small, well-equipped closed theatres over further open-air venues given limited funds.
- A footnote (by the editor) updates the piece with two further open-air theatres since built, at Elphinstone Technical School and Victoria Gardens.

### East And West At Baalbek
*By RENE TAVERNIER*

Rene Tavernier, QUEST's Paris correspondent, offers a lyrical travelogue-cum-review of the 1956 Baalbek International Festival in Lebanon, evoking the ruined Temple of Jupiter and its literary associations (Barres, Lamartine, Loti) before turning to the Festival programme: Leon Barzin and Wilhelm Kempff in the music week, Robert Atkins's oddly moonlit Hamlet, and above all Jean Marchat's troupe performing Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, Cocteau's The Infernal Machine and Racine's rarely staged Mithridates before 15,000 spectators. Two photographic plates of the Julius Caesar and Infernal Machine productions accompany the piece. Tavernier closes by framing the Festival, held as the Suez crisis peaked, as a deliberate assertion of Lebanon's precarious East-West balance and of French cultural presence in the Arab world.

- Describes the ruined Temple of Jupiter at Baalbek and its literary associations with Barres, Lamartine and Pierre Loti.
- Reviews the 1956 Baalbek Festival programme: a music week (Leon Barzin, Wilhelm Kempff), an English theatre week (Robert Atkins's Hamlet), and Jean Marchat's French troupe performing Julius Caesar, Cocteau's Infernal Machine and Racine's Mithridates.
- Notes the productions drew 15,000 spectators from across the Middle East and beyond.
- Frames the Festival, staged as the Suez crisis peaked, as an assertion of Lebanon's fragile balance between Arab and Western/European identity.
- Two photographic plates (unnumbered) reproduce scenes from Julius Caesar and The Infernal Machine.

### Cairo Interlude
*By AAMIR ALI*

Aamir Ali, on the staff of the ILO in Geneva, offers a light, anecdotal tour of the Cairo Museum's lesser-visited manuscript room, treating ancient papyri as a kind of Sunday newspaper: a 3rd-century BC complaint by a bathing-woman scalded by an attendant, a 6th/7th-century letter breaking off an engagement over a suitor's misconduct, a magic love-incantation on a lead tablet, a testy bureaucratic exchange between a Tura quarry officer and Vizier's headquarters over clothing deliveries (which Ali cheekily updates with a 'Five Year Plan' aside), a Ptolemaic tax receipt, and a comic papyrus strip of anthropomorphic cats and mice. Ali's droll point is that ancient Egyptian gossip, bureaucracy, romance and humour read as thoroughly modern.

- A tour of the Cairo Museum's manuscript room, treating ancient papyri as gossip, correspondence and record rather than treasure.
- Recounts a 3rd-century BC complaint by a bathhouse patron scalded by an attendant, and a 6th/7th-century letter breaking off an engagement.
- Describes a magical love-incantation tablet and a Ptolemaic-era tax receipt as evidence that bureaucracy and superstition are not modern inventions.
- Wryly updates an ancient quarry-officer's supply complaint with a jab at the 'work norms of the 50 Year Plan'.
- Closes on a XIXth-Dynasty comic strip of talking cats and mice, joking that Walt Disney was merely four thousand years late.

### A Letter From Calcutta
*By KAUTUHALI*

Writing under the pen-name 'Kautuhali', this Calcutta column mixes gossip and cultural reportage: a satirical exchange with J. B. S. Haldane defending Calcutta's street cattle against traffic complaints; a sharp comment on the communal violence provoked by a single paragraph in K. M. Munshi's Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan publication Religious Leaders, read as politically convenient to Muslim League, RSS and CPI alike ahead of elections; a report of Dr. Nihar Ranjan Roy's Ramakrishna Mission Institute lecture on the Upanishadic concept of Shilpa (art) as harmony with Prakriti, contrasted with later Buddhist/Jain notions of formlessness; a comparative review of Satyajit Ray's Aparajita against Pather Panchali; and coverage of the All-India Bengali Literary Conference at Agra, quoting at length Sri Kumar Bannerjea's address on the disillusioned, Western-inflected 'new style' of young Bengali poets.

- Opens with a satirical exchange with biologist J. B. S. Haldane, who defends Calcutta's street cattle as slowing dangerous traffic.
- Criticises K. M. Munshi's Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan book Religious Leaders for a single paragraph that provoked Muslim violence in North India, seen as politically useful to the Muslim League, RSS/Hindu Mahasabha and CPI alike before elections.
- Reports Dr. Nihar Ranjan Roy's lecture distinguishing the Upanishadic concept of art (Shilpa, harmony with Prakriti) from later, formlessness-oriented Buddhist and Jain aesthetics.
- Reviews Satyajit Ray's Aparajita as technically strong but less affecting as 'a human document' than Pather Panchali.
- Quotes at length Sri Kumar Bannerjea's address to the All-India Bengali Literary Conference at Agra on young Bengali poets' post-war disillusionment and debt to English poetry.

### The International Theatre Conference
*By UMA ANAND*

Uma Anand delivers a sharply critical report on the October-November 1956 International Theatre Conference in Bombay, organised by the Bharatiya Natya Sangh (Theatre Centre) after three years' notice yet marred by last-minute confusion, absent Bombay theatre personnel, and discussions that largely ignored the two prepared papers on 'Theatre for Youth' and 'Theatre for the People'. She is skeptical of proposals to import foreign 'technician-producers' to villages, arguing India's own talent and living rural performance traditions (melas, tamashas) are better placed to revitalise the theatre, and that it is the cities, not the villages, that most need re-vitalising. The entertainment programme, staged in a converted open-air school ground, embarrassingly illustrated the very poverty of Indian theatre infrastructure it aimed to disguise, redeemed chiefly by the Little Ballet Troupe's Panchatantra.

- Reports poor organisation of the October 1956 Bombay International Theatre Conference despite three years of advance planning by the Bharatiya Natya Sangh.
- Criticises reliance on official/civic dignitaries for inaugural speeches and discussions that ignored the two prepared conference papers.
- Disputes Dr. Mulk Raj Anand's and others' calls to import foreign technicians to village theatres, arguing India's own talent and living folk traditions (melas, tamashas) are better suited, and that Indian cities, not villages, most need theatrical revival.
- Notes the conference's showcase entertainment, held in a converted open-air school theatre, itself demonstrated India's poor theatre infrastructure.
- Singles out the Little Ballet Troupe's Panchatantra as the one genuinely successful item, worth an international tour.

### Anti-Americanism
*By JAMES T. FARRELL*

American novelist James T. Farrell replies at length to D. D. Karve's earlier letter on anti-Americanism, conceding that American wealth provokes understandable envy and that Americans sometimes carelessly call other countries 'backward', but insisting American prosperity rests on hard work as much as historical advantage. He challenges Karve's charge that America uncritically backs 'Totalitarian, Imperialistic and Racist' regimes (Spain, Portugal, South Africa), arguing that moral double standards run both ways -- citing colonial peoples' silence on Soviet-annexed Latvia, Estonia and Lithuania while demanding Goa's return. Farrell distinguishes a democratic government's specific policies from 'the entire life of a people', and closes by affirming that, whatever their disagreements, 168,000,000 Americans think and speak freely, unlike populations under 'a secret police'.

- Responds to D. D. Karve's letter, acknowledging that American wealth provokes real envy and that loose talk of other nations as 'backward' causes genuine hurt.
- Argues American prosperity reflects hard work and struggle (citing labour unions) as much as historical advantage, rejecting simple resentment as a fair response.
- Rebuts the charge that the U.S. uniquely supports Totalitarian/Imperialist/Racist regimes, pointing to double standards in colonial critics' silence on Soviet-annexed Latvia, Estonia and Lithuania.
- Distinguishes between criticising specific U.S. government policies and condemning 'the entire life of a people'.
- Closes affirming that free, if disunited, American public opinion is itself a value distinct from any secret-police-enforced unanimity.

### Rewriting of Indian History
*By D. N. DESHPANDE*

D. N. Deshpande, writing from Bombay, rebuts an earlier correspondent (Satindra Singh, Vol. II No. 2) who had claimed India lacks serious historians and historical scholarship. Deshpande cites the Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan's four-volume History and Culture of the Indian People (to 1000 AD) and Prof. Nilakanta Sastri's three-volume History of India as substantial, scholarly rebuttals, and accuses Singh of generalising about 'Indian historians' from only two booklets and a book by 'historian-politicians' rather than engaging serious scholarship.

- Rebuts a prior QUEST correspondent's claim that India has no serious historians or historical scholarship.
- Cites the Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan's four-volume History and Culture of the Indian People and Nilakanta Sastri's three-volume History of India as counter-evidence.
- Accuses the original critic of judging all Indian historiography from just two booklets and one book by 'historian-politicians'.
- Concedes there is always room for improvement but insists 'what has so far been achieved is substantial and scholarly'.

### Recent Trends in Indian Economy
*By A Ranganathan*

A. Ranganathan, writing from Madras, warns that India's Second Five Year Plan is sliding toward Left-Wing collectivism, quoting John Strachey's Encounter essay on 'The Indian Alternative', C. D. Deshmukh's admission that insurance nationalisation was partly 'ideological', and Professor Dantwala's concern that cooperative-sector land policy edges toward the Chinese model of collective farming despite the 'Land to the Tiller' slogan. He also flags deficit financing of roughly Rs. 1,200 crores against a Rs. 4,800 crore outlay as an inflationary driver that hurts the poorest most, and argues the only alternatives are to scale back the Plan's ambitions or nationalise banking outright -- either of which, he insists, must not come at the cost of individual freedom.

- Warns the Second Five Year Plan is drifting toward Left-Wing collectivism, citing John Strachey's Encounter essay 'The Indian Alternative'.
- Cites C. D. Deshmukh's admission that insurance nationalisation was partly driven by 'ideological reasons' rather than pure economic advantage.
- Quotes Professor Dantwala questioning whether cooperative land-management policy is converging with the Chinese collective-farming model despite the 'Land to the Tiller' slogan.
- Flags roughly Rs. 1,200 crores of deficit financing against a Rs. 4,800 crore outlay as an inflationary burden falling hardest on the poor.
- Argues India must choose between scaling back the Plan or nationalising banking outright, but must not sacrifice individual freedom to either path.

### An Indo-Pakistani Literary Miscellany
*By A. L. KHATIB*
