# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 40 (JAN./MAR. 1964 on the contents page, 'winter 1963-64' on the cover) is the Bombay quarterly of the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, edited by Abu Sayeed Ayyub and Amlan Datta with Nirad C. Chaudhuri and Laxmanshastri Joshi as advisory editors, Nissim Ezekiel as Reviews Editor and Mrs Laeeq Futehally as his assistant. Its argumentative centre is the capacity of institutions to absorb change: Punya Sloka Ray on how laymen can check specialists they cannot understand, Noel J. Coulson on how an allegedly immutable shari'a was reformed into a prohibition of polygamy, Jayantanuja Bandyopadhyaya's long definition of a modern socialism that keeps Marx's prognosis and rejects his remedy, B. R. Shenoy's case for a national university built on faculty control rather than administrative authority, and A. P. Barnabas's comparative sociology of planning in Turkey, China, Russia and India. A second cluster is written under the shadow of the 1962 war and the Sino-Soviet split: Basudha Chakravarty arguing that the emergency showed Indian nationhood has not outgrown Hindu nationalism, Tibor Meray demanding a revision of the Soviet verdict on Hungary, and Sudhansu Bimal Mookherji treating Chinese expansion in South-East Asia as a continuity from the Ming period. The arts section carries Hermann Goetz's survey of Indian art since the Mughal twilight (translated from Indo-Asia by David McCutchion) and the issue's one editorial plate, two leaves of Tagore letters in facsimile; then four poems, a Khasi Hills short story, nine signed book reviews, and four correspondence letters in two paired exchanges.

Three things an editor should know. First, the issue is dense with undisclosed relationships: S. P. Aiyar reviews A New Look at Communism, a book edited by Quest's own Reviews Editor Nissim Ezekiel with A. B. Shah, published by Quest's own sponsor, and Shah reviews Barbara Ward two pages later; the reviewers' block identifies Shah as Secretary of the Committee while Aiyar's review calls him its Executive Secretary. Second, the contents page is incomplete and inconsistent: it omits a three-book Shorter Reviews column signed by the Assistant Reviews Editor (pp.108-110) and the 'Our Reviewers' block, and it disagrees with the cover on the issue's date and on six article titles, prints 'The Growth or Modern Socialism' for 'of', 'Herman' for 'Hermann' Goetz, 'Futurables... Conjucture' for 'Futuribles... Conjecture', and 'Bonamali' where the story and the contributors page print 'Bonomali'. Third, two pieces carry language a modern reader will notice: Mookherji calls the Chinese residents of South-East Asia 'Peking's potential fifth-column' and attributes smuggling, gambling dens and spurious manufacture to them as a group, and Goetz writes throughout of 'babu' Gothic and of Indian patrons who understand only the crudest naturalism. Both are the authors' own views, not reported in order to be criticised.

## Essays

### How to Live with Ignorance
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

Punya Sloka Ray begins from the premise that specialisation has made everyone frightfully ignorant, and that the growth of knowledge makes us collectively more knowledgeable and severally less so. Since specialists, consultants and authorities may err, lie, be biassed, lazy or incompetent, and since each of them is also one of us, he argues that no test of authority - self-proclamation, public recognition, or consent - will serve. He proposes a three-pronged strategy: learn faster than ever; build an art by which the ignorant may rule the knowledgeable, a system of checks and balances over the specialists; and learn to deal with situations in which neither knowledge nor ignorance can be given the upper hand.

The long middle section is a catalogue of possible checks on expertise, each with its own failure mode: separating fundamentals from details, results from methods, methods from results, demanding a general logic, asking for concrete hints, setting specialists to police specialists, judging by crude material results, preferring specialists with agreeable non-specialist qualities, peer evaluation, majority opinion among equally ranked experts, and exploiting overlaps in specialisation. None is a panacea. He then turns the argument on intellectuals themselves, who assume that what is good for them - adult, male, born intelligent, trained in evaluating information - is good for everybody, and compares the 'knower-sayer barons' to early nineteenth-century industrialists defending the right to offer anybody any goods or employment. The closing section identifies the third prong as philosophy proper: philosophy exists only where neither the technical neutrality of the specialist nor the inarticulate practicality of the layman is allowed to prevail.

- Knowledge growth makes us jointly more and severally less knowledgeable, so each person becomes more ignorant as the stock of knowledge grows.
- No single technique checks expert authority; different techniques must be used jointly and successively.
- Ray's paradox of research: the most knowledgeable is the one who becomes less knowledgeable fastest, because knowing the limits of a theory is where the next discovery starts.
- Intellectuals are as prone as anyone to universalise their own interests; he cites liberal defence of unsolicited obscene mail as insensitivity to those unlike themselves.
- Philosophy is neither objective nor subjective; it arises in the transition between certain knowledge, trusted authority and personal judgement.

### The Concept of Progress and Islamic Law
*By Noel J. Coulson*

Noel J. Coulson traces the clash between an allegedly immutable religious law and the demands of modern Muslim society, using the Tunisian prohibition of polygamy in 1957 as his test case. He first sets out the classical concept: law as the command of Allah, precedent to and not produced by society, discovered rather than made. He then gives a historical account that cuts against that self-understanding - the early schools of Kufa and Medina built different systems of positive law out of local custom and human reason, differing even on whether an adult woman could contract her own marriage; the appeal to Prophetic hadith by a doctrinaire opposition group forced the 'Establishment' to re-express its own doctrines as hadith; al-Shafi'i synthesised revelation and analogical reasoning; the Hanbalis rejected juristic speculation altogether, which is why their law alone allowed a wife a remedy if her husband broke a stipulation against a second marriage. The doctrine of ijma then closed 'the door of ijtihad'.

Against that background he reads twentieth-century reform as two successive strategies. The first stayed inside taqlid, using the ruler's acknowledged power to select among school doctrines (the Ottoman Law of Family Rights, 1917) and to limit his courts' jurisdiction (the Egyptian legitimacy law of 1929, which used a procedural device to bar claims resting on a gestation period of more than a year without formally denying the mediaeval rule). The second abandoned the pretence of taqlid and claimed the right of fresh ijtihad: Syria in 1953 made financial capacity a condition of polygamy, and Tunisia in 1957 turned the Qur'anic requirement of impartial treatment into an irrebuttable presumption that no husband can satisfy, prohibiting polygamy outright. Coulson compares Morocco (1958), Iraq (1960) and Pakistan's Muslim Family Laws Ordinance (1961), notes that modernism 'often wears an air of opportunism', and concludes that its firmest foundation is the historical evidence that shari'a law itself grew out of local social conditions.

- The traditional Islamic concept of law - divine, comprehensive, immutable - is the obstacle to progress the paper sets out to analyse.
- Modern scholarship shows shari'a law was historically the implementation of Qur'anic precepts inside existing local custom, not a timeless code.
- First-phase reform worked through siyasa: selecting among the four schools' doctrines and restricting court jurisdiction, without formally contradicting classical law.
- Second-phase reform claimed ijtihad outright; Tunisia's 1957 law uses an irrebuttable presumption to abolish polygamy while remaining formally Qur'anic.
- Coulson expects growing diversity rather than uniformity in Islamic legal practice, and a re-orientation of the authority of reported Prophetic precedent.

### The Growth or Modern Socialism
*By Jayantanuja Bandyopadhyaya*

Jayantanuja Bandyopadhyaya writes a long three-part survey. Part I is historical. Pre-Marxian and non-Marxist socialists - Saint-Simon, Fourier, Owen, Louis Blanc, Proudhon, Lassalle - all sought workers' ownership or control as a remedy for the inequity free enterprise produced; crucially, he insists this socialism was not opposed to individualism but an extension of it, since the state was to secure economic equity for the sake of the individual. Marx changed everything: dialectical materialism made socialism 'scientific', internationalism became programmatic, religion was condemned, the labour theory of value supported a political sociology of class struggle, violence was sanctioned, and the remedy became the elimination of a class. But Marx left no blueprint for construction, which the author calls Marxism's chief weakness, since almost anyone seizing power unconstitutionally in the workers' name can claim to be Marxist. The Russian Revolution then miscarried: an undemocratic party dictatorship 'unrestrained by law and based on force', forcible collectivisation, famine, forced labour, regressive taxation and the New Class described by Djilas.

Part II defines modern socialism as owing Marx its prognosis of capitalism while rejecting his remedy on four counts: violence, dictatorship in any form, the prophecy that socialism requires mature capitalism, and the neglect of programme. It then sets out a political philosophy - pluralist sovereignty after Laski, devolution of power upward from primary units, and a twelve-point specification of democratic institutions including elections insulated from money, bureaucracy under popular control, an independent judiciary, cheap and expeditious justice, protection of minorities, an education system free of governmental control, and secularism - and an economics of socialisation (auto-management, sharply distinguished from nationalisation, which he calls 'statization'), decentralisation, and equality within a range he puts at no more than 1:10, citing Israel's 1:3.5. Part III attacks the thesis, associated with Asoka Mehta, that a backward economy has 'compulsions' requiring socialists to suspend their movement and cooperate with a non-socialist ruling party. He answers that economic growth was never socialism's objective, that the Keynesian savings argument fails once Duesenberry's demonstration effect is taken into account, and that the claim that workers are too ignorant to manage industry contradicts the belief that they are fit to elect a government.

- Pre-Marxian socialism is presented as an extension of individualism, not its opposite - the state intervening for the individual's sake, not above him.
- Marxism's strength is its prognosis of capitalism; its weakness is the absence of any blueprint for constructing socialism.
- The Soviet Union is treated as having lost both political freedom and economic equity, and so as anti-Marxist as well as anti-socialist.
- Modern socialism is defined institutionally: plural sovereignty, devolution from below, a twelve-point democratic specification, and socialisation as auto-management rather than state ownership.
- Equality is to be bounded rather than absolute, with an income ratio of at most 1:10, and paid for by progressive direct taxation.
- Against the 'compulsions of a backward economy', he holds that socialism demands growth with equity at every stage: when a country is poor, an equitable distribution of poverty.

### A National University for India
*By B. R. Shenoy*

B. R. Shenoy argues for founding a model national university for India, and derives the case from what he regards as the virtual failure of the existing 54 universities. His diagnosis is that they cannot attract or keep able people, for three reasons: university salaries have fallen further behind comparable employment (a young man in a bank may pass the professor's salary in six or eight years and double it in a dozen), the administrative set-up treats teachers as employees of the Vice-Chancellor's office, and regionalism - driven by regional media of instruction - both repels teachers from outside the region and pushes others out of the profession. He quotes Masefield on the beauty of a university and Laski's dictum that the university teacher is the university, and insists that the real malady is not weak administration but 'the fatal separation of power and accountability': administrators hold authority in academic matters while faculties carry the responsibility for delivering the goods, so decisions of expert selection committees can be overturned by interested majorities in Executive Councils.

His remedy inside existing universities is that all academic decisions - appointments, promotions, disbursement of academic funds, courses, examinations and grading - rest with the faculty concerned, the administrators reduced to routine and general matters; he cites Eric Ashby that a university is a society, not a public service, and that you cannot issue directives for scholarship or devise assembly lines for research. Since reform of existing universities will be slow, he proposes a national university: possibly with two independently managed wings, one affiliating colleges that teach in English across the language regions and one for teaching and research; English as the medium of instruction with Hindi compulsory at the Intermediate level; an Executive Council renamed a Board of Trustees headed by a President whose primary function is to attract endowments; complete autonomy from government and political organisations; recruitment from overseas where Indians of the requisite competence are unavailable, and posts left vacant or downgraded rather than filled indifferently. He warns specifically against building an administrative machine first or leaving the task to a non-academic 'organizer' who may unwittingly build an empire for himself.

- Shenoy's case for a national university rests on a diagnosis of failure in the existing 54 universities, not on an expansion argument.
- Three causes of the flight of talent: the widening salary contrast, bureaucratic administration modelled on the government secretariat, and regionalism.
- The structural fault he names is the separation of power from accountability - authority with administrators, responsibility with faculties.
- His remedy is faculty control of all academic decisions, with administrators confined to general and routine matters and a Board of Trustees raising funds.
- The proposed national university is to be English-medium, wholly autonomous of government and parties, and to grow slowly from one or two faculties rather than from buildings and councils.

### National Planning and Social Change
*By A. P. Barnabas*

A. P. Barnabas offers an ex-post-facto sociology of national planning, drawing observations from Turkey, China, Russia and India. He asks what pre-existing conditions led to planning, how societies were changed by it, and what has been done to stabilise those changes. His answer to the first is a three-stage sequence: disorganisation of society, the emergence of new ideas, and a crisis. Disorganisation was gradual and structural - in India beginning with a central agency's revenue collection dislocating rural society some 150 years before Independence - and everywhere followed the unplanned transition from village to national economy, which broke 'mechanical' solidarity without supplying 'organic' solidarity, left status and role in confusion, and was aggravated by the unconcern of elites and by unintegrated technical innovations. New ideas (westernisation in Turkey, nationalism in India, communism in China and Russia) gathered followings, and the clash with the existing power structure - violent in Russia and China, hardly violent in Turkey, largely non-violent in India - was resolved in favour of the new ideas everywhere.

He then notes that planning began only after long preparation and well after the seizure of power (China 1949/1953; India 1947/1952; Russia 1918/1928), that it required a strong centralised bureaucracy which these societies willingly accepted, and that the plans were everywhere economic in nature with industrialisation as the main emphasis. The social changes he identifies are consequential rather than intended: a shift from ascribed to achieved status with greater social mobility (the hereditary village headman replaced by the elected Pradhan or Sarpanch), a rise in the status of women (the veil in Turkey, property and divorce rights in India), a direct attack on religion in the communist cases and indirect effects in India, the declaration of secular republics, and the displacement of religion and family by legislation as the major means of social control. Stabilisation has been attempted by rewriting history and legends, changing the content rather than the structure of education, new organisations for young and old, legislation, and large capital projects. He notes finally that techniques differed - Chinese planning almost entirely bureaucratic, Indian planning centralised for national schemes but with non-official bodies bringing bureaucracy closer to the people - and dismisses the Chinese communes as unthinkable in a country that believes in the dignity and freedom of the individual.

- A comparative, deliberately ex-post-facto method: no general theory of social change being available, four planning countries are compared after the fact.
- Planning is traced to a sequence of social disorganisation, new ideas and crisis, rather than to economic doctrine.
- The break-up of the self-sufficient village economy left societies without norms - 'mechanical' solidarity broken with no 'organic' solidarity to replace it.
- Planning everywhere required and received a strong centralised bureaucracy, accepted because societies had neither strength nor will to resist.
- Social change under planning was incidental to economic aims: achieved status, mobility, the raised status of women, secularisation, and legislation as the chief instrument of social control.

### Moral Implications in Hemingway's Work
*By S. K. Narain*

S. K. Narain sets out to rebut the charge that Hemingway lacks a moral sense. He begins from Hemingway's own aesthetic creed - the Nobel statement that each book should be a new beginning, and the obligation to put down what the writer sees and feels in the best and simplest way - and argues that the accusations against him (social irresponsibility in the political 1930s, obsession with bull-fighters, gunmen, prostitutes and hard drinkers, an art of 'surfaces and anecdotes', naturalistic determinism) stem from critics' failure to consider that aesthetic, the formative circumstances, and above all the development of his work across three decades. Narain divides that work into a negative phase and an affirmative one. In the early phase - the stories of In Our Time, The Sun Also Rises, A Farewell to Arms, Death in the Afternoon, The Green Hills of Africa - Hemingway records the post-war 'lost generation', writing by suggestion and implication, using nature symbols (rain as disaster) rather than editorial comment; Narain reads Henry's desertion as both protest and an assertion of the moral value of life, and insists that an implicit condemnation of war's senselessness runs through both novels.

The turn comes in the 1930s. To Have and Have Not returns to the American scene and the Depression, and carries Harry Morgan's dying line about a man alone having no chance. For Whom the Bell Tolls, written out of the Spanish commitment, keeps an artistic balance: the idealism of the International Brigade and the cruelty on both sides, Pillar's account of Pablo's massacre making the reader share her horror, characters actuated by mixed motives rather than stereotypes of vice and virtue. The Old Man and the Sea is his most affirmative work, a parable of fighting the good fight in which literal and symbolic levels fuse completely, and Santiago's defeat is a moral victory. Narain ends by quoting the President of the Swedish Academy on the heroic pathos in Hemingway's awareness of life, and concludes that Hemingway works by implication rather than explication and elicits from the discerning reader a response that is essentially moral.

- The charge of nihilism is answered developmentally: the early work's despair gives way to the later work's affirmations, so charges valid of the one do not hold of the whole.
- Hemingway's method is read as moral suggestion - evocative natural symbols, editorial comment scrupulously excluded.
- To Have and Have Not is treated as the transitional book, anticipating the ethic that man cannot stand alone.
- For Whom the Bell Tolls is praised precisely for refusing to gloss over the excesses of the side Hemingway supported.
- The Hemingway code is summarised, via Carlos Baker, as courage, honesty and the determination to live head high in the face of adversity.

### The Image of a Nation
*By Basudha Chakravarty*

Basudha Chakravarty examines what the 1962 national emergency revealed about Indian nationhood. He accepts that the emergency produced national self-awareness - even the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam abandoned Dravidastan on discovering it was impossible without the security and integrity of India - and argues that what had looked like disintegration was in fact 'a frightful fight for integration' by states, classes and communities each claiming a worthy place in an emergent India. He then probes the content of that nationalism through M. N. Roy's idea of a philosophical revolution: freeing the popular mind not only from unconcern with worldly affairs but from authoritarianism and fatalism. He doubts the ascetic self-image is more than 'spiritual salesmanship', quotes Gandhi on wanting to identify himself with everything that lives, and cites Puranic verse on Bharata as the Indian's eternal home while insisting the religious overtone of that integrating factor sets limits to its operation.

The central argument is that nationalism is compatible with reactionary philosophies (it thrived under Nazism and Fascism) and that communism in power appears as ultra-nationalist, stopping short of the philosophical revolution that would challenge its own authority. Quoting Lewis Mumford on nationality as the bond of common purpose and on the man who uproots nationality killing personality, he holds that nations remain units of the world community. Turning back to India, he argues that the emotional appeal of the unity aroused by the Chinese invasion rests largely on the Hindu religious background - the temple bell, the Gita, a tradition of religiosity set against 'the atheistic character of the aggressor' - which he links to the accession of strength to Rightist parties with a religio-communal emphasis and to the stultification of the communists by transgressing nationalism. His conclusion is that religious minorities, particularly Muslims, remain negatively integrated, tolerated on the fringe of a family danger; he traces this to a history in which Kabir's syncretism failed to reach ritualistic religion, Azad's secular orientation was branded a surrender to Gandhian eclecticism, and even Gandhi could not neutralise Jinnah before obscurantists in his own community struck him down. Secular nationalism, he argues, must transcend Hindu nationalism and also neutralise the residual communal nationalism of minorities, while the absence of normal relations with Pakistan keeps reacting on internal integration.

- The emergency is read as evidence of integration-in-conflict rather than of disintegration; the fear of break-up proved unreal.
- M. N. Roy's 'philosophical revolution' is used as the measure of how far Indian nationalism has emancipated the popular mind from authoritarianism and fatalism.
- Nationalism is shown to be philosophically promiscuous: compatible with Nazism, Fascism and, in power, with communism, which he calls ultra-nationalist.
- The unity aroused by the Chinese invasion is argued to rest on a Hindu religious background, which strengthens religio-communal parties and leaves minorities negatively integrated.
- The deficiency of nationhood is traced historically through the failure of medieval religious synthesis, Azad's isolation, and the killing of Gandhi by obscurantists of his own community.
- Secular nationalism must transcend Hindu nationalism and neutralise minority communal nationalism; relations with Pakistan react directly on internal integration.

### The Inevitable Revision
*By M. Tibor Meray*

Tibor Meray writes on the seventh anniversary of the Hungarian revolution, prompted by the Chinese declaration of 5 September, which said that at the critical moment when Hungarian counter-revolutionaries had occupied Budapest the Soviet leaders 'momentarily entertained the idea of capitulating and abandoning socialist Hungary to counter-revolution', and that it was Chinese opposition and 'correct suggestions' which produced the harsh measures. Meray argues that the interest of the document lies less in confirming that the Russians might have let Hungary go - Khrushchev had alluded to Soviet indecision in a 1959 speech at the Ganz-MAVAG works - than in dating the decision. He reconstructs the Soviet hesitation: Mikoyan and Souslov, not the Molotov group, opposed intervention; they fired Gero, inspired the declaration of 30 October admitting the violation of equality between socialist states, assured Imre Nagy of their confidence, guaranteed that no more troops would cross the frontier and accepted a multi-party system, asking only that Hungary's socialist gains be safeguarded and that it not become an anti-Soviet base.

He lists five contributing factors for the reversal - fear of the empire's disintegration, pressure from Molotov's Stalinists, the army's refusal to withdraw 'defeated' from a country of ten million, the Suez affair dividing Western attention, and the American presidential election - and concludes that the Chinese position could have tipped the balance. Since Peking's own communique of 1 November had endorsed the Polish and Hungarian demands and accused the Russians of great-power chauvinism, and was transmitted by Tass on 2 November, the advice to intervene can only have been given on the Friday before the tragic Sunday: 'a disquieting detail, which shows that the Hungarian revolution had every chance of triumphing'. Meray then turns the Chinese claim against the current Soviet line with a series of questions: if the men who demanded the suppression and Nagy's execution have since been declared adventurers and madmen, were they good counsellors then? If the Yugoslavs who sheltered Nagy are now true Communists, were they not right five years ago? And since Khrushchev has praised workers' councils at Rakovica and conceded Yugoslavia's right to stay outside the blocs, how can Nagy's advocacy of the liquidation of power blocs remain a crime? The revision, he concludes, may be painful and postponable but is inevitable, and must be made not by the people of Hungary but by those who suppressed them.

- The Chinese declaration of 5 September 1963 is read as a Communist-source confirmation that Moscow considered abandoning Hungary.
- Mikoyan and Souslov, not the Stalinist Molotov group, are identified as the opponents of intervention, and their promises to Imre Nagy as probably sincere.
- Five converging factors, not one, produced the reversal; Chinese advice could only have been decisive on 2 November, forty-eight hours before the attack.

### Problems of Contemporary Indian Art
*By Herman Goetz*

Hermann Goetz, translated from Indo-Asia, surveys Indian art from the late Mughal twilight to 1963 as a succession of revolutions and reactions. He begins by denying that Indian art was ever rigidly fixed: the mediaeval canon lasted about a thousand years, arose with feudalism and collapsed with it, and India has known more fundamentally different styles than Europe. The princely courts of the British period drew on a traditional art no longer understood, degenerating into empty imitation, while classicism and 'babu' Gothic arrived with the English; he dismisses the Victoria Memorial as having 'a christmas cake effect'. Ravivarman is treated as the pivotal failure - an industrious self-taught painter whose mythological cycles reached every Indian home as oleographs and whose gods remain 'well-fed bourgeois dressed up for the theatre'. The Bengal School's national orientation was itself inspired from Europe and, Goetz argues, remained more dependent on English taste than its painters suspected: Abanindranath on Turner and the Pre-Raphaelites, Goganendranath on cubism, Rabindranath developing an expressionism reminiscent of Redon, Munch and Ensor; Ajanta gave the school its form, and mass-produced national painting its harmonious linear rhythm and its hysterical sentimentality.

The second half treats the present. As classicism degenerated, attention turned to Pallava, Chola and Khajuraho models and then, with independence, to the living present and to genuine folk art - Jamini Roy's Kali-temple models producing pictures of 'enchanting naivety' before his later work turned artificial - and Goetz catalogues dozens of contemporary painters by region and manner, from K. Sreenivasulu in Madras to Almelkar, da Fonseca and Satawalekar in Bombay. Western movements were acceptable because they were revolutionary: Impressionism suited an Indian sense of the world as dream-like illusion, Expressionism the Rajput technique, Surrealism the tantric symbolism he sees revived in Husain. He is caustic about the new rich, who talk about the spirituality of national art but understand only the crudest naturalism, and about a romanticism of peasant life that refuses to see the hardness of rural existence; the younger generation he finds uprooted and pessimistic. Amrita Sher-Gil is his highest valuation - unrecognised in her lifetime, now the treasure of the National Gallery of Modern Art in New Delhi, which he notes was built up by himself in 1953-55. Sculpture has developed less; arts and crafts have been rescued economically but not aesthetically, sinking into tourist souvenirs; architecture has turned functional, with Le Corbusier and Maxwell Fry, Konigsberger and the American embassy as good examples, though most new building still falls back into the sins of 'babu' Gothic.

- Indian art is presented as historically plural and change-driven, against the view that it was fixed by canon.
- The Bengal School's nationalism is read as itself European in inspiration and covertly dependent on English taste.
- Ravivarman is the article's central case of popular success as artistic failure - millions of oleographs, gods like costumed bourgeois.
- Western modernism was adopted in India for its revolutionary character rather than as colonial influence, and was matched to indigenous techniques.
- Amrita Sher-Gil is ranked as one of the greatest painters of modern India; the author discloses that he built the collection in which her work hangs.

### Folksongs of India
*By Lina Mayadas*

Lina Mayadas reviews Hem Barua's Folksongs of India for the Indian Council for Cultural Relations, and turns the author's own preface against the book. If folksongs are 'a natural flowering of the rural landscape', she argues, therein lies the futility of such collections: songs particular to a region, a tribe or a village have no more chance of surviving translation into a language foreign to them than buds picked from a variety of soils, cultures and climates have of blooming in a city warehouse. She illustrates with a Bhojpuri song whose repetitive refrain, depending on rhythm for a feeling of fellowship in community singing, reads monotonous and stilted out of context, and notes that direct metaphor flowing from the sounds of a language is painfully forced in translation - the lover as bumble bee, a girl's hair as a tamarind tree - while exact paraphrase can be merely embarrassing.

Her wider point is about self-consciousness. However rich, robust, tender or philosophical the songs are, the modern reader's attitude to them is self-conscious, as is the author's with his painstaking commentary, and self-consciousness is the ruination of any spontaneous art. She accepts that urban life is making a rude inroad into folk-life and that for most people the spontaneity and participation of group singing are lost, and suggests that a tinge of guilt at growing away from a joyful tradition is what prompts such anthologies; but what is left in them is neither poetry nor song, only a mixture of elementary sociology and nature study, from which a careful reader may at best get an inkling of flora, fauna and local custom. She ends by widening the blame from the author to the institutions: the book is as depressing and unsound as the mentality that awards prizes to city artists painting with folk motifs, or factories 'like Jamini Roy's, specialising in tradition', and the fault lies with the bodies that sponsor sterile and thoughtless transcribing of tradition into textbook.

### Prospects of Democracy in India
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

Punya Sloka Ray's reply, printed under the standing head REPLY, restates Pasricha's thesis - that the transition to democracy in India has been rushed at an unsafe speed - and notes that Pasricha presupposes without discussing that democracy is worth having and that a poor ruling class will end it, sensing the danger from the armed forces and the extreme right. Ray answers the prior question directly: democracy is worth having not because it is respectable but as a sheer necessity for survival and prosperity, since policy decisions need adequate information, information needs free inquiry and free speech, and independence was not won until the masses rose from their traditional apathy and will not be kept if they do not choose to help again. The miserable illiterate peasant who knew only how to keep his place would make neither the best worker nor the best customer; freedom, wealth, knowledge and power are changing from things that decrease if divided into things that multiply when shared.

Against Pasricha's claim of a deteriorating ruling class he offers several possibilities: membership means different things to different people, unfitness comes in kinds, one unfit to talk to Premier Khrushchev may be fit to say who is, the unfit may know it and not press their power, and may be eased into symbolic positions. The right question is not the proportion of inferior men but how useful and economical the ruling class's total performance is - and he compares the pre-independence government of fewer than a couple of thousand men of outstanding integrity and impartiality, which nonetheless placed another country's interests ahead of India's. He adds that a technique for minimising the harm done by not-so-good men is to give them restricted legitimate scope, the proverbial 'join 'em if you can't lick 'em'. He then denies that a single uncrossable barrier now divides rulers from ruled: the real barriers are caste, religion, language, region and vocation, so India's internal cold wars are diffused into many overlapping ones - a healthy way to hold mutual distrust within safe limits, since abolishing conflict rather than civilising it is a weakness in much political thinking.
