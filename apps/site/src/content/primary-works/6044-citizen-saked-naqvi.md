## Summary

In the rendered page, Saeed Naqvi reports from Jaipur on the public reception of Madho Singh, described as a notorious dacoit taking part in a surrender ceremony. The article contrasts the authorities’ interest in the event with the crowds’ fascination: villagers gather around the dak bungalow, attend the ceremony, and treat the surrendering outlaw as a celebrity. A section titled “Sarvodaya Rituals” depicts the ceremony’s highly theatrical atmosphere and the apparent admiration shown toward Singh and his family.

In the rendered page, Naqvi then turns from the spectacle to Singh’s background and conduct. The article sketches his earlier army service, his descent into outlawry after being implicated in a case involving a rape, and his years as a fugitive. It closes by suggesting that the dacoit’s rough justice had acquired a social function for villagers who had learned to live with terror, and that his surrender could therefore be understood as a significant change in their lives as well as his own.

## Key points

- The clipping is a Jaipur report on Madho Singh’s surrender as a prominent dacoit.
- Crowds gather at the dak bungalow and ceremony, treating Singh and other surrendering dacoits as celebrities.
- The “Sarvodaya Rituals” section emphasizes the ceremonial and theatrical aspects of the surrender.
- The article recounts Singh’s earlier army career and subsequent life as a fugitive.
- Naqvi presents village attitudes toward dacoits as shaped by fear, admiration, and dependence on rough informal justice.
- The conclusion suggests that the dacoit’s authority had acquired a social function in the surrounding communities.
