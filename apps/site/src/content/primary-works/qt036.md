# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 36 (cover-dated 'winter 1962-63', contents-dated JAN./MAR. 1963) is the first number of the Bombay quarterly to appear after the Chinese attack of October 1962, and the war reorganises the issue around it. A two-part section headed CURRENT POLITICS / 'Reflections on the Chinese Invasion' carries Sibnarayan Ray on the collapse of pan-Asianism and of the belief that communist states cannot wage war, and co-editor Abu Sayeed Ayyub on the logical trap the invasion has sprung on the Communist Party of India; an unlisted dossier at the back reprints the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom's 'Appeal to the Intellectuals of the World' with signed responses from France, the United Kingdom and Japan. Around that political core the issue keeps its usual range: Naresh Guha on the Yeats-Tagore relationship, Subhash Chandra Mehta on why caste has outlived its constitutional abolition, Sudhansu Bimal Mookherji on the failure of parliamentary democracy across South-East Asia, A. K. Saran on the incompatibility of planning with a democratic conception of welfare, Marie Seton on the erotic sculpture of Konarak, Allen Ginsberg's long ether poem 'Aether' written in Lima in 1960, a short story by Kewlian Sio, and a DISCUSSION pair on group integration and the three-language formula.

## Essays

### Yeats and Rabindranath Tagore
*By Naresh Guha*

Guha reconstructs the arc of W. B. Yeats's relation to Rabindranath Tagore from the first meeting at William Rothenstein's house on 30 June 1912 to Yeats's later coldness, and argues that the standard explanations miss the point. Yeats's initial rapture, Guha shows, was not the enthusiasm of a promoter of an exotic but the recognition of an ally: he thought he had found in Tagore a poet rooted in a living popular tradition, one whose lyrics united sensuous image and spiritual apprehension, and so realised the 'Unity of Being' Yeats had inherited as an ideal from his father and could not find in a post-Renaissance European tradition disabled by what Eliot called the dissociation of sensibility.

The cooling, Guha insists, was intellectual rather than personal. He dismisses the gossip about how much of the English Gitanjali Yeats had rewritten as irrelevant on both sides - Tagore's stature never depended on his English - and locates the real break in Tagore's Sadhana lectures of 1913, in his devotion to Upanisadic abstraction, in his flat contrast of a nature-subduing West with a nature-embracing ancient India, and in the mocking treatment of theosophical ghosts in The Hungry Stones. Yeats, whose interest in Yoga and Tantra had just been fed by Woodroffe's texts and the Harvard Patanjali, came to think abstraction the root of India's downfall and wanted tragedy and conflict where Tagore dissolved the clash of opposites into reconciliation. Guha traces this disagreement into the poems, reading 'The Hero, the Girl, and the Fool' and 'For Anne Gregory' as replies to the heroine of Tagore's Chitra, who repudiates her own borrowed beauty.

- Dates the first Yeats-Tagore meeting to 30 June 1912 at William Rothenstein's home, and the India Society reception to 10 July 1912.
- Argues Yeats admired Tagore because he read him as a 'popular poet' of a living tradition, confirming Yeats's own ideal of the Unity of Being.
- Treats the controversy over Yeats's share in the Gitanjali translations as a false issue on which both detractors and defenders argued badly.
- Locates the rupture in Tagore's Upanisadic abstraction (Sadhana, 1913), his East/West generalisations, and his scepticism about ghosts and the body.
- Reads two Yeats poems as direct rejoinders to the self-repudiating heroine of Tagore's Chitra.
- Notes that the printed article head is 'Yeats and Rabindranath: A Study in Tradition and Modern Poetry', not the contents-page title.

### Persistence of the Caste System
*By Subhash Chandra Mehta*

Mehta asks whether caste is dying and answers that it is not. He begins by conceding that the 1950 Constitution formally destroyed caste's legal basis - Article 15, the ban on untouchability, the right to practise any lawful calling - and that urbanism, industrial organisation, transport, new occupations and the spread of education all cut against commensality rules and hereditary work. But he argues that the same modernisation has strengthened caste as a corporate body: adult franchise turned every numerous caste into a political unit; the weakness of organised peasant and worker interests left no rival loyalties; cheap paper and caste newspapers gave fluid local custom a permanent written form; and panchayati decentralisation carried caste consciousness into the village council. Marriage advertisements placed by foreign-trained professionals are his standing proof that education reorganises the surface of life and leaves its 'more intimate core' untouched.

The second half is a polemic against caste-based preference. Drawing heavily on M. N. Srinivas - for Sanskritization and Westernization, and for the argument that backwardness should be measured by a father's income, education and rural or urban residence rather than by birth - Mehta describes a competition among castes to be listed as Backward, with the Lingayats of Mysore campaigning their way onto the list, and a state where 'everybody belongs to the Backward Class except the Brahmins'. He distinguishes the humanitarian case for the Scheduled Castes and Tribes from what he sees as the political manufacture of Backward Class status, argues that reservation in promotions demoralises services and lowers standards, and closes on Radhakrishnan's line that caste has ceased to be a social evil only to become a political and administrative one.

- Grants that the Constitution abolished caste in law and that industrialism, transport and new occupations erode its restrictive side.
- Argues adult franchise, caste associations (sabhas), caste newspapers and democratic decentralisation have converted caste into an effective political corporation.
- Cites the 1951 Census figure that of 5.13 crores of scheduled castes only 64 lakhs were in traditionally 'untouchable' occupations.
- Uses Srinivas's Sanskritization/Westernization scheme and his income-education-residence test of individual backwardness.
- Attacks the scramble for Backward Class listing, with Mysore and the Lingayats as his central example, and rejects reservation in promotions.
- Transcribes, in order to attack them, Tilak-era and orthodox defences of caste hierarchy, and quotes Ambedkar's description of Hinduism as a gospel proclaiming 'the worship of the superman-the Brahmin'.

### Democracy in South-East Asia
*By Sudhansu Bimal Mookherji*

Mookherji surveys the collapse of parliamentary democracy across South-East Asia and asks what conditions were missing. He dates the region's entry into world politics from Japan's entry into the Second World War, notes that all its states except Thailand became independent within a few years of 1945, and observes that by Ne Win's coup of 2 March 1962 parliamentary democracy survived only in Malaya and the Philippines. Communist insurgency - the Hukbalahaps, the P.K.I., the Red Flag and White Flag Communists, the Malayan rebellion, the Viet Cong - is one part of his explanation, though he thinks the communists 'missed the bus' thanks to nationalist prestige and their own reputation for extra-territorial loyalty.

His substantive argument is about preconditions. Democracy is rule by majority and therefore requires a 'doctrine of obedience' that minority groups based on military power or local independence have refused. It requires popular participation at every administrative level, which the region's authoritarian history never provided, leaving democracy 'a head without a body'. It requires ideas generated outside politics, and the region has no established agency of free political thinking: he walks through the founding dates of Rangoon, Chulalongkorn, Hanoi and the Singapore colleges to show that no South-East Asian university is older than about forty years, and adds that colonial educational policy wanted clerks rather than thinkers - quoting Vivekananda on students taught that their fathers were fools. It requires trained personnel, a disciplined opposition, mass and higher and technical education, and a politically conscious middle class independent of official patronage, which has only begun to form. Quoting K. M. Panikkar, he concludes that these are 'text book democracies' with 'an air of unreality', and that where democratic institutions are merely formal the army becomes a rival centre of power.

- Treats South-East Asia as one region with sharply divergent linguistic, religious and political patterns but convergent post-colonial problems.
- Dates the region's democratic debacle to Ne Win's coup of 2 March 1962, after which only Malaya and the Philippines retained parliamentary forms.
- Argues democracy must 'grow from below, not be imposed from above', so there is no universal pattern of democratic forms.
- Identifies five missing preconditions: obedience to majority decisions, participation at all administrative levels, ideas generated outside politics, qualified personnel, and a disciplined opposition.
- Uses university founding dates (Rangoon 1920, Chulalongkorn 1917, Hanoi 1907, Raffles College 1928) as evidence that no tradition of free political thinking has had time to grow.
- Notes as a first-hand observation that in spring 1949 'more than 90% of Burma had slipped out of the hands of the Nu Government at Rangoon'.
- Names the Muslim League, Jana Sangh, Akali Dal and D.M.K. as 'reactionary communal parties' that nonetheless won seats in India in February 1962 - the author's own characterisation.

### A Note on Planning and Welfare
*By A. K. Saran*

Saran separates planning from welfare and then sets them against each other. Historically, he argues, the two became associated because liberal societies that began from the premise of minimal state interference needed a justification for interventions that had become frequent and unavoidable, and welfare supplied it; logically, however, welfare has been achieved without planning and planning can serve other ends. Taking welfare to mean equality, freedom and justice - 'a projection, in the field of economy and society, of the political ideals of liberal democracy' - and to include non-material goods, he then argues that a planned order must presuppose a set of goals the individual has to accept as given, so that planning requires a holistic concept of welfare while democracy requires an individualistic one. The two concepts do not harmonise.

The longer argument concerns technology. Both Marxists and non-Marxists hold that increasing technological power can be turned to universal welfare - the Marxists on condition of a communist order, the non-Marxists by further technical development, psycho-social science, education for altruism and fear of nuclear war. Saran replies that technological advance is a system of means whose accepted purpose is leisure; that leisure without a supra-human purpose is either boredom or is spent on war and persecution; and that technological power over nature is by the same token power over men, so that 'the moment of its highest success is also the moment of the loss of man's human status'. He denies assuming a fixed or evil human nature, claiming only that the new man will still be man and will have to use his leisure. Faced with the objection that in a fully planned future the very concepts of welfare and exploitation may become obsolete - Alfred Weber's pure power relationship - he concedes he has no answer, and asks whether such a society could still be called a society of man.

- Separates the historical association of planning and welfare from any logical implication between them.
- Defines social welfare in terms of equality, freedom and justice, and as including non-material 'imponderables'.
- Argues planning presupposes given goals and therefore a holistic welfare concept, incompatible with the democratic individualistic one.
- Defines exploitation, in the Marxian tradition, as the concrete expression of alienation and as loss of self-determination.
- Contends that leisure without a supra-human purpose issues in boredom, war or persecution, so that the only safeguard against exploitative technology is an accepted supra-human purpose.
- Finds it 'surprising' that so many thinkers trust future social science, whose ambition is precisely to control human conduct as fully as natural science controls nature.
- Concedes he has no answer to the objection that in a fully planned society exploitation may become a meaningless idea.

### Anguished Awakening
*By Sibnarayan Ray*

Ray welcomes one consequence of the Chinese attack: the destruction of two myths that had governed Asian intellectual life. The first is pan-Asianism, formulated in Okakura's 'Asia is one' of 1903 - a myth built on the real fact of European exploitation but refusing to see that the cultural distances among India, China and the Islamic Middle East are as great as any between them and Western Europe, and that the Asian choice of the twentieth century is not Asian but universal. The second is the Leninist belief that wars belong to capitalism and communism means peace, a thesis made plausible by the Soviet Union's early preoccupation with internal struggles and propped up afterwards by Khrushchev's co-existence theory. Indian neutralism under Nehru, he writes, was never quite neutral, and against every evidence the government held that Mao's China could not be aggressive because it was both Asian and communist; Korea was glossed over and Tibet condoned.

The second section argues that the deepest cause of Chinese hostility is India's commitment to democracy, which presents Asia with an alternative model that a totalitarian regime cannot tolerate; the invasion has been prepared for nearly a decade, and its objects are to humiliate India, keep her permanently vulnerable, wreck the Third Five Year Plan and create conditions for a communist coup. The third turns to what India must now do: appeal to the United Nations and then directly to any nation that will help; go more than half way to win Pakistan, if necessary conceding an internationally supervised plebiscite in Kashmir once Chinese forces have withdrawn; resist the wartime drift to regimentation and militant chauvinism; refuse the CPI the benefit of the doubt despite its pro-government resolution; and build consumers' cooperatives and citizens' vigilance committees against hoarding and black-marketeering. The piece is dated 9 November 1962, with the front still giving way.

- Names two myths destroyed by the invasion: Okakura's 'Asia is one' (1903) and the Leninist identification of war with capitalism.
- Holds Krishna Menon primarily to blame but insists the political intelligentsia, including 'many liberal intellectuals', share responsibility.
- Argues India's democratic choice, not territory, is what Red China is attacking, and lists four Chinese objectives including subversion of the Third Five Year Plan.
- Proposes conceding a plebiscite in Kashmir under international supervision, conditional on prior Chinese withdrawal from Indian territory.
- Warns that war breeds chauvinism and that militant nationalists may displace liberals and democratic socialists in the country's leadership.
- Notes India has three times held free and fair elections on universal adult suffrage and that 'the independence and integrity of the Indian judiciary is above reproach'.
- Datelined 'November 9, 1962', while 'one post after another has fallen to the Chinese invaders'.

### The Silver Lining
*By Abu Sayeed Ayyub*

Ayyub takes the Communist Party of India's National Council resolution of 1 November 1962, which condemned Chinese aggression and backed Nehru's appeal for unity, and turns it into a dilemma. A party member had called the resolution proof that the CPI 'had come of age' because it had judged a grave issue on its own reading of the situation; Ayyub congratulates the coming of age while noting the admission it contains, that for years the party had let people thousands of miles away in Moscow, Peking or London decide for it. His logical point is sharper: the resolution, taken with the axiom that no socialist state can commit aggression, forces the CPI to choose between holding that the Chinese party has ceased to be communist and holding that communism is as compatible with military aggression as capitalism or fascism - and the Dange majority, he shows from the New Age of 11 November and Hiren Mukherjee's Lok Sabha speech, has been asserting all three incompatible propositions at once while the pro-Peking minority stays silent.

He dissents from Nehru's claim that the issue is expansionism rather than communism: China does not want a governor-general in Delhi but the acceptance of Chinese communism and tutelage across south and south-east Asia, and NEFA and Ladakh are a foothold, not a prize. Turning to the CPI's talk of a 'madness' seizing the Chinese leaders, he recalls Khrushchev's 1956 account of Stalin and asks, with heavy irony, whether absolute power in a communist party produces mental derangement rather than mere corruption. He then attacks neutrality as distinct from military non-alignment: India spoke out for Egypt and would speak for Cuba but faltered over Hungary and condoned Tibet, and now finds Indonesia, Burma, Ghana and Ceylon silent. He contrasts the United Kingdom's and the United States' immediate help with the Soviet Union's calling Indians friends and the Chinese brothers, notes Nehru's 1954 envy of Mao's Long March, and argues that the Chinese have supplied both at once. The piece is dated 15 November 1962 and adds in a footnote that Nehru 'symbolises for us the democratic spirit and secular temper of life'.

- Builds a formal dilemma from the CPI resolution: either the Chinese party has stopped being communist, or communism is compatible with aggression.
- Shows the Dange group asserting three mutually inconsistent propositions and quotes New Age of 11 November 1962 against it.
- Rejects Nehru's Lok Sabha framing of the war as expansionism rather than communism versus democracy.
- Reads the CPI's talk of Chinese 'madness' against Khrushchev's 1956 account of Stalin, and suggests absolute power in a party produces derangement rather than mere corruption.
- Distinguishes military non-alignment, which he endorses, from political and ethical neutrality, which he calls immoral in a clear case of aggression.
- Contrasts British and American speed in answering India's appeal with Soviet support for Chinese territorial claims, citing a proscribed Soviet atlas in a footnote.
- Written by one of the issue's two named editors, and advertised on the cover under a different title, 'CPI's Dilemma and Our Duty'.
- Printed p.51 carries a typesetting error in the sentence before the pull quote: 'the cause of the Communist govern- ment fo China' for 'of China'.

### Konarak
*By Marie Seton*

Seton's essay on the thirteenth-century Sun Temple is an argument about the difference between the erotic and the pornographic. She begins from her own 'ingrained aversion to the pornographic', which she defines by lewdness, suggestiveness and decadence, and reports that the Konarak sculptures struck her as the furthest thing from it - unlike Khajuraho, which she knows only from photographs and finds poised between passion and decadence. Long digressions on Lady Chatterley's Lover, which she first read at eighteen as a 'naughty' book and later judged the only novel true to sexual experience, and on Lolita, which she calls the most morbid book she has read because both characters abhor their own sexuality, set up her central contrast: Western erotic art, including the Greek and Roman, is dominated by conquest, rape and the dirty joke, while Hindu myth - the Krishna Lila, the character of Ravana, Sita - turns on mutual passion and consent. 'Hardly anyone enjoys themselves' in Western pornographic art; at Konarak it is the mutuality of enjoyment that makes the impact.

The second and third sections read the building. The figures of Surya on his horses stand at the apex; the Natmandir is 'a pillared delight'; the Jagamohan records the evolution of human love from its most bestial aspect to its most sublime, with the scale of the figures increasing and the emotion refining as the motif of union rises towards the parapet. She notes, and refuses to be embarrassed by, the small inconspicuous plaque at the base depicting bestiality, and observes that not one scene in the whole temple suggests rape. She speculates, while admitting there is not a shred of evidence, that Narasimha Deva built the temple not in triumph over the Muslim invaders he had defeated but in honour of life against the horrors of war, recalling Asoka in the same land of Kalinga. The essay closes on the transformation worked by dawn and sunset, when shadows bring the figures alive and the free-standing women appear to step forward from the wall, and on an unphotographed frieze under a tree that she says she will remember for the rest of her life.

- Defines pornography by lewdness, suggestiveness and decadence, and argues Konarak is its opposite because nothing there is done against anyone's will.
- Contrasts the conquest and rape motifs of Western and classical erotic art with the mutuality and consent she finds in Hindu myth.
- Uses Lady Chatterley's Lover and Lolita as test cases, praising the first and calling the second the most morbid book she has read.
- Reads the Jagamohan's rising motif as a progression from bestial to spiritualised love under the jurisdiction of the Sun God.
- Records the small basal plaque depicting bestiality and argues that its inconspicuousness is exactly what keeps the temple from being pornographic.
- Speculates that Narasimha Deva (1238-1264) may have built the temple in honour of life rather than in triumph over defeated invaders.
- Accompanied by an unlisted two-page plate of Satyajit Ray's sketches of Konarak sculpture, tipped in between printed pp.56 and 57; Seton's contributor note records that she had just completed a book on Ray.

### Aether
*By Allen Ginsberg*

'Aether' is a twelve-page poem written under ether in Lima and datelined 'Hotel, Commercio / Lima, Peru / May 28, 1960'. An unsigned editorial headnote on printed p.61 explains that Ginsberg is then in India 'experimenting on the hallucination-inducing properties of certain indigenous drugs' but that this poem was written under a different stimulant and in different circumstances, that it has been 'somewhat condensed and reset for QUEST', and that Ginsberg has spent longer in Calcutta than in any city but his native New York and San Francisco because he finds much in common with the Bengali poets of the 'Hungry Generation'; the note reads the Beat interest in drugs as the belief that if poetry fails to deliver a supersensory revelation, narcotics may.

The poem itself is a timed log of consciousness dissolving and returning, marked by clock hours ('11.15 p.m. May 27', 'May 28 - 7:30pm', '9 PM') and punctuated by recurring external noises - trolleycars, church bells at midnight, and a pistol shot outside the window that becomes the poem's proof that experience repeats. Its recurrent claim is that this consciousness is one accident among 'Ether-possible worlds', that every insight has been had before and lost, and that the universe is 'an OLD mistake'. Ginsberg salutes Kerouac, Gregory Corso, Peter Orlovsky and Philip Whalen by name, addresses the 'GREAT CREATOR' in capitals asking permission to publish his name, and attacks the concept of a single God as arbitrary madness, rejecting the Hebrew Adonoi Echad along with Christ and Buddha. Peruvian and Andean material runs through it - Nasca, Paracas, Chancay, the millipede on the staircase at Macchu Picchu, the Mochica potter, the Plaza de Armas and the Gran Hotel Bolivar - and the poem ends in the street at midnight, the poet turning thirty-four, alone among friends in a Chinese restaurant.

- Written in Lima under ether on 27-28 May 1960 and datelined from the Hotel Commercio; condensed and reset for this issue of Quest.
- Prefaced by an unsigned editorial headnote linking the Beat poets and the Calcutta 'Hungry Generation' as sharing a craving for an experience art can hardly give.
- Structured as a timed log of repeated dissolutions of consciousness, keyed to clock chimes and a recurring pistol shot outside the window.
- Argues that consciousness is an accident of one among many 'Ether-possible worlds' and that all knowledge is recollected rather than new.
- Rejects monotheism explicitly - 'What is One but Formation of mind?' - and names Kerouac, Whalen, Gregory and Peter within the text.
- Saturated with Peruvian material: Nasca, Paracas, Chancay, Macchu Picchu, the Mochica potter, the Plaza de Armas.

### Laurie Lewis
*By Kewlian Sio*

A first-person story set in wartime Calcutta, narrated in retrospect by a man recalling the summer holidays in which he turned thirteen. He and his cousin, teaching themselves to ride a hired bicycle in a back lane, are jeered at and then coolly taught by Laurie Lewis, an Anglo-Burmese boy who has fled Rangoon with his brother Edgar ahead of the Japanese and is living with an aunt, Mrs Robinson. Laurie is a natural teacher, patronising in an elusive way, and a compulsive fabulist: he produces a Burmese ruby that is a magic ring, a monk with shining eyes and long nails, a glowing warning that saved a whole party from a Japanese scout in a tree, and finally a bamboo movie projector from Rangoon that costs twenty rupees and takes two-rupee colour reels of Superman and Captain Marvel. Between the stories he plays a 'Burmese tune' on the piano using only the black keys, cadges gum and Hershey's chocolate off two GIs in a cinema lounge, and keeps the chocolate for his mother.

The narrator, whose birthday present is a magic lantern, hands over two rupees as an advance on the projector; Leong, the third boy, is pressed for an advance too and his widowed mother gives five rupees 'just to end it all'. The suspicion the narrator has been suppressing surfaces at night as rage - 'He's a stupid liar! And a cheat!' - and when he goes to the Lewises' rooms he finds patchy walls, a few sticks of furniture, a sleeping brother, and a mother who does not know the time because the servant-boy stole her alarm clock and who wishes for a small one. Laurie comes once more, sweating, to ask for a hundred rupees for school fees, is refused, drinks a glass of water, says 'That was a grand day' of the birthday, and goes down the stairs muttering. The Lewises leave for Delhi; the narrator forgets them; the story ends with him awake as an adult, seeing the boy on the stairs again.

- Set in wartime Calcutta among schoolboys, with refugee arrivals, GIs on Chowringhee and Betty Grable musicals as the texture of the period.

### Overintegration and Underintegration in Indian Society
*By Satish C. Saberwal*

Saberwal offers a conceptual vocabulary for the debate on 'national integration' that earlier Quest contributors had joined: overintegration, underintegration, group separation and group overlap. An overintegrated unit commands strong, emotionally laden allegiance and, in return for meeting its members' multiple needs, claims unquestioning loyalty; overintegration, he suggests, is like overlearning, necessary for reliable performance but capable of exceeding the optimum for a group's goals. His examples are pointedly mixed - a family, an orchestra, an army platoon, but also the pre-revolutionary Bolsheviks, the Nazi party of the twenties and the R.S.S. of the mid-forties - and he observes drily that whether one likes a group's overintegration depends partly on whether one is inside it.

From his own residence in a village of about 750 people in a dozen castes less than twenty miles from Delhi between April 1958 and August 1959, he describes caste overintegration: residential segregation of sweepers, leather-workers and potters; hookah groups drawn from single castes or near-neighbours in the hierarchy; and Jats sitting together in a chartered bus by 'a deeply ingrained unconscious tendency'. In the city, caste integration weakens while the family persists, held together by intra-caste marriage; he warns that abolishing arranged marriage before alternative courtship mechanisms exist 'would be an act of folly', and calls for research on the several thousand intercaste couples already married. His constructive proposal is that overlapping membership in many small groups - adult education, civic bodies, music, religion - both meets the urban need for belongingness and multiplies the 'go-betweens' or brokers who can resolve conflicts, so that national integration can be redefined as the problem of increasing group overlap.

- Argues against dismantling arranged marriage before substitute courtship mechanisms exist, and calls for research on intercaste couples.
- Claims coinciding group boundaries scarcify brokers, so conflict resolution depends on overlapping memberships.
- Notes that the increasing political salience of caste indicates a 'ruralization of Indian politics'.

### The Three Language Formula
*By Kusum Chandra*

Chandra argues that the language debate is over and what is needed is an implementation timetable. She notes that the Constituent Assembly's 1965 deadline for the changeover from English to Hindi has effectively lapsed, that English is now to continue indefinitely as an 'associate' official language, and that with no time limit things will proceed at a leisurely pace; Hindi, once a symbol of national unity, has been reduced to one of fourteen contending forces. ### Tilak and Gokhale
*By P. Kodanda Rao*

Headlined 'Reform and Revolution', Kodanda Rao calls Stanley Wolpert's Tilak and Gokhale perhaps the best comparison of the two men's contributions to the making of modern India, and reads it as vindicating Gokhale. ### English, Sanskrit and the Regional Languages
*By N. K. Devaraja*

Devaraja answers Ray, welcoming the comments as a chance to elucidate points made in 'an all too brief article on a vast subject'. He shares Ray's regret about the traditional pandit but defends his extreme proposal: the pandit educated on purely traditional lines plays an essentially anti-democratic and anti-progressive part, lacks the sense of philosophic wonder, is insensitive to the complexity of problems and cannot stimulate new thinking; if he still seems useful it is only because of the vitality of the thought enshrined in Sanskrit, whose literal knowledge he transmits and whose vitality he himself largely misses. His constructive version is that the traditional pandit should not be allowed young pupils of his own but should be attached to modern institutions of learning and research, while graduate and post-graduate students are offered special inducements to take diplomas and degrees in ancient texts and gradually replace him as teachers of those texts.

On the regional languages he disagrees flatly. They are, he says, precisely the media where the interaction and amalgamation of India's different traditions is taking place. He describes the process concretely: U.P. graduates and MAs in Hindi staff the schools and colleges, and if they keep up with current Hindi literature through magazines they are bound to be affected by modern thought; writers in a modern Indian language may know the Sanskrit tradition well but are not tradition-bound like the pandit, and the more intelligent among them are eager to display acquaintance with the latest Western thought - a tendency no less prevalent among those who write in English. There is no sharp line between regional-language teachers and other teachers, who share the same urban culture, the same party propaganda and the same dailies and weeklies; and with the regional language installed as the medium of instruction at B.A. level in several state universities, students at Lucknow, Allahabad or Banaras Hindu University express themselves more easily in it than in English. Hence it is unrealistic to say the regional language 'cannot win India any more': any far-reaching change in the Indian mind can be effected only through it. He adds a ranking - Dayanand, Tilak, Gandhi and Tagore have influenced Indians more than Vivekananda, Aurobindo and Radhakrishnan - and concludes that professors who wrote exclusively in English on British writers failed twice over, winning no recognition in England and contributing nothing to their own literatures.

- Defends the anti-pandit proposal on the ground that traditional training produces neither philosophic wonder nor the capacity to stimulate new thinking.
- Softens it into a scheme: attach pandits to modern institutions, induce graduates to take degrees in ancient texts, and let them replace the pandits as teachers.
- Reverses Ray's diagnosis: the regional languages are where India's traditions actually interact and amalgamate.
- Describes the mechanism through U.P. Hindi teachers, magazines and the shared urban culture of all teachers.
- Notes that students at Lucknow, Allahabad and Banaras Hindu University now express themselves more easily in the regional language than in English.
- Ranks influence: Dayanand, Tilak, Gandhi and Tagore above Vivekananda, Aurobindo and Radhakrishnan, on the ground that the first group wrote in the regional languages too.
- Judges that critics writing exclusively in English on British writers 'have been failures in a double sense'.
