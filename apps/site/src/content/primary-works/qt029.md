# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 29 (cover 'spring 1961', contents page 'APRIL/JUNE 1961') is largely a single-subject number without saying so on its cover: five of its six articles, plus its reportage section, come out of a three-day seminar on 'The Place of Science and Humanities in Higher Education' held in Bombay on 26-28 December 1960 under the joint auspices of Quest and the University of Bombay. Michael Polanyi, who presided, opens with 'The Study of Man', an attack on what he calls scientific obscurantism — the Laplacean ideal of a complete atomic topography of the universe, which he argues would 'impose complete ignorance on us' — and a case for personal, skill-based knowing as the bridge between the exact sciences and the study of man. Nirmal Kumar Bose's inaugural address answers the same question from svadharma: leave scientist and artist each free to push his own path to its extreme. Co-editor Abu Sayeed Ayyub, in the issue's longest piece, takes the opposite view from Polanyi, insisting the dichotomy between scientific and humanistic knowledge is 'inescapable, and must be frankly recognised', and ends with a deliberately 'absurd' proposal that arts courses be abolished and all university education be science education with a compulsory humanities pass paper. David McCutchion's seminar report then shows the argument as it actually ran, with Sibnarayan Ray, A. B. Shah, Hans Simons, N. K. Devaraja and others defending the scientific method against Polanyi's pessimism about it.

Around that core the issue sets Manes Sperber's Congress for Cultural Freedom paper on tradition and 'mass-man'; Herbert Tingsten's comparative survey showing that Swedish, German and French writers on national character all reach identically flattering conclusions about their own peoples; and E. De Meulder's Jesuit case for 're-tribalizing' India rather than detribalizing its adivasis — printed with an unusual editorial disclaimer inviting readers to react. There are three poems, two short stories (Raja Proctor's Ceylonese fishing-village tale and Gopi Gauba's marital two-hander), D. K. Bedekar's review article recovering Gopal Hari Deshmukh as a pioneer of the Maharashtra renaissance, Sadhan Kumar Ghosh's books column, twelve book reviews, and three letters — two of them continuing Quest 28's quarrel over Indians writing in English, with P. Lal answering Jyotirmoy Datta at length in the Discussion section. The recurring argumentative centre is authority and who gets to certify knowledge: Polanyi against positivism, Ayyub against I. A. Richards, Bedekar's Deshmukh against the Sanskrit pandits, De Meulder against the census officer and the anthropologist, and Lal against the claim that a language must be one's mother tongue to be one's own. The issue carries no artistic plates or photographs at all: every non-text page is a paid advertisement.

## Essays

### Tradition and Mass Culture
*By Manes Sperber*

Sperber traces tradition through three stages of the human experience of time. In an illiterate, magical society memory belongs to old men, time does not appear to move, and tradition is invisible because it is simply the perpetual present; with written records comes historical consciousness, and each generation discovers it is not only heir to traditions but their creator; with the Renaissance and secularisation comes what he calls time-space, in which the present is lived as a moving threshold of the future. Mass culture, he argues, is what happens when human beings are cast out of time-space and forced back into one-dimensional time.

He then sketches 'mass-man' (MM) in five numbered traits: he feels deprived unless he owns each new commodity at once; he is attracted only by the newest, so that his 1960 dream is a 1961 television set; he measures his own happiness quantitatively against other people's means and so is permanently dissatisfied; unlike the parvenu he does not want to inherit the past but to monopolise the future; and he wants to own exactly what everyone else owns while being utterly unlike everyone else. Sperber's conclusion is unexpectedly hopeful and is aimed at the critics rather than the phenomenon: the reproaches directed at mass culture are aimed at the wrong target, which should be the age itself, and the phrase may prove to describe a transitional bridge rather than a condition.

- Tradition becomes visible as tradition only when its authority is shaken or when social and moral continuity is threatened; a ruling caste proclaims the sanctity of the tradition it says it was chosen to protect.
- Three successive regimes of time — motionless time in magical societies, forward-moving time with written records, and 'time-space' after the Renaissance — set up the argument.
- Marxists of fifty years ago saw the masses as a creative historical factor; Sperber says the great conglomerations are now only a 'masse de manoeuvre', an organisation-object acting like a military unit.
- 'Mass-man' is defined by his position in the rank of consumers rather than by his role as producer, and is psychologically conditioned accordingly.
- The critique is redirected: mass culture is the symptom, the age is the target, and the individual 'in the deepest recesses of his being remains unchanged'.

### Science and Humanities
*By Nirmal Kumar Bose*

Bose's inaugural address to the Bombay seminar starts from the Vedic view that all forms of knowledge, including Ayur-veda and Dhanur-veda, lead towards the realization of Brahman, and contrasts that with the modern cleavage between the sciences and the humanities. He characterises the difference in method — the scientist plodding from hypothesis to hypothesis towards generalization, the artist reaching the heart of things by a condensed, intuitive route he cannot widen into a road — but argues that on closer inspection the gulf narrows considerably.

His substantive claim is that the two mechanistic remedies on offer both fail: giving scientists a patchy acquaintance with the classics, and giving artists a ballast of scientific knowledge, produce second-hand knowledge that stays 'separate and unusable in one's intellectual storehouse' and fails when a crisis threatens life. Against this he offers a specifically Indian proposal drawn from the Hindu tradition and from Gandhi: since all views of truth are partial, each man should live by his own light and persist in his own dharma rather than imitate another's, and where opposition in action becomes necessary it should be informed by non-violence — taking suffering on oneself rather than inflicting it. Add to this the Vedic injunction charaiveti, move onwards, never resting satisfied with one's partial view, and the bridge between scientist and humanist becomes a bridge between spirits rather than a curricular device. He ends with the image of rivers that need not take each other's course but must each flow on to the same ocean.

- Opens from the Indian past, where secular knowledge such as medicine and warfare counted among the highest forms of knowledge because all knowledge led to Brahman.
- Rejects both curricular remedies — classics for scientists, science for artists — as second-hand knowledge that never integrates into a life.
- Grounds mutual respect in the doctrine that all human views of truth are partial, so conflict in the sphere of thinking is unnecessary.
- Invokes Gandhi twice: that every man should live according to his own light, and the anecdote of Gandhi answering the claim that a community must live with the single question 'Why?'
- Proposes that both scientist and humanist keep questioning — the scientist adding wonder and humility, the humanist refusing to treat his world-view as finally settled.

### Essay 3
*By Michael Polanyi*

Polanyi's presidential address — printed as 'The Study of Man', though the contents page files it under the group heading 'Science and Humanities' — argues that science has replaced religious dogma as the greatest single source of popular fallacies, not because science is bad but because its professed ideals are nonsensical. The Laplacean ideal of representing all knowledge as an exact topography of atoms in motion would, he says, answer none of the questions anyone actually wants answered: a list of atomic positions on 1 May 1961 tells you nothing about whether your primroses will blossom. He gathers examples of the resulting absurdities — three authorities at a 1954 symposium denying that consciousness exists; anthropologists describing witch-killing as a cultural achievement and head-hunting as functional; positivism discrediting all ethical statements as unverifiable — and links the corrosion to Spengler's and Simone de Beauvoir's moral inversions, arguing that scepticism drives moral sentiments underground where they return as a creed of salvation by violence.

His positive proposal is personal knowledge. No map can read itself; even the exact sciences depend on a skill of identifying numbers with observed events at both ends of a computation, and Newton's laws can never foretell an actual instrument reading. From the hammer whose handle he is subsidiarily aware of while focally attending to the nail, through the blind man's stick and the scientific textbook used as a tool rather than scrutinised as an object, he builds an account of knowing by indwelling, in which tools and theories become part of the body. Geometrical crystallography supplies a case of an exact science that supplies a framework of appraisal rather than predictions. If personal participation is the universal principle of knowing, he concludes, then evaluating human actions morally is a legitimate extension of scientific knowledge, and a code of laws is no longer unscientific merely because it predicts nothing.

- The charge is specific: not that science is harmful but that 'the ideals of science are nonsensical', and that the Laplacean ideal would leave us knowing nothing we care about.
- Collects contemporary denials of consciousness and value-free anthropology as the concrete costs of scientific detachment, with the aphorism that the stability of evil is the worst of evil.
- Argues every exact science relies on an art — the trained delicacy of eye, ear and touch that connects prediction to experience; 'No map can read itself.'
- Introduces the distinction between focal and subsidiary awareness (hammer, probe, blind man's stick, textbook, one's own body) to produce 'knowledge by indwelling'.
- Concludes that personal knowing sets up impersonal standards of excellence, so that appraising good from evil carries the same authority as distinguishing order from disorder.

### Essay 4
*By Abu Sayeed Ayyub*

Ayyub takes C. P. Snow's Two Cultures as his starting point but argues that India's problem is different in timing and in kind: the scientific attitude here is still embryonic and confined to a tiny minority, so the seminar anticipates a divide that will arrive within a decade rather than reacting to one already arrived. Section I argues, against I. A. Richards, that art is a form of knowledge. Richards's 'Neutralisation of Nature' defines knowledge and truth so as to make them the scientist's monopoly, leaving poetry a set of pseudo-statements to be retained without belief; Ayyub replies that poetry cut free from belief becomes a merely decorative pattern of words, and that even Clive Bell, the purest formalist, cannot avoid asking what significant form is significant of. He recruits Maritain and Tagore for a 'poetical knowledge' that is knowledge through emotion.

Section II then reverses course and dialectically negates that argument. Having established that art is also knowledge, Ayyub insists the two ways of apprehending the world — humanistic and scientific — differ sharply in method: the humanist attends to individuals and wholes and seeks meaning and value with the knower involved, the scientist seeks generalities and parts and laws with the knower eliminated. Against Polanyi, who was in the room, he argues that personal participation does not bridge the gulf, because the scientist's passion is to discover a reality uncoloured by his passions while the artist dyes his world with his; recognition of scientific truth carries a compulsive, pragmatic force that no other truth has. He sides with the Jaina doctrine of anekantavada — the multiplicity and basic divergence of standpoints — and declares the dichotomy inescapable. Sections III and IV move to education, where his worry is not communication between the two cultures but the existence of half-cultures on both sides, and he offers what he himself calls an absurd suggestion: abolish arts courses, make all university education science education with one compulsory humanities pass paper in literature, philosophy and history, restrict admission to those with aptitude, and campaign incessantly for the indispensability of the liberal humanities.

- Reframes Snow's problem for India, where the two cultures are not yet both present and the divide is anticipated rather than diagnosed.
- Attacks the bifurcation of language into emotive expression and referential statement; art lays claim to universality, which involves a claim to truth.
- Argues that pure mathematics and music are not self-complete: mathematics is 'hypothetical or potential physics', and a symphony is a potential mould of emotional structure.
- Dissents explicitly from Polanyi, the seminar's president: personal participation is real in both spheres but differs so much in degree as to amount to a difference in kind.
- Invokes the Jaina anekantavada to argue that wholeness of culture means embracing both standpoints, not reducing either to the other — 'Man is the great amphibium'.
- Proposes a deliberately provocative reform — abolish arts degrees, universalise science degrees with a compulsory humanities paper — and offers to withdraw it as soon as something less absurd appears.

### National Self-Examination
*By Herbert Tingsten*

Tingsten sets out to test a single, narrow question: what do nations say about themselves? He first distinguishes the older doctrine, in which a national character determines historical events, from the modern scientific consensus, in which differences between nations are superficial habits produced by history rather than basic dispositions — so that character does not determine history but the reverse, and a nation can change character. He warns that national psychology is a subject that stimulates intellectual sloppiness, and that popular notions constantly reverse themselves: the English were thought capricious and immoderate in the seventeenth century and the opposite later; the French were revolutionaries and decadents in the nineteenth century and conventional petty bourgeois afterwards.

He then works through Swedish, German and French self-characterisation, with briefer notes on Austrian, English, Spanish and pan-Slavonic material. From an 1803 children's encyclopaedia through Heidenstam's 1896 essay and Gustav Sundberg's best-selling 'The Swedish National Character' (1911), the Swedes emerge as uniquely contradictory, nobly self-ironic, envious among themselves and submissive to foreigners. Schulze-Maizier, Bismarck, Treitschke, Wilhelm Wundt, Thomas Mann and National Socialist propaganda produce almost word-for-word the same picture of the Germans: brilliantly gifted, individualistic, quarrelsome, weak in national feeling, born cosmopolitans with an excessive respect for the foreign. French writers from Montegut and Renan to Maurras, Fouillée and Paul Gaultier say the same of the French. Tingsten's conclusion is that the pattern is not borrowed — most of the writers were ignorant of each other — but structural: national self-analysis is an extension of personal self-examination, in which self-praise is coated with a varnish of self-criticism and one's own weaknesses turn out on inspection to carry a mark of nobility. The political sting is in the last paragraph: this attitude motivates nationalism, because one boasts of not being a boaster, and so finally goes into battle with a good conscience.

- Distinguishes the older determinist doctrine of national character from the modern view that national differences are historical habits, so that 'Character, thus, does not determine history but vice versa.'
- Documents that popular characterisations of the English and the French have reversed completely over two or three centuries.
- Shows Swedish, German and French self-portraits converging on an identical template: contradictory and gifted, individualistic, internally divided, weak in national feeling, over-admiring of the foreign.
- Rules out literary borrowing as the explanation — the writers were largely unaware of each other's national-psychology literature and write with a joy of discovery.
- Reads national self-analysis as collective self-examination, and warns that its self-flattering humility is precisely what licenses nationalist aggression with a clear conscience.

### Human Values in Tribal Life
*By E. De Meulder*

De Meulder, a Jesuit social worker in Bihar, argues in three parts that tribal policy fails because of a wrong vision of man. Part I opens from Ashoka's rock-edict on the Pratyantavasino and Harekrishna Mahatab's use of it, and insists tribals are men par excellence: the Mundas, Santhals and Hos call themselves Horo, Hor and Hos, all meaning men, and the worst insult to an adivasi is to call him a Banjati or jungli. He argues that Harijans are mostly old tribals absorbed into the caste system, quotes Verrier Elwin and Grigson on that absorption, and reproduces Jagjivan Ram at length on untouchability as an attack on the liberty of the individual which requires attacking the theory of karma and re-birth.

Part II names three allies of the tribal people — Free India, the Christian revival, and the tribals' own self-reliance — and four opponents: the census officer, half-baked anthropologists, propagandists of an out-moded caste system, and rural capitalism and communism. He accuses the 1951 Census of suppressing sixty to seventy lakhs of tribals and of recording all but a fraction as Hindus, quoting a Census Superintendent's admission that classification 'has depended upon the whim of the enumerator'; argues that caste is hierarchical and vertical while tribal division is horizontal, so the word caste should never be applied to tribals; traces social Darwinism from Spencer and Nietzsche through the slave trade and reproduces the UNESCO 'Race and Modern Science' statement at length against it; and holds that capitalism and communism have both robbed peasant proprietors, citing the Permanent Settlement of Bengal. Part III concludes with his central inversion: do not detribalize the tribes in order to integrate them, but re-tribalize the whole Indian nation into a family of tribes with equal rights. He then makes a sharply partisan attack on Madhya Pradesh government policy under 'the Shukla-Nyogi Regime' for fighting Christian missions with government money, and argues that any school practising segregation — for adibasis only or for Harijans only — is anti-national.

- The organising claim is that wrong visions of man produce educational and social failure — instanced by Nazi Germany, Stalinist Russia and apartheid South Africa before India.
- Argues the census has statistically liquidated whole tribes by absorbing them into Hinduism and the caste system, and that the notion of caste is alien to the notion of tribe.
- Reproduces the UNESCO statement on race at length to rebut social Darwinism and its political uses.
- Proposes the inversion 'do not detribalize the tribes... but rather re-tribalize the whole of the Indian nation' as the way to build a democratic India.
- Argues against segregated schooling for adibasis and Harijans on national grounds, and notes that Verrier Elwin has changed his earlier view on segregating tribals.
- Printed with an editorial disclaimer — 'This is a Christian missionary's view of tribal problems in India. The editors of QUEST would expect readers to express their reactions.'

### A Poem
*By Sigurdur A. Magnusson*

A six-line poem, printed under the POETRY rubric with a bare question mark where a title would go — the contents page lists it simply as 'A Poem'. It figures Time as the force that pulled the speaker from his mother's womb and holds him tight in its palm, and locates behind Time not an answer but a question, imaged as a curved serpent with an apple. The Edenic emblem turns the poem's metaphysical question about origin and captivity into a question about knowledge and temptation.

- Printed with '?' in place of a title; the contents page supplies the neutral description 'A Poem'.
- Six lines, unpunctuated except for a colon and a closing full stop, with 'behind Time' repeated as a structural hinge.
- Closes on the Genesis emblem of serpent and apple, turning the question behind Time into a question about knowledge.

### This City
*By Amaresh Datta*

A four-stanza free-verse poem in which the city is the subject and the speaker its exhausted counterpart. Three of the stanzas open by naming the city differently — plebian, tired, howling — and record what it has known: the ecstasy of rains in its dry bones, songs of the sun, the coming impact of silence at night. Against each of these the speaker sets his own incapacity: what revives the city is fierce or deafening to him, because he is as dry as the city's soul and as old and preserved as its body. The poem closes by separating them for good, the city hearing the mysteries of the wind and the stars while the speaker seeks 'Death of memory in the pursuit of joy'.

- Built on a repeated contrast between what the city can still feel and what the speaker no longer can.
- The city's revival is registered physically — rains in dry bones, a mason's steel-frame dream melting into drizzle, trees growing tense in their rhythm of life.
- Ends by dividing city from speaker: the city will hear the wind and stars, the speaker will seek the death of memory.

### For All Who Suffer
*By Jeremy Nelson*

A devotional narrative poem in eight quatrains of loose iambic tetrameter, mostly rhymed or half-rhymed. The speaker recounts walking with God the Father before sleep and speaking to him as a trusting child; God smiles, touches his chin, takes his hand and leads him towards a hill bearing a sign few would care to look at. The journey crosses towns, lakes, ancient peaks of snow and 'the shell of time' until anguish fills the skies, and God says that pain is part of paradise and that he is 'both the wound and blade'. What the speaker is shown on the darkened hill is an angry mob calling for the crucifixion of the Son of God. The poem ends by reversing the expected consolation: the dreamer watches God weep and seek for men on whom to lean.

- A dream-vision framed by sleep at both ends — 'Last night before the hour of sleep' and 'Last night within the time of sleep'.
- The theology is explicitly one of shared suffering rather than rescue from it.
- Ends with the inversion of the consoling God into one who weeps and needs men to lean on.

### "Where Waters Meet"
*By Raja Proctor*

A frame story set on a Ceylonese lagoon. The narrator, an Information Office man from Colombo who fishes alone and drinks from a hip-flask, is warned off by fisherboys — 'No watersmit for you!' — and driven by a monsoon squall into a village church, where he finds two unopened half-pint bottles of Ceylon arrack at the feet of a statue of St. Anthony. An English priest, twenty years in the country, explains them.

The inner story concerns Parris, a powerful fisherman called 'The Bear', his wife Rua ('The Dove') and their son Robo, whose left foot is amputated after an accident between two out-rigger canoes. Parris begins to drink; the boy, taught by the priest, becomes preoccupied with death, suffering, fate and faith. On the morning after his mother threatens to leave, the boy intercepts his father on the way to fishing and spends the day manoeuvring him away from drink — buying provisions on credit, faking pain from his lame leg outside the tavern, and finally goading him into fishing the dangerous Waters Meet by calling him a coward and a drunkard, so that both his hands stay on the oar. A squall takes the boat; Parris, having just understood what his son has been doing, drowns holding the boat's head to the sea, and the boy is found clinging to an unopened half-pint, insisting to the village that his father did not drink. The bottles on the altar are Parris's vow, made before St. Anthony, to come back for them the day another drop of arrack wets his throat. The story closes by turning back on the narrator, who swigs from his own flask and asks himself, 'Can't Waters Meet happen to me?'

- A frame narrative whose object — two unopened arrack bottles on a Catholic altar — is explained only at the end as a dead man's vow.
- The child's campaign against his father's drinking is conducted entirely by indirection: provisions on credit, faked pain, and a deliberate insult.
- The fishermen's chorus misreads the death completely as a drunkard's folly, which is what forces the boy's protest.
- The closing pages implicate the narrator, whose own solitary drinking makes him the priest's next case rather than merely the story's listener.

### The Scar
*By Gopi Gauba*

A two-character story told almost entirely in alternating interior monologue. A painter, who left his father and a hosiery business for art, learns that his dead childhood friend Bholu has left money to his wife rather than to him, and the question 'Why to you?' takes possession of him. The story stages the interrogation twice over: what each says aloud, clipped and wounding, and what each thinks and longs to say, which is almost entirely tenderness. He insists he is not a villain, not jealous, trying to be just and reasonable; she, innocent, discovers that the fact of being asked at all is the injury — 'What good is my word, what good is our love if there is no belief, no trust.'

Pressed for details, she finally tells him that Bholu had asked her to marry him before he did, and that one day Bholu told her to leave this poverty and run away with him so that her husband could return to his father. She had kept it secret for years because telling would have killed something in a husband who trusted his friend implicitly. He retreats to the other room; she, alone, arrives at the understanding that the bequest was a confession from a man torn between loyalty to a friend and desire for that friend's wife — 'how utterly callously successful sometimes the dead can be.' She gropes her way to him and finds he has been weeping too, and the story ends on her line naming what has happened to them: 'Our first scar... a heavy price for growing up.'

- Technique carries the story: spoken dialogue and unspoken monologue run in parallel and contradict each other throughout.
- The wound is epistemic rather than sexual — being disbelieved, not being unfaithful, is what she cannot survive.

### Seminar on Science and Humanities
*By David McCutchion*

McCutchion reports the three-day seminar whose papers fill the front of the issue, and his interest is in the gap between Polanyi and his audience. Polanyi's presidential attack on scientific optimism ran contrary to the general disposition of the room and forced an initially philosophical orientation on the seminar, 'some under protest, loud or muttered'. The majority — from Radical Humanists to physicists, economists and technicians — regarded the scientific method as indispensable in India precisely because opposition to science is associated with die-hard traditionalism and obscurantist politics. Sibnarayan Ray led the defence of rationalism, arguing that personal error in judgement is exactly why science matters and that an emphasis on personal knowledge risks impeding communication and encouraging 'isolated self-sufficient microcosms'; Polanyi replied that a misguided ideal of exactness was distorting all teaching, poured scorn on the belief that science can predict, and accused Ray of conventionalism in ethics. - Summarises the practical consensus: pilot colleges, teacher training, methodology before content, internal assessment, restricted entry.

*By P. Lal*

Lal replies to Jyotirmoy Datta's Quest 28 attack on Indians writing creatively in English, opening with regret at its tone — 'Facts elevate, opinions only corrupt' — and dividing his answer into a clearing of six preliminary confusions and then Datta's four substantive reasons. ### G. H. Deshmukh and His Ideas
*By D. K. Bedekar*

Bedekar reviews N. R. Inamdar's edition of the essays Gopal Hari Deshmukh (1823-92, pen-name 'Lok-hitavadi') wrote in 1848-50, and argues their value is as a datum-line. Deshmukh belongs to the first generation of the modern Maharashtra intelligentsia, which came on the scene almost half a century after Bengal's, so that the ideas it confronted had already taken root elsewhere; because the decades after 1857 brought rapid, abrupt and violent change to Maharashtrian thought, a clear reference point in the preceding period is what makes those changes legible. He notes that the essays were written when Deshmukh was twenty-five, and that their direct and forceful diction contrasts with the heavy, ornate, Sanskrit- and English-loaded Marathi prose of the later university-trained generation — a deterioration he attributes partly to Marathi ceasing after 1863 to be part of the Bombay University curriculum.

The substantive analysis identifies three concepts. First, education: 116 of 375 pages are devoted to it, and Deshmukh's attack on the Brahmin priests is directed not at caste privilege but at their body of culture and learning, which he declares narrow, outdated and inferior to modern learning — Bedekar aligns this with Karl Mannheim's account of learning ceasing to be the property of a caste and becoming an open 'intellectual process'. Second, self-determination, a word Deshmukh did not have but whose principle underlies his advocacy of widow remarriage on the ground that a widow has a right to seek her own happiness, his economic self-reliance, and his political essays, which project an Indian parliament, an Indian majority demanding equal rights, and finally Swaraj, with the British reverting to the position of traders. Third, a non-aggregative concept of society, in which individuals' ideas are moulded by the total social environment — Deshmukh argues that Hindu belief in Fate is itself the product of lawless rule, and that under a rule of equal opportunity no one would speak of Fate's vagaries. Bedekar closes by noting that Deshmukh's integrated framework, linking individual and community dynamically, was not fully grasped by his successors.

- Frames the essays as a 'datum-line' for measuring later Maharashtrian social thought rather than as an intrinsic achievement.
- Argues the Maharashtra intelligentsia arrived half a century after Bengal's, into a field where ideas had already matured.
- Reads Deshmukh's anti-Brahmin polemic as aimed at a body of learning, not at caste privilege — a distinction his western-educated successors missed.
- Uses Mannheim on the laicization of learning to gloss Deshmukh's 'intellectual process', while correcting Mannheim's sequence for Indian conditions in a footnote.
- Quotes Deshmukh's 1848 forecast of an Indian parliament, an Indian majority, and Swaraj, with the British reduced to traders.
- Notes Deshmukh's sociological point that belief in Fate is an effect of lawless rule rather than a cause of passivity.

A severe review of Nancy Mitford's novel by a reviewer who admires her earlier work. He calls her celebrated and extremely facile — doubting whether those two attributes are entirely complementary — and the author of 'one of the finest light masterpieces of biography to appear in our times', her Madame de Pompadour; but returning to social comedy she has produced a boring novel, and if anyone except Mitford had written it he would call it not only boring but pretentious. His hypothesis is that she keeps a discard file of ideas beside her desk and has cleaned it out for this book, which reads as if she had not wanted to write it any more than this reader wanted to read it.

The plot — Lady Wincham, whose husband Alfred is suddenly and mysteriously appointed British ambassador in Paris, beset by a Zen-converted son with an English wife and a Chinese baby, and a social secretary irresistible to French dignitaries and incapable in any known language — he dismisses as a thin story with ivy of embassy vignettes and commentaries growing over it, neither profound nor light but 'in-between-half humor, half pompousness'. The review's substantive complaint is social: he has long thought that the English believe they are evolving up the ladder of intelligence by laughing generously at themselves, and while the inability to ridicule one's own countrymen produces depressing heaviness, Mitford's forced funniness is a world 'not of humor but of snobbism and narcissism' in which everyone is titled, posted high, rich or old tie, and beneath the invitation to laugh runs an undercurrent of complacent contentment in one's station in life that he finds sad. He concedes she has a large audience and that some may enjoy reading how the upper half lives, but says he cannot imagine anyone finishing the book, and admits he gave up on page 175 though the jacket blurb told him the conclusion is riotous.

- Separates admiration for Mitford's Madame de Pompadour from a flat rejection of this novel.
- Argues the book's humour is a vehicle for snobbism and narcissism rather than for self-criticism.
- Generalises about the English habit of mistaking self-ridicule for intelligence, while allowing that its absence produces heaviness.
- Ends with an unusual reviewer's confession — abandonment at page 175, against the jacket blurb's promise.

### The Indian Intellectual
*By Vinod Sena*

Sena's letter defends his Quest 28 'Counter-Statement' on the Indian intellectual against a critic writing as 'Sanjaya', opening with the wish that his critic had read what he wrote before criticising it and pointing to specific sections and footnotes to establish that he is no blind votary of the past, has no basic antipathy to British culture, and finds no special charm in poverty and misery. He restates his relation to Edward Shils: he never regretted Shils being a foreigner, since only an outsider could have observed 'us' with such accurate objectivity, and never challenged the validity of his account; his objection was to Shils's explanations, offered after too short a stay and therefore lacking the inwardness that observation alone cannot supply. Against them he had proposed three further factors — an inevitable Industrial Revolution, the aggravation caused not by British culture per se but by the unfavourable conditions in which India fell under its shadow, and the absence of any readily available ideology consonant with Indian attitudes and suited to modern technological needs.

What has really troubled 'Sanjaya', he argues, is not the argument but the words 'spiritual' and 'tradition', which for the Angry Young Men of the generation have become archetypes firing independently of context. He insists he is as aware as anyone of the need for change — 'to stop would be (for us) instant disaster' — but that sound psychological development rests on an initial, not uncritical, acceptance of one's personality followed by steady transformation, and that tradition is not static: foreign elements may be grafted on and transform it out of recognition, but the base must be of native stock, and a tradition that ceases to shed dead foliage degenerates into 'the choking weed of mere convention'. On ethnocentricity he reaffirms that egoistic tendency is natural in peoples as in individuals and makes purposive collective living possible. What he regrets about British rule is not what was learnt but the unnaturalness of imperial conditions, which led Indians to learn 'often to the prejudice of what was ours: languages, arts and sciences' — synthesis being possible only when traditions meet on a footing of equality. The cure is to wean ourselves from an irrational attitude to the West before we can truly learn from it.

### The Nagy Affair
*By Thomas Schreiber*

Schreiber writes to deplore the brief review of 'The Truth About the Nagy Affair' that appeared in Quest 28, and his method is to turn the reviewer's own credentials against him. He notes that Quest introduced John Smithard as 'a very talented amateur actor and producer (who is) keenly interested in the arts', and professes ignorance of why such a person was moved to pronounce on a book devoted not to the arts but to an important political problem. Against Smithard's verdict that there is no reason whatever for this expensive, difficult and out-of-date propaganda, he quotes the editorial committee's own statement of purpose — to help throw light on certain points in an important chapter of contemporary history — which he says hardly indicates propaganda rather than a serious document based on irrefutable facts that will aid future historians.

On 'out-of-date' he notes that the original French edition appeared four months after the Budapest verdict, 'a fact which constitutes a veritable tour de force', and argues more generally that publication date is secondary: new information and documents on the Dreyfus Affair and on the causes of the First World War are still appearing, so the events of 1956 can never be out of date. He then sharpens the sarcasm, suggesting that for Smithard such publications are perhaps useless and the money better spent on amateur theatricals, and that the next thing to expect is a demand that all institutions documenting contemporary history be closed down since their publications too are costly and their public limited. He closes by informing Quest's 'collaborateur' that the book, in French, English, German, Spanish and Hungarian editions, aroused great interest in Europe and the United States, not only in the press but above all in circles of the political left and among historians of considerable note — and adds, with a final turn of the knife, that it is gratifying to find a talented amateur actor suddenly interested in political literature even if his first attempts are not a great success.

### Indo-Anglian Writers
*By K. Raghavendra Rao*

Rao writes as a self-described small-scale Indo-Anglian writer, in general agreement with Jyotirmoy Datta's position but deploring the manner of his case, and adds an emotional diagnosis of the whole controversy: behind the elaborate theoretical poses on both sides lies a very human conflict of frustrations and jealousies, since he knows Indian-language writers with odd ambitions to write in English and Indo-Anglians frustrated at not being able to write in their mother tongues. On money he agrees that a true writer is moved by higher motives, but notes that many non-Indo-Anglians are content with Rs 25 only because they know they can get little more, so the mercenary motive should not be saddled specially on the Indian writer in English; Datta's sneering at the 'IEW' has the unfortunate effect of straining already strained relations.

He then raises two analytic doubts. Relating creative writing to a living language is right but risks oversimplification: unless carefully qualified it degenerates into an argument for mass literature. And Datta's line on Goethe — 'No one was a better European because no one was a truer German' — puzzles him, since it could mean either that Goethe was the most German among European writers or the most European among German writers, and in neither case can his Europeanness simply be assimilated to his Germanness. Defending P. Lal's position without presuming to defend Lal himself, he argues that the only contact currently available between India's regional languages is English, and asks whether Datta has not heard of the narrow prejudices and destructive contempt between writers from different regions. He signs off with an exasperated blessing on both houses: to hell with all quarrels, let the Indo-Anglian and the non-Indo-Anglian each go his own cursed way, and let them create first, leaving the wrangles to pseudo-pundits and nosey journalists.
