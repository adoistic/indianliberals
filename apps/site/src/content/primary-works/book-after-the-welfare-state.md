## Summary

An anthology against the welfare state, edited by Tom G. Palmer for Students For Liberty and the Atlas Network in 2012 and reprinted in India by the Centre for Civil Society with a foreword written for the Indian edition by Bibek Debroy. Palmer's case, stated in the introduction, is that the welfare state is responsible for two crises at once: the financial crisis that stalled growth across the world, and the debt crisis gripping Europe and the United States. Both, he argues, load obligations onto the people least able to refuse them, which is to say children and the young.

The volume is organised in sections. The first treats the welfare state as a common pool problem: Palmer's own essay argues that a system in which everyone may draw on a fund nobody owns produces the same result as a lake nobody owns, and Piercamillo Falasca and Aristides Hatzis supply the Italian and Greek cases. The second section is historical, tracing the welfare state to Bismarck and describing what it displaced, with David Green on the evolution of mutual aid and David Beito on American fraternal societies. The third connects the welfare state to the financial and debt crises, with Michael Tanner treating it as a pyramid scheme and Johan Norberg arguing that the pursuit of affordable housing inflated the bubble that broke the world economy. A closing section turns to poverty and the moral case.

Bibek Debroy's foreword makes the Indian argument. He takes the socialism written into the Preamble by the Forty-second Amendment as a large part of the problem, argues that the market failures which justified state provision in the 1950s no longer hold at India's present level of development, and asks whether entry regulations have been relaxed enough to let the private sector in. Growth, he writes, is not an end in itself; it is what pays for public expenditure and what carries improvements in life expectancy, literacy and infant mortality with it.

## Key points

- Tom G. Palmer argues that the welfare state produced both the financial crisis and the debt crisis, and that its costs fall hardest on children and young people.
- Palmer analyses the welfare state as a tragedy of the commons: a fund nobody owns and everybody may draw on creates an incentive for its own exhaustion.
- Piercamillo Falasca and Aristides Hatzis treat Italy and Greece as worked examples of unsustainable promises.
- A historical section traces the welfare state to Bismarck and argues that it displaced older institutions rather than filling a vacuum.
- David Green on mutual aid and David Beito on American fraternal societies describe the voluntary welfare arrangements that preceded state provision.
- Michael Tanner argues that the welfare state functions as a pyramid scheme, paying present claims out of future obligations.
- Johan Norberg argues that the political commitment to affordable housing inflated the credit bubble whose collapse became the global financial crisis.
- Bibek Debroy's Indian foreword argues that the socialism inserted into the Preamble in 1976 shaped the wrong path, and that market failure arguments from the 1950s no longer fit India's present level of development.
- Debroy treats growth as a means rather than an end: it funds public expenditure and carries improvements in literacy, life expectancy and infant mortality with it.
