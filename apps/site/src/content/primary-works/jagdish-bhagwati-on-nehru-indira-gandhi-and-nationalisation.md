summary: |-
  Jagdish Bhagwati distinguishes between Nehru's and Indira Gandhi's approaches to state ownership. He notes Nehru's own admission in The Discovery of India that his inclination was to be a dictator, and links this to a cultural tendency to accept authority that liberals must challenge by insisting the market be allowed to work. Bhagwati characterises Nehru as a moderate Fabian socialist and "slowpoke" who never actually called for nationalisation; instead his policy sought to enlarge the public sector gradually by raising its share of investment, so that over a long horizon public means of production would asymptotically come to dominate the private sector.

  Bhagwati contends that the serious damage to the system came not from Nehru but from his daughter Indira Gandhi, whom he judges neither a socialist by conviction nor an intellectual. He argues she turned to nationalisation strategically, to defeat the Congress syndicate, and, finding that it delivered power, adopted socialism as a purely strategic pose rather than out of belief.
