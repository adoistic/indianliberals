# QUEST

## Summary

Quest No. 51 (Oct./Dec. 1966) is the third number to appear under Abu Sayeed Ayyub as sole editor, with the eight-man Editorial Board (R. Bhaskaran, Buddhadeva Bose, Amlan Datta, Rajni Kothari, Daya Krishna, Raj Krishna, M. P. Rege, A. B. Shah) printed on the cover beneath his name and no advisory editors at all. The number has two argumentative centres. One is the university: Amlan Datta argues that a developing economy needs a dynamic, problem-oriented and deliberately selective system of education and research, and D. Anjaneyulu supplies the polemical companion piece, a mordant survey of Indian higher education in which the scholar's real vice is 'Academese'; a review of G. K. Saiyidain on universities and a review of a book on the teaching of English extend the same quarrel into the review pages. The other centre is the standing anti-communist commitment of the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, carried here by Michael Polanyi's address on the tenth anniversary of the Hungarian Revolution, which reads 1956 as a wholesale return to the ideals of 1848 and indicts value-free behavioural political science for being unable to see it, and by M. M. Sankhdher's argument that socialism has quietly dissolved into welfare statism and 'regulative capitalism'.

Around those poles the issue runs unusually wide: R. B. Patankar on the proper relation of philosophical aesthetics to literary criticism, Gulabdas Broker on whether Indian writers still project an Indian image, Dilip Chitre on the Marathi poet Keshavsut in his centenary year, two policy pieces on the tribal areas and the Mizo insurgency written from opposite ends of the same argument, a Nigerian journalist's post-coup autopsy of the first republic, a two-part Arnold-and-Keats exchange between Vinod Sena and Devindra Kohli that continues from No. 48, four poems, two short stories, nine book reviews and four letters. The undisclosed overlaps between the masthead and the reviewed matter are conspicuous: the lead review is of a book by a sitting member of the Editorial Board, and the poetry review is of a collection published by the journal's own Reviews Editor and written by a poet printed in this same number.

## Essays

### Aesthetics and Literary Criticism
*By R. B. Patankar*

Patankar sets out to demarcate what literary criticism can legitimately ask of philosophical aesthetics. He rejects F. R. Leavis's view that criticism has nothing to do with aesthetics, but grants that critics came by that view honestly, having asked philosophers for the wrong kind of help and been answered with omniscience and no critical sensibility. His own account is that evaluation is inescapable in criticism (there are no valuationally neutral literary facts), that evaluation implies criteria, and that criteria form competing 'spirals' which drive the critic from 'Is this a good poem?' to 'What is good poetry?' and finally to aesthetics.

He then tries four schools in turn. Metaphysical aesthetics flatters the artist and helps nobody: Absolute Idealism makes every sensuous presentation beautiful and so abolishes ugliness. Non-metaphysical system-builders such as Harold Osborne discipline critical discourse usefully but end by denying part of aesthetic life for the sake of self-consistency. Intuitionists and Emotivists make the decisive gain, by showing that universal aesthetic propositions state criteria rather than the meaning of beauty, so that many sets of beauty-making properties are possible. The cautionary example is the Marathi critic B. S. Mardhekar, whose three Laws of Rhythm worked (perhaps) for the plastic arts and were then forced on literature. Patankar's conclusion is that the aesthetics of literature is nothing but poetics, that the Analysts' method is excellent but their interest in literature negligible, and that critics must therefore analyse their own concepts.

- Rejects Leavis's separation of criticism from aesthetics while conceding the historical grievance behind it.
- Argues no literary fact is valuationally neutral, so criteria and therefore aesthetics are unavoidable.
- Metaphysical aesthetics cancels the distinction between beauty and ugliness and so cannot serve the practical critic.
- Uses B. S. Mardhekar's Laws of Rhythm as the case study in illegitimately extending one art's criteria to another.
- Concludes that critics, not philosophers, must do the analysis of critical concepts; 'the aesthetics of literature is nothing but poetics'.

### Education, Science and Economic Development
*By Amlan Datta*

Datta argues that economic development is qualitative, not merely quantitative, and asks what kind of knowledge a developing economy needs. His first target is the diversification of higher secondary education recommended by the Sapru Committee of 1934 and the Mudaliar Commission: secondary school should be a preparation for university, not a vocational sorting house, and the selection of students for the university stream should be made at about fourteen, with the rest directed to technical institutions or on-the-job training. He argues in a long footnote from Soviet experience that multiplying institutions without regard to quality wrecks standards.

The paper then treats teaching method (a traditional society transmits settled conclusions; a dynamic one must teach through dialogue, which requires abandoning sole reliance on written examinations), the return migration of scientists (the graver loss is not defection abroad but the researcher who comes home and stops producing), the case for concentrating resources on a few centres of excellence, promotion by output rather than seniority, and the need for a class of 'dedicated intermediaries' and regional-language scientific literature to carry science down to people who will never attend a university. It closes on philosophy: science demands not the acceptance or renunciation of God but the belief that the measurable universe obeys measurable laws, and 'Science is intolerant of magic'. A closing footnote records that the article was written before the Education Commission reported and was not revised in its light.

- Opposes vocational diversification of higher secondary schooling; wants a consolidated pre-university course with mathematics carried to the end.
- Proposes selection for the university stream at about the age of fourteen, with technical institutions for the rest.
- Identifies the loss of researchers who return from abroad and stop researching as more damaging than outright brain drain.
- Argues for concentrating resources on a few centres of excellence, on the Japanese imperial-university model, rather than upgrading all institutions equally.
- Calls for promotion by research output and student assessment rather than seniority, and for 'dedicated intermediaries' to popularise science in the regional languages.

### The Message of the Hungarian Revolution
*By Michael Polanyi*

Polanyi marks the tenth anniversary of the Hungarian Revolution by insisting on what it was about. The rebellion began in June 1956 at the Petofi Circle, an official Party organ, among Communist Party members who had been the regime's genuine beneficiaries, and what they demanded was that truth be recognised as an independent power in public life: a free press, public condemnation of the faked trials, the restoration of the rule of law, and an art released from subservience to the Party. He reads this, and the whole post-Stalin transformation of the Soviet countries, as a wholesale return to the ideals of 1848 and of the Enlightenment, a 'smashing of spectacles' rather than a change of them.

The polemical weight of the piece falls on Western social science for being unable to see this. Polanyi quotes Richard Pipes's admission that he suppressed the claim that Russian intellectuals were fighting for truth because it sounded 'naive and unscientific', then turns on a 1963 textbook of political behaviour introduced by Robert A. Dahl, which restricts science to observable events, excludes statements of value, and derides the question 'What is a good society?' as 'political theology'. He argues the position is self-contradictory by two complementary routes: a scientist who makes moral judgments privately must concede that moral truth exists and so that not all action can be explained without reference to moral judgment; a scientist who can explain all action value-freely must deny that his own acts have moral motives. The cost of the evasion is not academic, since it discredits both the motives and the aims of those fighting for freedom. He ends by predicting that the Russian Revolution may be recognised by its own successors, by its fiftieth anniversary, as having been virtually pointless.

- Insists the Hungarian rising began among committed Party members and demanded truth as an autonomous power in public life.
- Reads 1956 as a return to the ideals of 1848 and the Enlightenment, continuous with Western intellectuals' defections from communism and with the release of the Kremlin doctors after Stalin's death.
- Attacks value-free behavioural political science, and Robert A. Dahl's introduction in particular, for excluding value statements and so being unable to describe what happened.
- Offers two complementary arguments that moral neutralism in social science is internally inconsistent.
- Cites the post-1963 acceptance of Liberman's critique of detailed planning as evidence that 'there is in fact no viable socialist alternative to the market'.
- Reprinted by permission from a Phi Beta Kappa copyright holder, and delivered as a talk rather than written as an article.

### Socialism Revised
*By M. M. Sankhdher*

Sankhdher argues that socialism has changed its content while keeping its name. Conventional dictionary definitions in terms of community ownership of the means of production are formal and misleading; the word, used in Italy in 1803, by Owen in 1827, by Saint Simon in 1832 and by Marx in 1867, has meant contradictory things, and Western socialists now face 'a dilemma: how to keep faith in socialism without loss of face'. What has been injected in place of ownership is the welfare state: social justice, full employment in a mixed economy, extended social services, graduated taxation, relief for old age and sickness, and much less noise about equality of wealth or nationalisation.

He attributes the shift to the experience of office, to the mistaken identification of labour movements with socialism, and to the failure of Marxian prognosis (the masses have not grown more miserable, the middle class has not disappeared, the state shows no sign of withering away). Socialists now adopt individualist arguments, accept capitalism's motive of self-interest, and differ from liberals and conservatives chiefly in professing an ideology; he notes that in England liberals and conservatives 'did more for the welfare of the working classes than the socialists'. The conclusion is that socialism has become welfare statism and 'regulative capitalism', and has lost its identity as an independent creed in transit.

- Treats the incoherence of 'socialism' as a historical fact about the word, dated from 1803 through Owen, Saint Simon and Marx.
- Identifies the substitution of welfare-state goals for ownership or control of the means of production as the decisive revision.
- Lists the failed Marxian predictions that forced the revision, and notes that socialists now regard themselves as liberal empiricists.
- Argues socialists differ from conservatives mainly in professing an ideology, and quotes Otto Bauer that 'Nobody manages industrial undertakings worse than the state'.
- Concludes socialism has become 'regulative capitalism' aimed at social welfare, its separate identity lost.

### Projecting the Indian Image
*By Gulabdas Broker*

Broker argues that a culture, not a country, projects an image, and that it does so through its arts and letters by an almost subconscious process rather than by design. He contrasts Greek tragedy with classical Sanskrit drama, Grecian sculpture with the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas of Ellora and Ajanta, and Tagore with Goethe or Rilke, to show that identical artistic excellence can carry wholly different patterns of approach. The Indian image he identifies is one that hankers for peace after war and for renunciation after kingship, that mellows the tragic into the Santa rather than ending in blood, and that works through symbols such as the lotus; he illustrates the last with an anecdote of an inverted stone lotus found beneath a pillar in an ancient church outside Rome.

The second half turns on the present. India seems to him in a sorry plight and its writers and artists correspondingly lost, sending up a cry of despair where they once sent up a shout of hope, borrowing new Western forms without the emotional impact that would justify them. His argument is not that art should be consoling but that it must be genuine: an honest record of a depressed and unsure spirit would still be an Indian image, whereas imitation is where art perishes.

- Distinguishes culture from country as the unit that projects an image, since several countries can share one culture.
- Holds that the projection is subconscious, and that tradition itself is the product of a people's inner urges rather than their cause.
- Characterises the Indian image through classical drama, Ajanta and Ellora, and the refusal of the blood-soaked tragic ending.
- Finds contemporary Indian writers imitating Western forms without the emotional impact behind them.
- Argues that genuineness, not cheerfulness, is the test: a depressed but genuine work still projects an Indian image.

### A Tribute to Keshavsut
*By Dilip Chitre*

Chitre writes on the Marathi poet Keshavsut in the year of his birth centenary, and opens by refusing the centenary's terms: the exact date of Keshavsut's birth is being debated, his horoscope has become a source of concern for literary critics, and Marxists, pure poets, Dadaists and Hindu mystics are all scrambling to claim him. Against that he argues there is no one Keshavsut, since the poems are 'Janus-faced, multivalent and void', and that he approaches him as a fellow poet in the same language.

His judgement is severe and admiring at once. Keshavsut was a less talented poet than many who followed him but a genius, because he was inspired by life rather than by literature; his failure is traceable to his models, drawn from The Golden Treasury and mid-Victorian ideals, which cut his generation off from Marathi tradition without giving them a living English. Chitre traces the decay of that borrowed Romanticism into 'Parnassianism' in his followers Balakavi and Govindagraja, reads Keshavsut's so-called social consciousness as moral sensitiveness grounded in Advaita rather than politics, and finds the real achievement in the short metres and the metaphysical poems, where symbols are charged with power and the verse attains 'a terse, rugged expression: an economy, a grip, a functional pertinence'. He closes by saying he translates the poems as a tribute because, being neither legislator nor building-contractor, he cannot raise a monument.

- Written for Keshavsut's officially celebrated birth centenary in October 1966, and openly sceptical of the centenary industry.
- Argues there is no single Keshavsut, since the poems are formal metaphors that different readers legitimately use differently.
- Blames the failures on models taken from The Golden Treasury and on a generation cut off from Marathi tradition without a living English.
- Reads Keshavsut's 'social consciousness' as Advaita-grounded moral sensitiveness, consonant with his Romanticism and symbolism.
- Locates the achievement in the short metres and the metaphysical poems, where he shows 'a remarkable ability to charge symbols with power'.

### Approach to the Development of Tribal Areas
*By C. V. Raghavulu*

Raghavulu attacks the policy of preventing the transfer of tribal land to non-tribals, which he treats as an inheritance from British 'isolationism' continued unchanged after Independence. He accepts that the Scheduled Tribes need special treatment but argues that protection of tribal land was wrongly made an adjunct of it, and tests the policy against three questions: whether its assumptions are justifiable, what it has done to development in the tribal areas, and whether it helps integration.

On the first, the assumption that all outsiders are exploiters and that there is no exploitation among tribals is, on N. K. Bose's and S. C. Dube's evidence, patently absurd. On the second, protection has not prevented large-scale alienation, and schemes such as the Tribal Development Colonies failed anyway: settlers in the Asoknagar colony sold 75 per cent of their assigned land within a decade and went home, and the Todas of the Nilgiris refused assignment outside their existing habitations. He attributes much of this to weak 'achievement motivation' bred by ecological isolation and stagnant living standards, and to policy conditioned by 'too much of sentimentalism for the tribals'. On the third, isolation has strengthened separatism, as N.E.F.A. in 1962, Nagaland and the Mizo Hills showed. His proposal is to admit plainsmen and their capital, by analogy with the liberalisation of foreign private capital, with government surveillance, forest surveys and joint-stock or cooperative vehicles in which government, plainsmen and tribals are all represented. He is candid that the plainsmen will not come from benevolence.

- Treats non-alienation of tribal land as a survival of British isolationism rather than a considered development policy.
- Cites N. K. Bose and S. C. Dube against the premise that outsiders alone exploit and tribals never exploit each other.
- Uses the Asoknagar colony in Warangal district and the Todas of the Nilgiris as evidence that protected settlement schemes failed.
- Argues that isolation has bred separatism, pointing to N.E.F.A., Nagaland and the Mizo Hills.
- Proposes admitting plainsmen and non-tribal capital under government surveillance, with joint-stock companies or cooperatives representing government, plainsmen and tribals.

### The Grooves of Academe
*By D. Anjaneyulu*

Anjaneyulu opens on the economics of education, quoting Harbison and Myers that human-resource planning can be brought inside the framework of development planning and that investments in man are not subject to diminishing returns, and Adam Curle that nobody can prove a sensitive appreciation of poetry is unrelated to economic growth. He then turns his attention to the universities, and the tone changes from policy to satire.

The charge sheet is that Indian higher education works under 'a deteriorated copy of an obsolete model'; that external examinations split teaching from learning, so that teaching becomes oral rendition of an out-of-date textbook and learning becomes guessing topics and cramming for regurgitation; that neither teacher nor student is curious outside a fixed subject; and that the teacher's favourite idol is Lakshmi rather than Saraswati, with note-books, tutoring a millionaire's son and a new examinership preferred to the library. Research in the humanities is 'another name for the collection between the covers of one book of material found scattered in several', proceeding with due dignity 'from inadequate facts to untenable conclusions', and written in what he names 'Academese'. Against this he sets English and American academics who argue publicly, Taylor against Trevor-Roper, Crossman against Crossland in Encounter, Galbraith and Schlesinger, and Leavis swooping on C. P. Snow and J. B. Priestley, and he asks whether any Indian professor now attracts students by the force of his work as Laski did. He notes the new Sussex and Essex models and observes that Indian institutions follow neither, being neither liberal enough for the humanities nor pragmatic enough for the sciences.

- Frames education as an investment not subject to diminishing returns, quoting Frederick Harbison, Charles A. Myers and Adam Curle.
- Blames the external examination system for severing teaching from learning in Indian higher education.
- Satirises the careerism of university teachers, whose 'favourite idol is Lakshmi rather than Saraswati'.
- Coins 'Academese' for the prose of Indian humanities research, which turns the familiar obscure and the commonplace profound.
- Contrasts Indian academic silence with public intellectual quarrels in Britain and America, and finds Indian universities following neither the Sussex nor the Essex pattern.

### The Mizo Problem
*By Hirendranath Phukan*

Phukan, reporting from Assam, argues that separatist and secessionist demands have crystallised among the hillmen because of plainsmen's indifference and because the rest of India has failed to widen the mainstream enough to include them as equals. His test of indifference is personal: how many hillmen can a plainsman count among his friends, and how often has he tried to establish rapport with one. In Shillong, where both live together, there is almost no intermixing, no knowledge of hill languages, and excommunication for anyone who marries a girl from the hills.

The administrative charge is 'pampering': overlooking a hill student drawing both salary and scholarship, hesitating to discipline a delinquent tribal official, and above all backing away from arresting law-breakers until the situation requires military force. He narrates the growth of the Mizo National Front out of the Mizo Cultural Society and the Mizo National Famine Front through the mautam famine, the unpunished incidents at Aijal in 1960, the enrolment of 'volunteers' after the MNF's 1962 defeat, Laldenga's and Lalmunawia's secret trip to East Pakistan in 1963, the extortion of traders from early 1965, the memorandum demanding independence handed to Lal Bahadur Shastri at Gauhati in October 1965, and the March uprising precipitated by the movement of the 18th battalion of the Assam Rifles. His remedy inverts the usual direction of integration: because minorities cannot be expected to dissolve into a majority, it is the plainsmen, as the majority, who must change first and form a composite community, with the Assamese carrying the greatest responsibility, and with a non-political, non-religious body on the model of the American Peace Corps to send the best plainsmen to work in the remotest hill areas.

- Locates the cause of Mizo separatism in plainsmen's social indifference rather than in economic neglect alone.
- Names the administration's policy 'pampering', and argues that unpunished violence taught the MNF that government could be intimidated.
- Supplies a dated narrative of the MNF from the Mizo Cultural Society through the mautam famine to the March 1966 uprising.
- Argues the majority community must change first, because minorities cannot be expected to dissolve into the majority.
- Notes that the educated plainsmen are as 'westernized' as the hillmen, and rejects blaming the British or foreign missionaries eighteen years after Independence.
- Proposes a non-political, non-religious service body on the model of the American Peace Corps.

### Anatomy of Developing Politics in Nigeria
*By Kejawa Assaf*

Assaf writes a post-mortem of Nigeria's first republic after the January 1966 army mutiny in which the Federal Prime Minister, the Premiers of the Northern and Western Regions and the Federal Finance Minister were killed. He treats the coup as the crude expression of grievances built up over years rather than as a rupture, and traces them to a constitutional settlement made in the 1950s by leaders who could not agree in London and so accepted three autonomous regions built on the Ibo, Hausa and Yoruba, which deprived the country of any national outlook.

He catalogues the resulting corruption: Coker's Commission finding the Western Region government to have misappropriated over fifty million pounds, the allegations against four federal ministers and against the Eastern Region's investment in Zik's Bank, and the North's missing financial records, with Wraith and Simpkins's Corruption in Developing Countries cited for more. The political narrative runs through the creation of the Midwest to split the Action Group's voting power, Awolowo's treasonable-felony trial (which he treats, following Alun Davies Q.C., as unsatisfactory from start to finish, with a convicted forger among the chief prosecution witnesses, no jury, and his British counsel excluded), the arrest of Wole Soyinka, the disputed 1964 census, and Sir Ahmed Bello's religious-fanatical politics and unauthorised promises abroad. His account of the old political class is contemptuous: semi-literate career politicians with no commitment to democracy who used tribalism to blindfold voters and expected to be treated as emperors. The prescription is a political institution built for turbulence, provincial devolution manned by a sound civil service, a free press, no tribe brought to the centre to dominate others, and a body of ombudsmen drawn from senior barristers of all regions.

- Reads the January 1966 mutiny as the expression of long-accumulated grievance rather than a sudden rupture.
- Blames the three-region constitution, produced by the deadlocked London conferences, for destroying any national outlook.
- Documents corruption through Coker's Commission, Zik's Bank and Wraith and Simpkins's Corruption in Developing Countries.
- Treats Awolowo's treason trial as procedurally indefensible and the 1964 census as inflated in the North's favour.
- Prescribes provincial devolution, a strong civil service, a free press and a body of ombudsmen drawn from all regions.

### Matthew Arnold and Keats
*By Vinod Sena*

Sena replies to Devindra Kohli's article on the Odes in Quest No. 48, which had been provoked by Matthew Arnold's judgement that for the faculty of moral interpretation 'Keats was not ripe'. Sena's first point is that Kohli picked the wrong opponent: Arnold's essay works hard in the opposite direction, arguing that a merely sensuous man cannot be a very great poet, that Keats had 'flint and iron in him', and that the yearning passion for the beautiful is an intellectual and spiritual passion connected with the ambition of the intellect. Sena accounts for Kohli's blind spot as the effect of an untempered Keatsolatory which will not brook qualified praise.

His substantive argument is developmental. Keats died at an age when Shakespeare was still in his first phase, and the Odes dramatise a critical point in his growth rather than its completion: the Psyche ode fixes on a love that 'had not bade adieu', the Nightingale and Grecian Urn odes try nature and art in turn as escapes from time and find both wanting, the 'Ode on Melancholy' (which he notes Kohli inexplicably ignores) accepts time's inevitability as the condition of intense beauty, and 'To Autumn' achieves the full acceptance that frees Keats to move past the naturalist plane, as the revised Hyperion begins to do. To transpose Leavis's 'tragic impersonality' from the second Hyperion back onto the Odes is to collapse the phases into each other and to have the sapling as the tree. Elegiac sadness and the pathos of mutability are not tragic vision; he invokes Yeats's own classification of Keats among 'the great lesser writers' and distinguishes the 'artifice of eternity' from the arrest of time on Keats's urn.

- Objects to transposing Leavis's term 'tragic impersonality' from the revised Hyperion back to the odes.
- Denies that the pathos of mutability by itself amounts to tragic vision, citing Yeats's classification of Keats among 'the great lesser writers'.

### Matthew Arnold and Keats
*By Devindra Kohli*

Kohli answers on two fronts. On Arnold, he concedes he knew the qualifying remark but argues it does not help, because Arnold generalises about the poetry from the life and letters rather than from the poems, as he did in dismissing Shelley as 'a beautiful and ineffectual angel'. Arnold's saying that the odes are both perfect and immature amounts to conceding sensuous density while holding the moral vision thin, which Kohli calls deadly unjust; and Arnold's excuse that in shorter things 'the matured power of moral interpretation... [is] not required' he rejects outright, since what matters in poetry is the cumulative quality of the experience communicated, not the size of the form.

On chronology he accuses Sena of dogmatic insistence on a rigid order among poems written within four or five months, pointing out that Keats had already finished the first 180 lines of The Fall of Hyperion before writing 'To Autumn', so that the neat progression to tragic impersonality will not hold; he prefers to see the odes as the culminating stage of a circular growth. He then builds the positive case for tragic intensity in the odes: the King Lear sonnet of January 1818 predicting the 'deep eternal theme', the fire in which Keats wishes to be consumed, the apprehension of early death, and a sustained parallel between the Nightingale ode and Hamlet's soliloquy, in which both contemplate a permanent 'shuffling off' and both are brought back to the realistic plane. He turns Sena's Yeats against him by asking in what sense Sailing to Byzantium and The Tower embody a Shakespearean vision of evil, and closes on Keats's negative capability and Yeats's 'A Drinking Song'.

### Sword and Shield
*By O. P. Bhagat*

A first-person story set in the town of Daryapur. The narrator, Prakash, befriends his neighbour Lala Sadhu Ram, a broker for the Pathans who bring camel-loads of chopped straw and firewood to the town: spare, robust, in his early sixties, dressed in the heavy salwar and seven yards of turban of an older generation, who walks miles daily, distrusts tea as a physician's brew, and tells his grandchildren stories at bedtime. Lala Sadhu Ram suggests the family buy a cow, and takes charge of the search.

The comedy of the story is the succession of disqualifications. One cow has no tuft to her tail and is therefore 'like a jar with the handle broken'; another is too old; a beautiful black animal is rejected because all four legs are white and so inauspicious, though a white patch on the head would have cancelled it; a farmer's cow five miles away is rejected for shaking her head, which proves her ill-natured and a danger to children. While Lala Sadhu Ram is out of town the narrator buys a cow himself, and the whole lane approves. On his return Lala Sadhu Ram looks at her horns, one gracefully curved and one straight and downward, and pronounces them the sword and the shield, meaning battle and daily quarrels in the house. The family, unwilling but unable to disobey him, sells her. The narrator is left feeling guilty at having sold an ill-omened animal to an ignorant man, until he meets the buyer again and finds him delighted: his mother-in-law, Shanti Devi, 'Goddess of Peace' but privately his Goddess of Strife, left for her son's town the day after the cow arrived, and he is celebrating her departure with a tumbler of the same creamy milk.

- A comedy of omens in which each candidate cow is disqualified by a bodily sign rather than by yield.
- Sets the narrator's affection for an older man against his inability to argue with the man's convictions.
- Turns on the horns read as sword and shield, the story's title, which condemns an otherwise ideal animal.
- Closes with an ironic reversal: the buyer's household becomes peaceful the day the 'unlucky' cow arrives.

### The Zamindar of Pallipuram
*By Sujatha Bala Subramanian*

From that humiliation the monologue slides between tenses into the Pallipuram of the past: Sankranti, when two or three hundred families filed into the courtyard newly clothed and fed at his expense, a manager whispering the accounts deferentially behind his chair; and the Green House, where the dancer Mallika became the centre of his version of paradise, and where a silver pan box with a woman's figure on the lid was given to him as a memento. The convention that a present to the mistress must be duplicated for the wife is noted without embarrassment, as is his wife Radha's calm knowledge of the whole arrangement. The dispossession arrives as a young collector stuttering 'Government-compensation-' with a piece of paper that the old man wants to throw in his face and instead folds and hands to his son, 'as if I have handed over my whole life into another's keeping'. The monologue closes with the arrival of Appu, a former cook who sold the gold thoda he was once given to start a hotel and has brought his old master a packet of jelabis; the zamindar, unable to let him leave empty-handed, gives away the silver pan box, the last thing he has.

### Towards a Theory of Social Change
*By P. C. Chatterjee*

Chatterjee reviews Daya Krishna's Considerations Towards a Theory of Social Change (Manaktala) as a philosopher's intervention in a field previously divided between sociologists describing particular societies and philosophical historians such as Toynbee and Spengler speculating. He reconstructs the argument carefully: that 'social' points both backwards to what an individual derives from his background and forwards to what his acts have consequences for, which if pressed reduces the individual to a cipher; that this consequence is unacceptable, since any theory denying the uniqueness of man must be false; that 'social' therefore reasonably means interpersonal; and that causal explanation, which explains narrower classes of events by wider ones, denies the 'specificity' Daya Krishna wants to preserve. In its place the author offers understanding in terms of value, treating society as more like a work of art than an object of nature, though he concedes the exaggeration, and distinguishes a realm of existence that prescribes the limits of freedom from a realm of value that is the realm of freedom.

The review then turns critical, and does so on the ground of the book's obscurity rather than its conclusions. Having promised a middle course between speculation and fact-gathering, Daya Krishna loses himself in abstraction and metaphor; the book 'abounds in felicitous generalities which have the appearance of profundity'. Freedom, the key concept, is never defined, and may mean no more than indetermination; the account of society is largely negative, saying what it is not; and the analogy with art objects looks artificial and, on the author's own premises, unnecessary, since if freedom means indeterminism he has already shown there is freedom in nature. Not disclosed anywhere in print: the author under review is one of the eight members of this issue's Editorial Board.

### India's Policy of Recognition of States
*By S. B. Mookherji*

Mookerji reviews K. P. Misra on India's policy of recognition of states and governments, opening with the history of the doctrine: Grotius and Pufendorf paid it little attention, but by the end of the nineteenth century Lorimer could call recognition 'the basis of International Law, with a fair measure of justification'. He rehearses the competing characterisations of recognition as a legal act (Quincy Wright, Ti-Chiang Chen), a political act (Philip Marshall Brown), or both (Kelsen), noting in passing that Soviet legal theory treats it as legal pure and simple.

The substance is India's record across China, Israel, North and South Vietnam, the German Democratic Republic, the Congo, the G.P.R.A., Syria and Mauritania, with the Algerian sequence given in detail down to the four-day interval between the end of French power on 2 July and Indian recognition on 4 July. The review's argument is that India's practice has been unusually consistent, which it attributes to eighteen years of single-party rule and to Nehru's supremacy as his own Foreign Minister for sixteen of them. It closes on the gap between recognition and diplomatic relations, noting that of eighty-one states recognised by 1961 India had no diplomatic relations with twenty-four, and finds India 'more honest and more idealistic than many nations', though the book's price is high.

### Secularism in Letter and Spirit
*By Punya Sloka Ray*

A letter answering a thesis attributed to Shri N. W. H. Pande, that Indian Muslims are a half-cut branch of the main Indian tree which must either fall off or reintegrate. Ray makes four numbered replies. First, the metaphor of a single tree is invalid: a viable society can be focussed on several partly separated, partly overlapping, partly competing and partly cooperating centres, as India is between legislature and executive, Union and States, English and Hindi, Right and Left, with no prospect of homogeneity. He argues that this polycentrism is hard for those used to monocentric societies to grasp, that 'Hindu' is itself not a simple concept (the monocentric Hinduism propagated by Dayanand and Vivekananda succeeded only partly, and pre-Muslim society was bicentric, with Brahmans competing with Kshatriyas), and that the Indian tradition is polycentrism, 'or as our President puts it, of unity in diversity'.

Second, responsibility goes with power, so most of the responsibility for Partition rests with the British, who had the most power; Muslim opinion was guilty only in proportion to its very little power, and his reading is that the Muslim League wanted Pakistan to regroup for a revival of the Mughal Empire, aiming not against the country's unity but against its secular and democratic constitution. Third, moral reciprocity is necessary: the Muslims who refused to go to Pakistan 'are with us now', and inward loyalty must be matched by inward consideration, which is why Gandhi supported the Khilafat movement. Fourth, parochial and even treasonable feelings in minorities are too common to excite anyone, and what matters is actions, not feelings; in a polycentric society where 'First Class is visibly vacant', the temptation is to explain one's own second-class position by an invisible, intangible 'them', a conjugation he says the Bengalis, the Mizos, the Marwaris of Calcutta and the fishermen of Midnapore all perform. He ends by observing that it is fortunate no single collection of fanatics can dominate the nation, and that this good fortune is precarious.
