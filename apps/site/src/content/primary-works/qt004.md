# QUEST

## Summary

QUEST Vol. I No. 4 (February-March 1956), edited by Nissim Ezekiel for the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom, opens with an unsigned editorial worrying at the line between legitimate 'agitation' and mob rule in the wake of the States Reorganisation Commission report and the Bengal-Bihar merger controversy, then runs six articles (on the prospects of Indian democracy against Communism, the ethical thinness of the Indian novel, the peculiar non-totalitarian shape of Japanese authoritarianism, a satirical dispatch from a Marathi literary conference at Pandharpur, a mocking account of the 1955 Indian Philosophical Congress, and Rene Tavernier's regular political-cultural letter from France), a short story translated from the Marathi, a theatre/dance/cinema section (Bharat Natyam and Kathak, the Kerala dance-form Ottam-Thullal, the 1955 Frankfurt Book Fair, and Satyajit Ray's Pather Panchali), ten book reviews, and three letters on Tamil Brahmin/non-Brahmin linguistics, Indian dress, and a Marie Seton film seminar. Contributors are a mix of journalists, academics and the magazine's own reviewers-in-house (several signing only with initials), and the issue's argumentative centre of gravity is anti-Communist, pro-democratic and cosmopolitan, consistent with its sponsorship by the Cold War-era Congress for Cultural Freedom network -- made explicit in an unlisted back-of-book note reporting the Indian Committee for Cultural Freedom's Third Annual General Meeting (with Nicolas Nabakov of the parent Congress for Cultural Freedom as guest of honour, and Minoo Masani, Philip Spratt and Jayaprakash Narayan among the newly elected Executive Committee). Beyond the printed contents page, the issue also carries two tipped-in photographic/art plates not listed anywhere in the TOC: portraits from a Marathi Literary Conference, and -- the kind of unlisted art portfolio this cataloguing pass was asked to hunt for -- two sculptures ('Finding of Moses' in teak, 'Torso' in pink Agra sandstone) by A. M. Davierwalla.

## Essays

### Prospects of Indian Democracy
*By SATINDRA SINGH*

Satindra Singh surveys whether India will 'go Communist' and concludes the pessimists overstate the danger. He runs through the standard alarmist case -- Communist control of a quarter of the world's territory, a unified command against sixty divided democracies, Comintern infiltration -- and rebuts it point by point: the 1952 general election gave the Communists and allies only 7.7% of votes and 246 of 580 contested seats against Congress's much larger tally; India's administration, army loyalty and communications remain sound; and the intelligentsia's flirtation with Communism is 'sentimental and opportunistic', not ideological conviction. He argues Marxist philosophy is fundamentally alien to Indian metaphysics (which treats the world as illusion/Maya and seeks detachment rather than the Marxist 'recognition of necessity'), and that authoritarian regimes in Russia and China have paid in millions of lives for gains no longer looking as impressive against Western living standards. The real danger, he concludes, is economic distress and unemployment, which the Five-Year Plan and continued US aid must address; India's task is to build 'bulwarks of resistance' on the strength of its own democratic and nationalist traditions rather than panic or complacency.

- Rebuts the pessimist case on Communist strength by citing 1952 election results: only 7.7% of votes and 246/580 seats against Congress's 2,228 State and 363 House of the People seats.
- Argues Marxist philosophy is incompatible with Indian metaphysics: Marxism holds consciousness determined by material conditions, whereas Indian thought treats the world as Maya (illusion) and seeks detachment, not 'recognition of necessity'.
- Cites Soviet mass terror (25-30 million dead 1926-1939) and post-Stalin liberalising concessions as evidence the Communist model is losing its shine even among fellow-travellers.
- Identifies the educated middle class's attraction to Communism as sentimental and opportunistic rather than ideological, rooted in revolt against 'encrusted values' of the past.
- Frames Communism as 'the twin brother of economic distress' and calls for rapid industrialisation, foreign aid, and vigilance rather than either panic or complacency.

### Dilemma of the Indian Novelist
*By HAMDI BEY*

Hamdi Bey argues the Indian novel remains a 'curious image of Indian life viewed through Western spectacles' because it borrowed a European art-form without confronting the underlying difference in ethics: the Indian novelist has never resolved whether his characters are free agents or merely instances of teleological, karma-bound archetypes, and so his characters do not grow, change or exercise real freedom of choice. He contrasts a supposed Indian ethic of collective/karmic destiny with a Western ethic in which immorality is 'an offence against the doer himself... against his own conscience', arguing this internalised conscience is what allows Western fiction to build character through genuine moral conflict. No post-war Indian novel, he says, has taken on the universal, civilizational-scale problems tackled by writers like Camus, Koestler, Vercors or C. P. Snow; Indian novelists instead attack minor social 'windmills' (child marriage, dowry) while leaving the deeper question of individual freedom unexamined, producing sincerity-starved fiction because, per Freud, genius and sincerity are close kin and Indian novelists have not lived out their own social protest.

- Argues the Indian novel is technically Western (borrowed 'art-form') but ethically confused because it has never assimilated a coherent, examined ethic of its own.
- Contrasts an Indian idea of character as 'god-given' (fixed virtues/vices per birth/karma) against a Western idea of character built through free, examined moral choice.
- Claims no Indian novel has addressed universal, civilization-scale problems the way Camus's The Plague, Koestler's The Age of Longing, or C. P. Snow's The New Men do.
- Criticises Indian novelists for attacking safe 'institutional boils' (child marriage, dowry, widow celibacy) while never questioning basic personal freedoms (whom to love, whether to have children).
- Invokes D. H. Lawrence and Freud to argue that sincerity in fiction requires the novelist to have lived out his own social protest, which Indian novelists have not done.

### Authoritarianism in Japan
*By TOMOO OTAKA*

Tomoo Otaka traces Japanese authoritarianism from the Tokugawa feudal hierarchy (1600-1868), through the Meiji Restoration's paradoxical combination of modernisation and Tenno-deification, to its collapse under the postwar Occupation and constitution. His central argument is that Japanese authoritarianism differed structurally from Italian Fascism or German Nazism: there was no Duce or Fuhrer with personal charismatic rule (the Tenno's authority was 'nominal', wielded by whoever occupied his name), no single fascist party, and no concentration-camp system, because the whole society was already 'all-embracing' under Tenno-worship and had no room for organised resistance in the first place. He explains Japan's rapid postwar acceptance of democratisation as resulting from two forces: a genuine liberal undercurrent since the Meiji era, and -- more paradoxically -- the very cultural habit of obedience to authority, now simply redirected toward the Occupation's orders. He closes by arguing that authoritarianism's real root is economic distress, and that only the cultivation of an empirical, fact-respecting cast of mind, plus economic stability, safeguards against its return -- explicitly warning that postwar left-wing radicalism among Japanese students mirrors the very charismatic-authority psychology (replacing the Tenno with Marx and Lenin as 'infallible' prophets) that produced prewar right-wing ultranationalism.

- Distinguishes Japanese authoritarianism from European Fascism/Nazism: no personal dictator (the Tenno's will was constructed by whoever held power in his name), no single ruling party, and no concentration-camp system.
- Attributes Meiji-era 'deification of the Tenno' to the practical need for recentralised authority after the Tokugawa feudal breakup, not to the Tenno system alone.
- Explains postwar democratisation's surprising success partly by a long liberal undercurrent since the Meiji era, and partly (paradoxically) by the Japanese habit of obedience simply redirecting toward new Occupation authority.
- Draws a direct parallel between prewar ultranationalist students who worshipped the Tenno and postwar radical Communist students who worship Marx and Lenin as equally uncriticised 'charismatic' authorities.
- Concludes authoritarianism is 'the twin brother of economic distress' and can only be durably prevented by empirical/factual habits of thought plus economic stability.

### Pilgrimage to Pandharpur
*By D. G. NADKARNI*

In a wry, personal-essay account, D. G. Nadkarni describes travelling to the Marathi Sahitya Sammelan (Literary Conference) at Pandharpur over New Year 1955-56, contrasting it unfavourably with the previous year's staid, over-politicised Delhi Sammelan. He is sardonic throughout about the conference's ceremonial excess -- a President's thirty-six-page inaugural address, a Samyukta Maharashtra resolution turned into a political rally by Congress politician N. V. Gadgil, and pilgrims of 'Parnassus' mixing with pilgrims of the god Vithoba. The essay's real interest is two symposia: one on new trends in the Marathi short story, chaired by the critic Gangadhar Gadgil (no relation to the politician), in which Nadkarni himself speaks and observes short-story writer Vyankatesh Madgulkar being praised without having to speak; and a 'strictly feminine' session on women writers chaired by novelist Malati Bedekar, which Nadkarni dismisses as sentimental and old-fashioned. He closes with vignettes of local organisers (Shri Dingre, D. M. Mirasdar) and an impromptu argument with a student defending his own short story against charges of obscenity.

- Describes the Pandharpur Marathi Sahitya Sammelan (Dec 1955-Jan 1956) as devotional pageantry mixed with literary politics, unfavourably compared to the 1954 Delhi Sammelan.
- Reports Shri N. V. Gadgil's overtly political speech in support of the Samyukta Maharashtra (united-Maharashtra) resolution, which passed unanimously.
- Covers a short-story symposium chaired by critic Gangadhar Gadgil, featuring Mukundrao Kirloskar's 'art for life's sake' speech and a largely silent Vyankatesh Madgulkar.
- Dismisses a parallel symposium on women writers, chaired by novelist Malati Bedekar, as sentimental and behind the times.
- Ends with the author defending a recent short story of his own, condemned by some as obscene, in an impromptu discussion with local students.

### Appearance and Reality
*By S. OOKERJI*

S. Ookerji gives a satirical account of the December 1955 Indian Philosophical Congress at Nagpur, mocking both the ceremonial pomposity of its inaugural addresses (a Governor's confused remarks on 'Natural Philosophy', the President's claim that only Indian wisdom truly looks inward) and the actual quality of its academic debate. His centrepiece is a symposium on 'The Nature of Error', where most of thirteen additional speakers -- including one of the four official symposiasts -- entirely missed the philosophical topic, instead offering lists of historical errors, spiritual homilies, or, in one case, 'a lecture on Death'; the chairman closed the session congratulating everyone on its peacefulness, missing that there had been 'no heat... because there was no contact between the speeches'. Ookerji uses this as evidence that Indian academic philosophy has become 'a menace and a mockery', producing muddle-headed reasoning dressed up as spiritual profundity, and driving talented students away from a discipline that rewards verbosity over rigour.

- Describes the December 1955 Indian Philosophical Congress at Nagpur as long on ceremony (Governor, Chief Minister, Vice-Chancellor on the dais) and short on rigorous argument.
- Singles out inaugural rhetoric claiming Indian philosophy alone 'turns man's eye inward' as prejudging the East-West philosophical debate before discussion even began.
- Recounts a symposium on 'The Nature of Error' in which most speakers, including a symposiast, misunderstood the topic entirely, some delivering irrelevant lectures or jokes instead.
- Notes the chairman praised the session's lack of 'heat', when in fact there had been no real engagement between speakers at all.
- Concludes that if the Congress represents Indian philosophy today, 'philosophy among us has become a menace and a mockery'.

### Letter from France
*By RENE TAVERNIER*

Rene Tavernier's regular column surveys French political and cultural life at the end of 1955: the Sultan's return to Morocco and the more intractable settler crisis in Algeria; the January 2, 1956 legislative elections fought among four blocs (Communists, Mendes-France's Republican Front, the governing centre of Edgar Faure and Antoine Pinay, and the new hard-right Poujadist movement); and a wave of company-union wage agreements modelled on the American Reuther/General Motors precedent. On culture, he reports the Goncourt Prize going to Roger Ikor's Mingled Waters over Louis Pauwels's The Monster Love, laments the commercialisation of literary prizes, praises Bernard Franck's The Comic Illusion as genuinely alive writing, notes the deaths of composers Guy Ropartz and Arthur Honegger, and singles out Rene Clair's film Army Manoeuvres as the season's one notable release.

- Covers the Sultan's return to Morocco and the harder Algerian settler crisis as the two live North African questions for France at end-1955.
- Analyses the January 2, 1956 French legislative elections as a four-way contest: Communists, Mendes-France's Republican Front, the Faure-Pinay centre, and the new Poujadist hard right.
- Reports new company/union wage agreements (echoing the US Reuther-General Motors model) at Renault and elsewhere as a hopeful social development.
- Reviews the year's literary prizes, criticising the Goncourt jury for favouring 'traditional and even outmoded forms of art' in Roger Ikor over Louis Pauwels.
- Notes the deaths of composers Guy Ropartz and Arthur Honegger and reviews Rene Clair's film Army Manoeuvres as the season's standout cinema.

### Bindo
*By ROOP KATHAK*

A short story, translated from the Marathi, narrating a first-person narrator's changing relationship with Bindo, a milkwoman married to Monsi, whom he first observes being tattooed unflinchingly in the bazaar at Lakheri. He initially hires her as his milk-seller out of curiosity/suspicion; when he hints crudely at wanting more than milk, Bindo calls his bluff by stripping off her choli and daring him to 'tell me frankly what you want', shaming him into honesty. Chastened, he agrees to a straightforward milk arrangement, and the story ends with Bindo becoming a regular, teasing presence who has made him 'forget all that had gone before'.

- Introduces Bindo through her unflinching endurance while being tattooed in the bazaar, established as a point of local gossip and admiration.
- The narrator, newly posted to Lakheri, hires Bindo as his milk-seller partly out of a prurient interest sparked by the bazaar scene.
- Bindo confronts his implied improper motive directly and physically, stripping off her choli and demanding he 'tell me frankly what you want'.
- The narrator is shamed into treating her plainly and fairly, closing the transactional/moral tension of the opening.
- The story ends on a note of ordinary, teasing familiarity, with Bindo becoming simply 'the milk-woman' rather than an object of scandal.

### Bharat Natyam and Kathak
*By KRISHAN SONDHI*

Krishan Sondhi reviews recent Bombay performances by Shanta Rao (Bharat Natyam) and Gopi Kishen (Kathak), using the pairing to draw out a broader contrast: Bharat Natyam is a 'masculine' dance form traditionally performed by women and rooted in Shiva-worship, exalting the dignity of woman even as it depicts male divine power, while Kathak is a 'feminine' dance traditionally performed by men, centred on Radha's emotional submission to Krishna, and historically driven off the stage for women by the added sensuality introduced during Mughal-era revival. He praises Shanta Rao's exuberant footwork and rhythm but criticises her weak 'abhinaya' (facial expression) and the fact that dance items go unexplained to lay audiences, and closes arguing the two forms are complementary and should learn from each other -- Bharat Natyam gaining expressive softness, Kathak gaining Bharat Natyam's poise and variety.

- Contrasts Bharat Natyam ('masculine' theme, performed by women, South Indian, Shiva-centred) with Kathak ('feminine' theme, performed by men, North Indian, Radha-Krishna-centred).
- Traces the difference to social history: South Indian matriarchal inheritance versus the loss of female performers in Kathak after Mughal-era 'sensuality' was added to the form.
- Praises Shanta Rao's vigour and rhythm but criticises her weak abhinaya (expressive facial performance) and the harshness of her accompanying singer.
- Criticises Bharat Natyam performances generally for using untranslated South Indian-dialect songs that lose North Indian audiences.
- Argues the two dance forms are complementary and should borrow from one another for a stronger, unified national dance tradition.

### Ottam-Thullal
*By UMA ANAND*

Uma Anand profiles Ottam-Thullal, a roughly two-century-old Kerala dance form created by the drummer-performer Nambiar (a single-generation art whose creative development ended with its creator's death), on the occasion of a rare Bombay performance by P. K. Poduwal during the Keraleeya Samaj's Cultural Festival. She argues Thullal never fully matured into a 'classic' art form like the older Kathakali, but that its core innovation -- performance in the spoken Malayalam vernacular, on immediately topical social and political subjects -- was genuinely valuable and has since been lost, occasionally abused instead for Communist propaganda. The bulk of the review is a sharp complaint about the festival's shoddy staging: a cramped, badly lit five-foot platform, drab curtains, singers in Western shirts and cufflinks bawling into microphones, and no minimal backstage comfort for performers -- evidence, she argues, that supposed 'cultural revival' events are often really tamashas staged to flatter political guests rather than to serve the art.

- Introduces Ottam-Thullal as a roughly 200-year-old Kerala dance form created single-handedly by the performer-drummer Nambiar, evolved from Chakkiar-Koothu and Kathakali mime.
- Argues Thullal's core value was performance in spoken Malayalam on immediately topical social/political satire -- a value largely lost since Nambiar's death.
- Notes Communists have 'occasionally employed' the form for political propaganda, which Anand calls an abuse rather than proper use of the art.
- Sharply criticises the staging of the Keraleeya Samaj's Cultural Festival: a cramped five-foot stage, drab curtains, amplified singers in Western dress, no backstage comfort for artists.
- Frames poor staging at 'cultural revival' events generally as tamasha -- performance used to court political guests rather than to honour the tradition.

### The Frankfurt Book Fair
*By SADANAND BHATKAL*

Sadanand Bhatkal, who organised India's first-ever stall at the 1955 Frankfurt Book Fair, reports on both the Fair's scale (685 stands, 1,149 publishers including 599 foreign, some 48,500 books exhibited, 50,000-60,000 visitors) and the practical lessons for Indian publishing. He credits West Germany's postwar book-trade recovery and Frankfurt's rise to replace Iron-Curtain Leipzig as Europe's book-fair centre, notes India exhibited for the first time alongside Japan, Russia, Yugoslavia, Poland and Czechoslovakia, and reports that German-standard production quality outstripped Indian books generally, though some Indian titles won real admiration. He closes with a concrete action plan: publicise available Indian titles abroad, build a comprehensive export catalogue, seek translation-rights deals, and set up a dedicated exporters' organisation, all as part of 'breaking through national frontiers' into the wider world book market.

- Reports Frankfurt 1955 hosted 685 stands and 1,149 publishers (599 foreign) exhibiting ~48,500 books, with 50,000-60,000 visitors over six days.
- Notes 1955 was the first year India, Japan, Russia, Yugoslavia, Poland and Czechoslovakia exhibited at the Fair.
- Credits West Germany's rapid postwar book-trade recovery and explains Frankfurt displaced Leipzig as the Fair's home after Leipzig went behind the Iron Curtain.
- Concedes German book production (jacket design, typography, binding) generally outclassed Indian books, though some Indian titles drew real praise.
- Recommends India build an export catalogue, pursue translation-rights deals, and establish a dedicated book-exporters' organisation.

### Pather Panchali
*By FOY NISSEN*

Foy Nissen reviews Satyajit Ray's Pather Panchali (from Bibhutibhushan Bandopadhyay's novel) as 'perhaps the first consistently cinematic treatment of Indian feature film', arguing it converts the sentimental tradition of Indian dramatic narrative into disciplined technical control rather than indulgence. He praises the film's visual intelligence -- its refusal of merely picturesque imagery in favour of images that carry 'the bitter significance of meaning' -- and its structure of expanding audio-visual motifs (a haunting musical score by Ravi Shankar, deliberate use of silence, the monsoon as recurring symbol) rather than plot alone. He summarises the plot (poverty, the children Durga and Apu, the grandmother's and then Durga's deaths, the father's search for work, the family's eventual departure from the village) and concludes Ray achieves 'a maturity far in advance of contemporary usage' in Indian cinema, remarkable for a director's first feature.

- Calls Pather Panchali 'perhaps the first consistently cinematic treatment of Indian feature film', crediting Ray with converting sentimental material into disciplined visual control.
- Highlights the film's use of physical/symbolic proximities (Durga-grandmother, Durga-Apu) to convey family relations without expository dialogue.
- Praises the score (music director Ravi Shankar) and the deliberate use of silence and recurring motifs like the monsoon.
- Summarises the plot: poverty, the grandmother's death, Durga's death, the father's search for work in town, and the family's eventual departure from the village.
- Judges the film's development is carried by 'expanding symbols rather than... the plot', marking a technical maturity ahead of contemporary Indian cinema.

### Asia and Western Dominance by K. M. Panikkar
*By N. J. N.*

N.J.N. reviews K. M. Panikkar's Asia and Western Dominance as a pioneering attempt to narrate the history of Asia-West relations (1498-1945) from an Asian rather than a Eurocentric vantage point, praising Panikkar's scholarship while pushing back on his central thesis that Western contact caused a 'qualitative break' with Asia's past. The reviewer argues Japan's Meiji Restoration was less a clean break than a restoration of an indigenous Shinto-based anti-Shogunate current that predated Perry's 1853 arrival, and that Asian traditional values (invoked via Okakura, Noguchi and Tagore) have persisted far more robustly than Panikkar's framework allows. The review also disputes Panikkar's claim that anti-colonial Asian leadership was uniformly 'Western-trained', noting Japan's modernisers were a clear counterexample, and closes suggesting Panikkar stops just short of endorsing an uninhibitedly modern, tradition-free vision of the new Asia.

- Credits Panikkar's Asia and Western Dominance as a pioneering Asian-perspective history of Asia-West relations from Vasco da Gama (1498) to 1945.
- Challenges Panikkar's thesis of a 'qualitative break' with the past, arguing Japan's Meiji Restoration continued a pre-existing indigenous Shinto/anti-Shogunate current rather than starting from a clean slate.
- Cites Okakura Kakuzo's 'cultural Asianism' and enduring 'basic concepts of patriotism, family loyalties and traditional values' as evidence Asian traditions persisted despite modernisation.
- Disputes Panikkar's claim that anti-colonial Asian leadership was generally Western-educated, citing Japan's modernising leaders as a clear counterexample.
- Suggests Panikkar implicitly favours an 'uninhibitedly modern' Asia stripped of past values, without stating this preference outright.

### The Future of English in India by A. R. Wadia
*By T. R. A.*

T.R.A. reviews A. R. Wadia's The Future of English in India unfavourably, agreeing with Wadia that the language problem is intrinsically simple but made 'needlessly difficult by pseudo-patriotism and parochialism', and that India's unity (paradoxically a British-era legacy later undermined by British-encouraged provincialism) must be preserved. The reviewer summarises Wadia's three-stage scheme (regional language at primary level, continued regional-plus-compulsory-English at secondary level, English displaced eventually by Hindi at university level) but calls it self-defeating -- 'a classic case of Jack of all languages but master of none' -- and accuses Wadia of undermining his own stated conclusion (that English must be displaced) through constant admiring asides about English's virtues, plus irrelevant digressions into legal-system description and cotton-mill statistics. The review closes that 'the cause for which Prof. Wadia writes is not much furthered by this book'.

- Agrees the Hindi-vs-English/regional-language problem is intrinsically simple but politically inflamed by 'pseudo-patriotism and parochialism'.
- Summarises Wadia's three-stage language scheme: regional language at primary level, regional-plus-compulsory-English at secondary level, eventual Hindi displacement of English at university level.
- Criticises the scheme as internally self-defeating, forcing students to learn three languages 'not one of them perfectly'.
- Accuses Wadia of undercutting his own argument for displacing English through constant admiring digressions on English's virtues.
- Faults the book for irrelevant tangents, e.g. a 'tedious account' of the English legal system's gown-and-bands and cotton-mill production statistics.

### To Whom She Will by R. Prawer Jhabvala
*By LAEEQ FUTEHALLY*

Laeeq Futehally reviews R. Prawer Jhabvala's novel To Whom She Will very favourably, arguing it stands apart from the wave of exoticised or dramatised accounts of Indian life by Indian women writers because it renders westernised middle-class Delhi Indians with plain, unvarnished, comic accuracy rather than colour or melodrama.
